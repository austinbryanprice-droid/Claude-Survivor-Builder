# Art direction

The owner wants the look to match the visual references as closely as possible: graphics, details, textures, aesthetics, fidelity and the HUD. This document says how, in words, because the reference images are third-party screenshots and stay out of this repository. From the look spike onward, our own in-engine screenshots become the references that pull requests compare against.

Two limits from the spec still apply (section 1): a stable 60 FPS at 1080p on mid-range laptops, read as laptops with a dedicated GPU (integrated-only graphics is not a target, see [decisions](decisions.md#hardware-target-2026-10-20)), and only CC0, generated or clearly licensed assets. The look is bought with budget. The Balanced preset is tuned to hold the frame rate; Ultra goes closest to the references.

## The world

- A lush golden-hour valley, bounded at about 192 by 192 blocks (spec section 12 asks for 8 to 12 chunks of view). Hills and forest close the horizon.
- A lake on one side. The crash site sits in the middle: wreckage, the Outpost Core, and a scorched ash patch around them with a faint amber-red mist, echoing the red mist pocket in reference 07.
- Day is a long golden afternoon. The sun stays between about 12 and 45 degrees, always warm, so shadows stay long and readable. Night is cool blue with cyan tech accents. Dusk and dawn blend over about 40 seconds.
- Terrain is static in version 1. Players place and break structure blocks, not ground.

## Light and atmosphere

1. **Sun.** One warm directional light with soft shadows from a shadow map that follows the player. The sun has a visible disc and glow.
2. **Fill.** A hemisphere light: sky color above, warm ground bounce below. Ambient occlusion is baked into vertex colors for terrain and structures, so no screen-space AO is needed on Balanced.
3. **Sky.** A gradient dome with procedural clouds that catch the sun on their undersides.
4. **Haze.** Height fog and distance haze tinted toward the sun, with colored mist pockets in hollows and over the crash site.
5. **Shafts, bloom and grade.** Half-resolution light shafts and bloom, then a final color grade with a light vignette. Strong grading (reference 08) is the cheapest way to look rich on modest hardware.

## Terrain, foliage and water

- **Terrain.** A smooth surface-nets mesh from the seeded density field, with collision from the same field, shaded with triplanar textures that blend by slope and height: grass on flats, rock on steep faces, dirt on worn paths, ash at the crash site. The look spike measured it against the smoothed-heightfield fallback and kept surface nets, because a heightfield smears rock faces (see `decisions.md`).
- **Foliage.** Instanced trees (pines and a broadleaf) with two levels of detail and distant impostors, grass cards and flowers that sway in the wind, and scattered boulders. Density scales with the preset.
- **Water.** One lake with a sky-colored fresnel, ripples and sun glitter. Ultra adds a half-resolution planar reflection for the still-water look of references 01 and 02.

## Structures and materials

Structures keep the spec's dark metal with amber and cyan accents, built with the construction language of references 04 and 05.

- **Geometry.** Thick pieces: walls read as deep, with inset panels and frame trim. Every face is inset and chamfered by 0.05 to 0.12 units, and faces are not merged, so seams stay visible. Phase 2c draws cubes with a 0.08 chamfer, a darker groove wherever two blocks meet and ambient occlusion in the corners. Slabs (half a cell high) and wedges (a 45 degree half-cube) give sloped roofs, eaves and strong silhouettes; since Phase 2e they take the same chamfer, groove and tiles as the cubes, and `?demo=hall` shows them as a gabled roof.
- **Tiers.** Scrap is patchwork salvaged plates with rust and rivets. Composite is layered grey-green panels with cyan edge strips. Alloy is brushed dark steel with amber inlays. Energy-Infused is dark with pulsing cyan veins. An upgrade must change the look, not only the stats: a block's material id picks its tile, so the Phase 5 upgrade changes one number and the block re-meshes.
- **Modules.** Turrets, generator, node, storage, terminal, bed and light are generated glTF models that feel engineered and modular. They glow cyan when powered and amber when active.
- **Materials.** Procedural, with detail bump from Balanced up. The tiles are baked on the GPU at load, four layouts for each tier, and each face of each block picks one by chance and turns it one of eight ways, so a wall never reads as one stamp repeated. Weathering, wear and exposed wiring come from the shared noise helpers, not per-piece textures, and no image file is involved.

## HUD

Reference images 04 and 07 are the model. The look spike built it (`src/ui/player-hud/`, with original icons) and the game page now shows it, fed by real numbers where the game has them (the compass follows the view, the Health and Energy bars are the player's own meters, the hotbar selection, the crosshair and the caption above the hotbar follow the player's building hands, and the day and night clock, the Core's gauge and the objective follow the run) and made-up ones where it does not yet (the hotbar's tools). The layout is drawn for 1280 by 720 and scales with the window. Type is a humanist serif system stack (Merriweather when installed, otherwise Georgia or the nearest serif).

- **Top left.** One torn, dark slate frame holds a wide health bar in three pale mint segments over a slim blue Energy bar that ends in a white cap. Under it, a column of round status icons, each with a short caption after a small triangle: green for a good one ("Charging", while the player stands in the Core's field) and amber for a bad one ("Low energy", "Out of energy"). The bars say how they stand without a number: Health empties from the right and turns red and breathes below a quarter; Energy turns amber below a quarter, where it starts to slow the player, and with none left its frame breathes. A wound flashes the Health bar and reddens the edges of the screen for a moment.
- **Top center.** A compass: a brush-stroke line about 320 px wide that shows 222 degrees, a small tick every 45 degrees, "N" inside a larger triangle with E, S and W as plain letters, and an orange diamond with an exclamation mark and a distance ("48M") for a waypoint.
- **Top right.** An objective banner: a slate bar that fades out to the left, a small letter-spaced place name over a bold objective line, and a mint diamond emblem straddling the end; under it, "Journal" with a white J key cap on a frosted pill.
- **Bottom center.** The hotbar: eight frosted slots with a numbered key cap at the top left, a status glyph at the top right, a green durability bar along the bottom, a stack count at the lower right, a cyan grid on blueprint items, and a white outline with a violet tint on the selected slot (a selected block keeps its cyan grid and gains a white ring and a soft glow). An "ALT" key cap sits to its left, and a "B Build" hint to its right that turns cyan while Build Mode is on. Slots 1 to 4 hold the four block materials as original icons, each a beveled cube in its tier's paint: scrap in rust with teal patches and rivets, composite in grey-green with cyan trim, alloy in steel with an amber hazard band, energy in graphite with cyan veins. Q turns the four into slabs and then wedges, drawn from the same paint, so the hotbar always shows the piece a click would place. A slot whose material has not been researched is dim and has a small padlock where its count would be ([Phase 5c](#phase-5c-research)).
- **Above the hotbar.** A caption: the name of the piece in hand in bold with "Cost 1 Scrap" under it in soft white, shown in Build Mode and for a moment after a new choice. When a click would do nothing, the second line turns coral red and says why ("Not enough Scrap", "Needs research · 12 of 20").
- **Center.** A small dot for a crosshair. Over a surface a block can go on it gets a thin cyan ring; in Build Mode a faint white ring is always there, and it turns red where a click would be refused.
- **Build Mode.** A translucent copy of the piece in hand, a block, a slab or a wedge, turned the way a click would place it, on the cell a click would fill (cyan when it works, red when it does not, with a bright rim and a slow breath) and a white outline, in the shape of the piece, on the block a click would break. The status panel at the bottom right lists the keys of the mode while it is on, Q for the kind of piece and R to turn it among them.
- **The day and night clock.** A small dark pill under the compass: the sun or the moon as a flat painted icon, the day or night with its number in letter-spaced capitals ("DAY 2", "NIGHT 2"), the time left until the light changes ("5:12"), and a thin bar along its bottom edge for how far through the day or night it is. It is amber by day and pale blue by night, and for the last 30 seconds before the light changes it breathes in its own colour. It is the spec's "subtle day/night or wave indicator", so it stays small. The waves join it when they exist.
- **The Outpost Core's gauge.** A second, slimmer pill under the clock: a small crystal icon, "CORE" in letter-spaced capitals, a thin bar and the points left. It is cyan while the Core is sound, amber below half, red below a quarter (and then it breathes) and flat grey at 0 once the Core has fallen; a hit flashes it. By night it is always up, even for a whole Core; by day it comes up only once the Core is hurt, so a morning with nothing wrong has nothing extra on the screen.
- **The research pill.** A third pill in the same column, under the Core's gauge and the respawn charges: a small atom, "RESEARCH" in letter-spaced capitals, a lavender bar towards the next row of the ladder and the points over it ("12/20", or the plain number at the top). It is up in Build Mode and for ten seconds after the points change, and it flashes when they rise ([Phase 5c](#phase-5c-research)).
- **Still to come.** The wave count by the clock, the held item's name for tools above the hotbar, a powered-area panel at the bottom right, and the inventory and research overlays (spec section 10) arrive with their phases and reuse the same frames and type.

The numbers live in `src/shared/quality.ts`. Balanced is the default and is tuned for 60 FPS at 1080p on a mid-range laptop; its governor lowers the internal resolution a step at a time when frames run long. The owner confirms it on real hardware with the benchmark switch (`?bench=1` on the game page). Presets change with the P key, the Graphics choice on the pause panel (both remembered in the browser) and a `?quality=` URL switch (for one visit); the full menu comes with Phase 6.

## Rules of thumb

- Match lighting and atmosphere first; they carry most of the reference look and cost the least.
- Pick the cheaper effect when two look alike, and measure before adding the next one.
- Never ship an asset that is not generated in code, CC0 or clearly licensed, and list every external asset in `CREDITS.md`.
- Do not commit reference images, screenshots of other games or anything traced from them.

### Frame budgets

Draw calls and triangles per frame at 1280 by 720, every pass counted (shadows, reflection, main view, post). The sandbox has no GPU, so these are the static budget; the milliseconds come from the benchmark below. The game page draws exactly these counts in the same views (checked after Phase 2b moved the valley into it, with `?shot=lake`, `valley` and `canopy`), so moving the valley into the game added no draw calls or triangles.

| View   | Low                 | Balanced             | Ultra                 |
| ------ | ------------------- | -------------------- | --------------------- |
| Lake   | 25 draws, 151k tris | 86 draws, 722k tris  | 91 draws, 1.52M tris  |
| Valley | 37 draws, 200k tris | 111 draws, 855k tris | 149 draws, 1.85M tris |
| Canopy | 35 draws, 218k tris | 103 draws, 892k tris | 131 draws, 1.91M tris |

Low draws at 0.75 scale. The terrain alone is 123k triangles per pass with surface nets. Ultra roughly doubles the triangle count because two shadow cascades redraw the scene.

Software rendering (SwiftShader, a 640 by 360 canvas) puts Balanced at about 12 times the cost of Low and Ultra at about 18 times. Only the ratios mean anything there, and a real GPU will not scale the same way, so they say which preset is heavier and nothing about frame rate.

Building the valley in headless Chromium takes about half a second: textures 20 to 30 ms, density field 3 to 6 ms, ground grid 150 to 380 ms, terrain mesh 150 to 180 ms (60 to 85 ms for the heightfield), scatter 60 to 80 ms.

### First real measurement

Austin ran the look page's `?bench=1` on 2026-10-20 (build 16034c2) on an RTX 4090 Laptop GPU (ANGLE, Direct3D 11) in Chrome 155 on Windows: a 2550 by 1273 canvas at device pixel ratio 1.25, a display of about 200 Hz, 30 seconds per preset with adaptive resolution off. Building the valley took 261 ms (field 2, ground grid 84, terrain mesh 70, scatter 29).

| Preset   | Internal size | Avg FPS | p95 ms | Slowest 1% FPS | CPU ms | GPU ms avg / p95 | Draws | Triangles |
| -------- | ------------- | ------: | -----: | -------------: | -----: | ---------------: | ----: | --------: |
| Low      | 1415 by 707   |   200.0 |    5.1 |            190 |    0.2 |        3.8 / 5.1 |    33 |      191k |
| Balanced | 2051 by 1024  |   200.0 |    5.1 |            187 |    0.3 |        1.7 / 4.0 |   102 |      816k |
| Ultra    | 2550 by 1273  |   200.0 |    5.1 |            191 |    0.4 |        2.9 / 4.3 |   131 |     1767k |

All three presets pass the game page's mark (at least 57 FPS on average, 95th percentile at most 18.5 ms) and no frame took longer than 33 ms. The internal sizes are the presets' pixel caps at work: 1.0 million pixels for Low, 2.1 million for Balanced and 3.7 million for Ultra, so Ultra drew the whole canvas.

**What this run shows.** It is the look spike's real-hardware result. On a machine this fast it confirms that nothing is broken: the preset switching, the benchmark route and the report work, and the draw and triangle counts match the static budget above. It does not show how much headroom there is. Every preset sat at the display's 200 FPS cap, so frame rate cannot tell them apart, and the GPU times are not monotonic across presets (Low 3.8 ms against Balanced 1.7 ms), which looks like power-state noise at the cap rather than real cost. Treat the GPU column as indicative only and do not scale it to slower hardware.

**What it does not show.** No mid-range dedicated GPU has been measured, so Balanced holding 60 FPS at 1080p on a mid-range laptop is still unproven. That is the target in SPEC sections 1 and 12, read as laptops with a dedicated GPU since [Austin's hardware decision](decisions.md#hardware-target-2026-10-20). To measure one, run the game page with `?bench=1` on it at 1080p, plugged in, with the tab in front (about 100 seconds), and check the report's graphics adapter line: on a laptop with two GPUs the browser has to run on the dedicated one (Windows Settings, System, Display, Graphics, High performance for the browser). See [the app README](../src/app/README.md#benchmark).

### Known gaps

- No mid-range dedicated GPU has been measured: the only real run so far was on a high-end laptop at its display's frame-rate cap.
- The crash site is an ash clearing with the Outpost Core in the middle of it (since Phase 4b, see [below](#phase-4b-the-outpost-core)); the wreck and the lanterns are still to come. Blocks are drawn since Phase 2c, and references 01, 04 and 05 are mostly about buildings (see [the blocks](#phase-2c-the-blocks)).
- Textures are detailed and smooth. References 01, 02, 03 and 08 use pixel cubes, and Austin chose smooth, detailed textures over them on 2026-10-20 (see [decisions](decisions.md#look-spike-2026-10-20)).
- Low has no shadows yet; blob shadows under trees are the planned stand-in.
- Balanced reflects the far shore faintly (0.3 scale); the near-mirror water of references 01 and 02 needs Ultra.
- The broadleaf canopies are brighter and simpler than the pines and oaks in reference 07. A second leaf texture and more colour variation are the next pass.
- Clouds are soft and procedural, not the sculpted volumes of references 01 and 03.
- Shaded grass at golden hour is dark, as it is in reference 01; reference 07's brighter mid-greens belong to a high-key daylight look that the morning and noon views already show.

### Frame budgets with blocks

Counted like the table above (every pass, 1280 by 720). The outpost is four chunks and 10 thousand triangles. A chunk that holds a block is one draw call in every pass that draws it, and the shadow pass counts, so Balanced pays twice for each.

| View                                     | Low                 | Balanced             | Ultra                 |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------- | -------------------- | --------------------- |
| Outpost (`demo=outpost`, `shot=outpost`) | 37 draws, 200k tris | 114 draws, 879k tris | 153 draws, 1.87M tris |

The other Balanced views: gate 108 draws and 768k triangles, closeup 118 and 813k, swatch 127 and 866k (nine chunks, four walls spread across the lawn). Beside the valley's own 111 draws and 855k triangles, a base this size costs a handful of draws and about 1 percent of the triangles. The sandbox has no GPU, so what a bigger base does to the frame rate is not measured yet. `/?bench=1&demo=outpost` flies the benchmark route with the outpost in view part of the way round; run it on a real machine once a heavier base exists (players can build one since Phase 2d).

The block tiles take about 43 MB of video memory at Balanced and Ultra (16 layers of 512 by 512, albedo and data, with mipmaps) and 11 MB on Low. They are baked the first time a block is meshed, so a world with no blocks pays nothing; under software rendering the bake takes about 9 seconds at Balanced and 3 at Low, a real GPU a few milliseconds.

Meshing a chunk on the sandbox CPU takes 0.3 to 1.3 ms for the demo's chunks and about 7 ms for a very dense one (a hollow room of 28 thousand triangles). The remesh queue spends 3 ms of a frame and always meshes at least one chunk, so one dense chunk can cost a frame; the mesher is pure and can move into a Worker if Phase 6 needs it.

### Known gaps in the blocks

- Slabs and wedges are drawn, can be walked on (a player steps up a slab or a ramp of wedges) and are placed by hand with Q and R. A slab's side shows a whole tile squeezed to half its height and a roof slope shows it stretched, The lighting pass looked at both in the Balanced shots and left them: they read as long panels on a roof and thin plates on a sill.
- One piece to a cell: a block put on a lower slab goes in the cell above, half a block over the slab, and two stacked slabs leave a gap a block cannot fill. A stair is a row of slabs one cell apart. The decisions page says why, and what would change it.
- The lighting pass against references 04 and 05 is done ([below](#lighting-pass-against-references-04-and-05)). A lit Scrap wall still reads orange at golden hour: its tiles are rust, and the low sun warms them.
- The tiles are small sci-fi panels. Reference 04's pieces are thicker and rougher (stone courses, timber); ours show the grid more. The lighting pass compared and left the tile art alone: how busy the tiles are is a decision about the art, and it is Austin's.
- A player standing in the grass sees blocks low in the frame through blades up to a meter tall (see the gate); that is the grass, not a gap in the blocks.

### Lighting pass against references 04 and 05

The references are single frames in their own light, so this is a check of range and not a match test. It showed that the brightness of a structure was already inside the references' range (a median of 0.14 to 0.34 against 0.16 to 0.45), so the pass does not brighten structures. Two things were outside it. Our shade was darker (the darkest 5 percent at 0.06 to 0.09 against 0.08 to 0.21) and violet, because the fill in shade is the golden-hour sky and it turned dark steel to plum, where the stone and timber of the references stay neutral. And Composite went yellow-olive in the low sun. Ours stays more saturated than the references' stone (0.40 to 0.52 against 0.20 to 0.40) on purpose: the amber marks, the cyan trim and the rust of Scrap are the spec's accents, and what the pass takes out is the tint the light adds, not the colour of the paint.

**What changed.** Three things in the structure shader and one in the mesher (the values and the reasons are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-2e-slabs-and-wedges)):

- The sky's fill on a structure is cooler and a little stronger, so shade is a blue-grey and not a violet.
- The low sun's orange is pulled part of the way toward grey on every surface (it already was on metal), so Composite stays grey-green.
- A polished surface shows more of the sky face on, so a steel roof reads as steel and not as a dark slab.
- Where a wall meets the ground the corner shading now knows the terrain: each vertex is darkened by how high it is above the ground under it, half as bright on the ground and easing to nothing at a meter, so a wall sits in the ground and not on a table.

**Cost.** Nothing is added to the scene and the geometry is the same, so the draw and triangle counts above stand. The shader has a handful more arithmetic operations per pixel. The contact shade is made when a chunk is meshed (the terrain never changes, so nothing is meshed again for it) and costs meshing time: a chunk of 29 thousand triangles (a hollow room) took 13.5 ms without it and 15.1 ms with it, and a checkerboard of 90 thousand triangles 46.7 and 50.2 ms, about 7 to 12 percent, in a Vitest run on the idle sandbox CPU (compare the pair: the 7 ms quoted above for a dense chunk was measured in the browser). The demo's chunks take 0.3 to 1.3 ms, so for them it is a tenth of a millisecond. The remesh queue still spends 3 ms of a frame and always meshes one chunk.

**Not checked.** Low and Ultra draw structures with the same shader and rendered the hall and the gate with no console error, but only Balanced's pictures were retaken. Nothing here has been seen on a real GPU: the light is judged on software-rendered screenshots, so the first look on Austin's machine is the one that counts.

## Phase 4b: the Outpost Core

The Core is the station's reactor, standing in the crater at the middle of the crash site, and the whole run is about it: the nights are about holding it, and the run ends when it falls. It is drawn to be found from across the valley and read at a glance, in the palette of the rest of the game: dark steel, amber trim and the cyan of the accents. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-4b-the-outpost-core-2026-10-21).

### The reactor

- **Shape.** A foundation the width of its 3 by 3 footprint, a body with three vents of light in each face under a line of amber trim, a deck, four pylons with amber bands and cyan caps, and a frame joining their tops. In the cage a six-sided cyan crystal turns and bobs over a plate of light, with a thin ring tilted about it. It is 4 blocks high, about two blocks above the rim of the crater, so it is the first thing in the clearing the eye finds. The foundation reaches a block into the ground, because the crater floor slopes under it.
- **Steel.** Dark blue-grey and fairly metallic, so the golden sun gives it warm edge light instead of turning it to clay (a first version in lighter steel did exactly that, a tan like wood) and the night makes it navy.
- **The crystal is the gauge.** Cyan while the Core is sound, amber once it is worn and red near the end, with the turns at about the marks of the gauge on the HUD (half and a quarter). The beat quickens as it weakens, it flickers below 40 percent, a hit flashes it white, and when the Core has fallen it sits dark, grey and tilted. The vents and the caps take the crystal's colour, so the whole plinth shows it from the other side of the clearing. The glow dims on the faces turned away, so it reads as cut facets.
- **Cost.** Six meshes: four merged by material (the steel, the lighter plates, the amber, the lights), the crystal and the ring. Counted like the tables above (Balanced, every pass, software GL), in view it adds 11 draws and about 24 thousand triangles: `shot=core` 105 draws and 747 thousand triangles against 94 and 724 thousand with the Core hidden, `shot=reactor` 106 and 752 thousand against 95 and 729 thousand. From `shot=lake`, where it is out of the main view, it still adds 5 draws and 11 thousand triangles, in the passes that are not the main view. Most of the triangles are the rounded corners of the small boxes (300 each); fewer segments on the thinnest trims would take about a third off if the frame ever needs it.

## Phase 4c: Health and Energy

## Phase 4d: the crawler

The first machine sets how the rest of them will look: low and dark against the lit ground, so that at a distance it is a silhouette with a few lights on it, and close up a small walker you can read at a glance. It is drawn from code like the Core (no assets, so `CREDITS.md` is unchanged) and in the Core's palette: dark steel, amber trim, and a hot light where the sensors are. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-4d-the-crawler-2026-10-23).

### The crawler

- **Shape.** A hull of dark blue-grey steel 0.9 blocks long with a plate and a thin amber spine on its back, the engine behind it, a head in front with a red strip for an eye and a pair of pale jaws, and six legs of bars with amber joints that spread to 1.7 blocks across. It stands 0.7 blocks high: below a player's waist, so a crowd of them reads as something on the ground, not as people.
- **Steel and lights.** The steel is the Core's steel, fairly metallic, so the low sun puts warm edges on it and the night makes it navy. The joints and the spine glow a little and breathe; the eye is the brightest point on the machine, so a crowd at night reads as a field of red points before it reads as shapes. A hit lights the whole machine white-hot for a moment, and the lights flicker as its health fails.
- **Movement.** The legs step in two tripods (front-left, middle-right and back-left, then the others). The stride follows how far the machine has walked and the swing follows its speed, so one that is slowing shuffles and one that has stopped stands. A bite is a lunge of the body with the jaws snapping, and a machine arrives by rising out of the ground over 0.6 seconds, so a spawn does not pop. Each differs in size by up to 5 percent from its id.
- **Cost.** One instanced mesh of 720 triangles a machine: one draw call in each of the three passes that draw it, as the heaps are counted in [Phase 5a](#phase-5a-salvage), however many there are. (This first said two, the main view and the sun's shadow map; Phase 5i1 counted three.) The field is not culled (one bounding sphere would have to cover the whole valley), so a full field of 100 costs the same wherever the camera looks: 72 thousand triangles in each pass, about 216 thousand on top of a Balanced frame of about 750 thousand (every pass counted, as in the tables above), more than a quarter at the worst. The vertex work is a handful of arithmetic operations a vertex. No real GPU has timed it yet.

## Phase 4e: the blaster's light

The pulse blaster is the first thing in the game that is drawn only as light: the bolt, the sparks where it lands and the burst of a machine that falls are added on top of the picture and glow, and nothing in the scene is lit by them. The palette is the game's own: a white-hot core with a breath of colour round it, the amber of the Core's trim and the crawler's joints where a machine is hit, and the shooter's seat colour wherever else a bolt ends, so that in a game of four every bolt says whose it is. Nothing here is an asset, so `CREDITS.md` is unchanged. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-4e-the-pulse-blaster-2026-10-23).

### The light

- **The bolt.** A streak that leaves the gun with its tail behind it: the head flies at 170 blocks a second and the tail trails 8 blocks, tapering to nothing, a white core in a halo of the shooter's colour. It is drawn the same few pixels wide wherever it is, with a core of three or four and a halo of about ten at 720p (wider with distance, so a far bolt still shows and a near one never fills the screen), and it fades in over its first four blocks, so that it does not begin as a smear in front of the player. A bolt shows for at least a tenth of a second, however near its mark. A shot at the longest range, 40 blocks, takes a quarter of a second to arrive, and what happens where it lands waits for it.
- **The muzzle.** A four-pointed flash for 0.08 seconds and four sparks, where the gun is held: 0.55 blocks ahead of the eyes, a quarter of a block to the right and a fifth below, which is the lower right of the view in first person and the avatar's right hand in third.
- **The strike.** Where a bolt ends: a flash that swells to a block and a quarter across in 0.14 seconds and ten sparks thrown back off the mark, which leap, fall and go out in about half a second. Amber on a machine, the shooter's colour on a block, the ground or the Core.
- **The kill.** When a machine falls, the burst: a hot core and a flash that swells to over three blocks across in 0.22 seconds, and thirty sparks flung every way at once, in a deep orange. It plays where the machine stood, and the machine is gone at that moment. Far flashes and sparks are drawn larger (up to two and a half times) so that they still read, and none is larger on the screen than a few dozen pixels, so one at the camera does not fill it.
- **Cost.** Two instanced meshes, one for the bolts and one for the sparks and flashes, so a firefight is two draw calls whatever it holds, added into the HDR target above the bloom's threshold. Nothing is lit by them and nothing casts a shadow. Everything an effect does while it plays (the streak flying, the flash swelling, a spark's flight and fall) is worked out in the vertex shader from the moment it was born, so the CPU writes a few numbers when something happens and nothing while it plays out. The buffers are rings of 64 bolts and 768 sparks, each one quad, so 832 quads (1,664 triangles) at the very most, and the meshes are in the scene from the start, drawing nothing, so the first shot of a session builds no programs.

### In the hands

- **Slot 5.** The blaster's icon, a pale pistol with a cyan-lit barrel, takes the fifth place of the hotbar on the plain dark slot the tools use (the blocks sit on the cyan blueprint grid), and is picked like any slot. Slots 6 to 8 stay the placeholders they were.
- **The sight.** A thin white ring with a dot at the middle and a tick at each quarter, so that it reads as a weapon and not as the build cursor, and the caption above the hotbar says "Pulse Blaster" and "Uses 0.6 Energy a shot" for a moment after the choice.

## Phase 4g: going down and coming back

Going down adds a pill under the Core's gauge and a screen, and both are made of parts the HUD already has (the gauge's pill, the status icons, the toast, the wound's red), so nothing here is new paint. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-4g-going-down-and-coming-back-2026-10-23).

### What the crew sees

## The intro film

A fresh run opens with a film of about 43 seconds: the station's reactor falls out of a dusk sky, burns a furrow through the valley and comes to rest in a crater; the Core wakes in the wreck, the machines of the wreck wake in the dark, and the sun comes up over the lake behind the title card. The last shot comes down to the shore where the player stands and becomes the player's own view. SPEC section 14 puts cutscenes out of scope, and this single film is the one exception, made at Austin's request; it is skippable at any moment. The choices, and the rules for when it plays, are in [decisions](decisions.md#the-intro-film-2026-10-27). Nothing in it is a model, a texture or a sound file: it is the game's own valley, sky and light seen by a camera on rails, with a wreck that is a lathe, fire made of instanced quads and machines that are the game's own crawlers.

### What the film shows

Six scenes, each opened by a cut that hides a change of place: a dip to black, or a white flash. The sky is the game's own day-night cycle run through in a few seconds, from the dusk of the day the reactor fell, through the night, to the morning the run opens on.

- **The fall (0 to 8 s).** The dusk sky, violet over a ridge of black pines with the last orange of the sun on the western hills. The camera stands at the edge of the clearing where the player will stand and looks up. A burning reactor comes in from far off as a comet with a curved tail, a white-hot head with four points of light and a long wake of fire and smoke, and the camera follows it down over the hills. The first caption fades in, "Station Vesper lost her reactor over an unnamed world."
- **The skid (8 to 14.5 s).** A white flash as it touches down at 8.0 s, and the cut is to a camera low beside the clearing's edge. The wreck, a drum nine meters long with its nose stove in and its back torn open, bursts out of the trees tumbling, drags its fire across the grass and ploughs the furrow the valley already carries into the crater. At 12.4 s it stops in a fountain of fire and sparks with a second, softer flash, and the hull opens on the Core.
- **The crater (14.5 to 22.5 s).** After a dip to black, the night. The camera, first above the grass of the rim with the Core a small white light on the horizon, drifts down toward the Core standing in the settling dust, embers lifting off the crater into the dark and the fires burning on among the wreckage. Twice the Core sends a ring of cyan light across the ground. "What the fall left standing, the night came to take."
- **The eyes (22.5 to 29.5 s).** Low on the crater floor, with the Core large at the left of the frame and the mountains beyond the fog. Five crawlers, the real ones, climb out of the ground at the edge of the dark one after another, each with a red glint at its eye and a little gold on its back, and start toward the Core as the camera drifts past it. "The wreck was not empty. Its machines woke in the dark."
- **The dawn (29.5 to 38 s).** The camera climbs out of the crater over the lamps and heaps of wreckage and swings out over the valley as the sun comes up across the lake. The title card arrives over the sunrise at 31.9 s: "Ashbound Outpost" in wide-set capitals and, under it in amber, "Scavenge by day. Hold the line by night."
- **The wake (38 to 43.5 s).** The picture is shut, and opens like an eye: two slow openings with a blink between them. The camera comes down out of the sky straight over the clearing to the height of the player's eyes at the spawn, looking across the lake at the sunrise, and the film ends there: the sky is exactly the run's opening sky, the camera blends into the player's own over the last 1.6 seconds, and the mouse, which was in hand from the click on Play, is the player's.

**Over the picture.** Letterbox bars to the cinema's shape (2.39 to 1) wherever the window is narrower than that, which slide in over the first 1.1 seconds and out over the last; the captions in the lower bar; the title card; the two white flashes and the dips to black at the cuts; the eyelids of the wake; and a prompt, "Any key to skip", that shows from 1.6 seconds in and dims after six more. The HUD is gone while the film runs. Any key or click ends it after the first 0.8 seconds with a dip to black and the run is there; Esc lets go of the mouse and leaves the pause card, whose "Watch the intro" button plays it again.

### How it is made

- **The wreck.** A reactor module 9.2 meters long and 2.5 across, turned on a lathe from a hand-written profile (a torn mouth at the back, a stove-in nose) with five ribs, swept fins, a few spars and a crumple from a cheap sine noise, so it has no model file. Its surface is one shader: steel plating in rings and panels with rivets and soot, seams that glow only where the hull is still hot, and the white-hot dome it flew in on, which the film lets die away once it has stopped. At the film's distances the ribs, the panels and the glowing seams read; the rivets and the soot are small. It tumbles in the air and the ground drags the spin to a stop.
- **Fire, smoke, sparks and embers.** Instanced quads whose whole life is a closed form of their age, written once when the film opens and moved by the GPU from the film's clock, so the CPU writes nothing while it plays and any moment can be drawn without having played the ones before. A ground fire is a flame, a tongue that narrows toward its tip and sways, not a round puff. The glows (the fireball's head, the flashes, the Core's breath and the machines' eyes) are squares that face the camera, bright in the middle with a halo and four points of light, added to the picture.
- **The camera.** Each scene is a track of keys in the crash site's own terms: so many meters forward along the wreck's last run, to its right and above the ground there. The same keys fit whatever the valley is, because the crater is the origin and the furrow the terrain already carries is the axis. The camera never goes nearer the ground than 45 centimeters, shakes while the wreck skids, and blends into the player's view at the end.
- **The sky.** The stage is told where the sun and the sky stand at each moment by the game's own day cycle (`Stage.setSkyOverride`), and gets its own back when the film stops.
- **The machines.** The game's own crawler renderer, at poses the film scripts: a held film settles them in a few steps. What the film shows is what the first night will bring.

### What the film costs

The film is ten fields of particles (7,180 instanced quads between them, one draw call each), ten glows, the wreck's hull and, from the eyes scene, the game's five crawlers. Counted like the tables above (Balanced, every pass, software GL) with the film's layers shown and then hidden at the same second, it adds 26 draws and 36.1 thousand triangles to a frame at 5.5 and at 9.8 seconds, when the hull is on the screen, 24 and 21.5 thousand at the stop, 22 and 21.5 thousand in the crater, 35 and 32.3 thousand in the eyes and the dawn, and 33 and 32.3 thousand in the wake. Underneath it is the valley as the camera sees it, so the film's frames run from 109 to 144 draws and 0.74 to 0.97 million triangles in all (the dawn, looking out over the whole valley, has the most triangles). Nothing is built or paid for while it plays. Its code is a chunk of its own, loaded when a page opens plain and only then (or when the pause card's "Watch the intro" is pressed); its particles are written once; and its shaders are compiled as it opens out of black. When it ends every buffer and material of the film is freed and the stage gets its own sky back.

### Known gaps in the film

- **The words are placeholders.** The spec gives no story, so the station's name ("Vesper"), the three captions and the title card's line are mine: `STATION_NAME`, `CAPTIONS` and `INTRO_TITLE` in `src/shared/intro.ts`, one constant and two tables to change.

- **The fall begins in the sky.** There is no station: no hull above breaking up, no space. An opening in orbit needs a model of the station and is left for a later pull request if it is wanted.
- **The wreck is a drum.** Its shape and plating are a profile and a shader, and it is seen at its closest, from about ten meters, at the skid. It would not bear a close look, and the film never gives it one.
- **The camera is on rails.** Every scene is one track, so nothing is aimed at by what is on the screen: if a valley's trees or rocks stand in a shot (the clearing's trees at the touchdown, the pines in the first scene) they are simply there. ## The title screen

It slows to a stop at each end (a raised cosine), so it reads as a drift and never a swing.

- **The camera.** A `Cinematic` (`engine/title-drift.ts`), the hook the film uses: the avatar is hidden, the mouse's look is ignored and the player's own view is still worked out underneath, which is what the glide flies to. - **The type.** The system's own sans-serif in bold capitals with wide tracking, as the film's title card has it, with a warm shadow so the name reads on bright sky and on dark forest alike.

## The film's sound

The intro film has music and sound effects, and the click on Play that starts it starts them. Austin asked for it on 2026-10-27 with the home page ("make sure the trailer has music and sound effects"). The soundtrack runs under the film's 43.5 seconds and rings on for 3.6 more into the first moments of play. Every sound in it is made in code with Web Audio (oscillators, filtered noise and a generated reverb), so there is no audio file to license or ship and `CREDITS.md` does not change. The choices, and the rules for when it starts, are in [decisions](decisions.md#the-films-sound-2026-10-27). **None of it has been heard**: the sandbox the game is built in has no sound device, so what follows is measured from the soundtrack drawn to samples by the page's own code, and how it sounds as music is for Austin's first listen.

### What the film sounds like

The music is in D: a low D minor while the night lasts and D major from the dawn. A sound that goes with the picture (the touchdown, the stop, each ring of the Core's light, each machine's rise, the title card, the eyelids) takes its time from the picture's script, so the two keep step when either is retimed.

- **The fall (0 to 8 s).** The wind comes up from nothing and under it a low D, felt more than heard, swells over the first four seconds. The hull groans three times, sinking in pitch, and sheds plates with high ticks and two clangs. A dark D chord swells from 3.2 s; from 3 s the air screams past in a band of noise that climbs from a rumble to a hiss; a thin A and then a B-flat float above. A low heartbeat starts at 5.8 s and quickens over five beats, and a riser sweeps up toward the touchdown. For the last fifth of a second the whole mix falls away by 26 decibels: a held breath.
- **The skid (8 to 14.5 s).** At 8.0 s the great hit: a sub-bass drop from 92 Hz to 33 that the ear feels, a thump, a crash of noise that darkens over three seconds and a crack, with three clangs of metal and a burst of crackle. The wreck grinds through the valley, metal dragged over rock fluttering fourteen times a second and slowing to three with it, with thuds and clangs and ticks scattered over the hull's tearing. At the stop (12.4 s) there is one last great clang, a thud, a groan sinking from 82 Hz to 54 and steam venting twice. The Core is uncovered at 12.75 s: its hum comes up, a D chord of pure tones whose third is the one that moves, and with each of its four rings of light a low swell and a glass ping.
- **The crater (14.5 to 22.5 s).** The quietest scene. A held chord of five low notes, seven bell notes (A, F, D, E, F, A, G) with a long room around them, breaths of steam, the tick of cooling metal now and then, the fires crackling and something stirring far off at 21 s. The drone and the hum are the only weight.
- **The eyes (22.5 to 29.5 s).** A B-flat chord, then G minor, and a pulse under them from 24.1 s that comes faster each time and turns from a heartbeat into drums. Each of the five machines has the ground give way under it, a plate scraping, a servo winding up and a blip as its eye lights, and then walks toward the Core with footfalls that get nearer and louder; the machines are panned apart. A riser climbs to the cut and, at the cut, the drone drops out.
- **The dawn (29.5 to 38 s).** G and then A as the title card comes, with drums, and at 31.9 s, as the card arrives, the key turns to D major on a boom, a drum and a bell flourish (D, F sharp, A, D), over D, A and B minor chords at 2.2 seconds each. The Core's hum glides its third from F to F sharp and goes out with the night. Bells in eighth notes begin and grow over a bar, and the fires thin out.
- **The wake (38 to 43.5 s).** The picture is shut and so is the sound: the whole mix is heard through a lowpass at 450 Hz, a wall, while the survivor's own heartbeat and two breaths come through it, and the wall opens with the eyelids, two slow openings with a blink between. G, then a D chord of nine notes (D, A, D, A, D, F sharp, A, D, E) that swells over the last bar as the bells thin out, and the wind comes up.
- **The tail (43.5 to 47.1 s).** The picture has handed over the run. The chord rings out into play with the wind still blowing, and the last 0.4 seconds are faded. A player who watches the film again while it rings (from the pause card, within a few seconds of the end) cuts it off over a quarter of a second, so that two scores never play at once.

### How it is made

- **A score as data.** `src/shared/intro-sound.ts` is a list of about four hundred **cues** (a sound, a second, a level, and a pitch, a length or a pan where the sound has them) and five **beds** (long sounds with their loudness and tone drawn as curves). Where the score scatters sounds (the fires, the thuds, the ticks) it draws them from a seeded generator, so every play is the same film. It touches nothing of the browser.
- **Twenty voices.** Each is a short recipe of oscillators, shaped noise and filters that is started at a moment and left to run out: `boom`, `thud`, `clang` (five partials that are not whole multiples, which is what makes metal sound like metal), `groan` (a saw sliding down through a narrow band with a wobble), `steam`, `tick`, `crackle`, `ping`, `whoom`, `servo`, `blip`, `rumble`, `step`, `pluck` (a bell: a sine, a sour high partner and an octave), `pad` (two detuned saws and a sine through a lowpass that opens as the note swells), `timp`, `heart`, `riser`, `breath`, and `duck`, which dips the whole mix. `src/engine/audio/voices.ts`.
- **Five beds.** The air `roar` of the fall, the `grind` of the skid, the `wind`, the `drone` and the Core's `hum`. A bed is built when it first falls due and steered along its curves in steps of 0.05 s (`engine/audio/beds.ts`, `INTRO_BEDS`). The hum follows the same `coreGlowAt` the picture's glow does.
- **One mixing board.** A fader in decibels for each kind of sound (`FADERS`) and one for each bed, so the numbers in the score are about the music and the picture, and the balance between the kinds is set in one place.
- **Noise and a room made, not loaded.** White, pink and brown noise come from a seeded generator (four seconds each, looped without a seam), and the reverb is a convolver whose impulse response is generated: 3.2 seconds of noise that falls 60 decibels and darkens as it ages, with its own noise in each ear, and a pre-delay of 18 ms so that a hit stays clear of its room.
- **The way out.** Everything goes into a bus, then the lowpass of the eyelids, then a limiter (a compressor set to catch only the loudest moments: threshold -14 dB, ratio 6, attack 3 ms) and the master volume the player set, then the speakers. The volume comes after the limiter, so turning it down never changes the mix.
- **Placed by the film's clock.** The director tells the sound where the film is every frame, and what starts in the next fifth of a second is handed to the audio thread for exactly its moment. A stalled picture holds the sound back with it.

- **Whole film.** The loudest sample is -1.7 dBFS and the true peak -1.7 dBTP, so the mix has about 2 dB of room at full volume; the loudest 50 milliseconds are at 8.01 s, the touchdown. Integrated loudness is -20.8 LUFS with a loudness range of 11.2 LU.

- **Both ends are silent.** The first quarter second is digital silence (-140 dB) and the last 0.05 s -88 dB, so the sound starts and stops without a click.
- **Stereo.** The two ears have a correlation of 0.62 (the machines are panned apart, the reverb has its own noise in each ear); every sample is a number, and the offset from zero is 0.00062.

### What the sound costs

On the page's first load the sound adds 2.5 kB of JavaScript (1.4 kB gzipped): the hub, the volume setting and the unlock. The synthesizer and the score come with the film's chunk, 21.4 kB (7.8 kB gzipped), and the renderer that draws them to a file (0.65 kB) loads only on a `?debug` page. No audio file is fetched, and no audio context is made until the click on Play.

Played in real time in the sandbox's headless Chromium (44.1 kHz, a four-core machine with no sound device, the page idle): setting the sound up, which makes the noise banks and the reverb's impulse response, takes 140 ms on the main thread, once, as the film begins; telling it where the film is, once a frame at 60 a second, takes 0.07 ms on average (0.7 ms at the 99th percentile and 3.3 ms at the worst of 2,653 calls, 0.2 seconds in all), which is where cues and beds are handed to the audio thread. The browser as a whole used 0.24 of a core while the film's sound played, against 0.02 with an empty audio context running, so the synthesis costs about a fifth of one core (the offline render that draws the soundtrack to samples takes 10.8 seconds for its 47.1, about the same). This browser does not report the audio thread's own load, so the figure is the whole browser's, with the page idle: the picture, which the film draws on top of that, is not in it.

The noise banks (three four-second buffers) and the reverb (3.2 seconds in each ear) are about 3.3 MB of samples, made when the sound is set up and freed with it; the audio context stays for the game's later sounds. A tab that is hidden puts it to sleep.

### Known gaps in the sound

- **It has not been heard.** The sandbox has no sound device. The mix is measured and the score is checked against the picture's script, but whether the music is good, the touchdown too loud or the crater too quiet is for Austin's first listen, which is why the slider is on the pause card and why a listening copy came with the pull request.
- **The music is mine.** The spec gives no music direction, so the key, the bell motif, the chords and the drums are my reading of the film. It is a second small score if it should change: `buildIntroCues` and `INTRO_BEDS`.
- **The title is silent.** A browser allows no sound before a click, so the screen the page opens on has none, and the sound begins with the click on Play and the fade to black.
- **Only the film, the valley's air and building have sound.** The valley's wind, the Core's hum, the low note of the night, a sparse music and the stinger before a wave came with Phase 6c1 ([the sound of the valley](#phase-6c1-the-sound-of-the-valley)), and the sounds of building with Phase 6c2a ([the sound of building](#phase-6c2a-the-sound-of-building)). Steps, shots, hits, the machines and the loss are Phase 6c2b, and can be built from the voices and beds written here.
- **One volume.** No separate music and effects, no mute key, and everything is heard in stereo from inside the head, not placed in the world.
- **The synthesis is judged only by its figures.** The voices are checked for what a browser's audio rejects (a stand-in context refuses a negative time, a ramp to zero and the like) and the soundtrack for its loudness, silence and shape, not for how the notes sit with one another.

## Phase 5a: salvage

Salvage puts one new thing in the valley, the heap, and three on the screen: a card under the crosshair, a line of toast and a number in the hotbar. The screen parts are made of the HUD's own pieces and the heap is painted in the Scrap blocks' palette, so nothing here is new paint. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5a-salvage-2026-10-24).

### What the crew sees

- **The heap.** Wreckage from the crash, built from boxes in code (no assets): about 2.9 blocks across and 1.7 tall at its usual size, and each heap is 0.85 to 1.25 times that. A buckled hull plate of patchwork Scrap with rust, teal paint and grey primer, an arc of the station's frame, a bent steel beam, and scorched rubble with a few embers still lit. One of three dressings sits on top, by the heap's number: hazard-striped crates with a cyan beacon, two barrels and a bent pipe, or a broken console whose cracked screen still flickers. The paint is the Scrap block's own, so a player who has stripped a heap knows what they were given.
- **The lamp.** A thin dark mast leans out of every heap to 4.4 blocks, with a short cross-arm and a small amber lamp that breathes. The valley's grass stands up to a block and a third and swallows a pile (the first model, a block tall, was lost in it), so the lamps are how a heap is found from across the valley.
- **A strip.** The heap flashes white-hot for about a third of a second and throws a spray of scrap sparks, and a layer of it comes away with each strip (on a heap of four: the dressing on top first, then the crates and rubble under it, then the beam, and the plate, the arc and the mast with the last, which shrinks them away as the heap sinks into the ground). The picture says how much is left, so a heap with two of its four strips taken is half of itself.
- **A dawn.** The heaps a new day uncovers come up out of the ground in about a second, the mast and the lamp first.
- **The card.** With the cursor on a heap in reach its edges glow warm amber, and a small dark card sits a little under the crosshair: a pale key cap "E", "Strip debris" in bold and "3 strips left" in soft type ("last strip" for the last one). ### Cost

One instanced mesh of 828 triangles a heap, drawn whole in every pass that draws the world, whatever is in view. Counted like the tables above (Balanced, every pass, software GL), from the far view: 22 heaps add 3 draw calls and 54.6 thousand triangles to a frame (97 draws and 781 thousand, against 94 and 726 thousand with the field hidden), about 7.5 percent. That is 2,484 triangles a heap, because three passes draw it. Each dawn adds four heaps, 10 thousand triangles; the 70 of the last dawn would add 174 thousand, about 24 percent, which is a number to watch rather than one to worry about on a dedicated GPU. Leaving out the heaps behind the camera or beyond the fog is the first thing to try if a real machine needs the triangles back. Nothing here is measured on a real GPU.

### Known gaps in salvage

- A block built in a heap's cell used to bury the heap, because nothing reserved the cell. That is closed in [5b](#phase-5b-costs): the ground round a heap with strips left refuses a block, and a dawn leaves a built-over heap down until the ground is free.
- Nothing was charged in 5a, so the pool only went up and the hotbar's counts had no use. That is closed in [5b](#phase-5b-costs) too: every block takes its price from the pool.
- A player walks through a heap, as through a machine. Collision for an odd shape is not worth it for a pile that is gone in a few strips.
- There is one look of the wreck in three dressings. A wider range is for later; heaps that give anything other than Scrap came with 5c ([Phase 5c](#phase-5c-research)).
- A strip has no sound, and neither has a lamp's hum; both are Phase 6 with the rest of the audio.
- The aim glow, the white-hot flash and the lamps' bloom are judged on software-rendered pictures. How they read on a real display is for the first look on a real GPU.

## Phase 5b: costs

Costs give the pool something to do: every block the crew places takes its price from it, and every block they break puts the price back. Nothing new is drawn. The ghost block, the line under the block's name and the hotbar's counts were all there already, and 5b gives them something to say, in the HUD's own colours. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5b-costs-2026-10-24).

### What the crew sees

- **A block the pool can pay for** looks as it always has: the ghost in cyan, the crosshair's ring cyan, and under the block's name the small line "Cost 1 Scrap".
- **A block it cannot pay for** is the ghost in the warm red of any refused placement, the ring red, and the line under the block's name turned coral and naming what is short: "Not enough Scrap", and "Not enough Advanced Alloy" for the dearer blocks, whose counts start at none (a count of none is dimmed; research opens them in 5c). Stripping the heap bare with E clears the ground, and the same cursor spot goes cyan again.
- **The count.** The first four hotbar slots (30 Scrap to begin with, shared by the crew) go down by one for each block placed and up by one for each block broken, whoever placed it.
- **A guest's screen** reads its copy of the pool, at most 200 milliseconds old, so its ghost goes red and clears as the crew's pool changes, with nothing for the guest to do.

## Phase 5c: research

Research puts three new things on the screen, a pill, a padlock and two lines of words, and repaints the one thing the valley has to show for it, the heap. Nothing is a new asset: the screen parts are the HUD's own pieces, and the heap's grades are colours in the same shader. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5c-research-2026-10-24).

### What the crew sees

- **The pill.** A third pill joins the column under the day and night clock, below the Core's gauge (at night) and the respawn charges: a small atom, "RESEARCH" in spaced capitals, a lavender bar and the points over the next row's, `12/20`. It is up while Build Mode is on, where the materials are chosen, and for ten seconds after the points change, so a player who does not build still sees what a dawn paid; it flashes lavender when the points rise. At the top of the ladder the bar is full and paler, and the words are the plain number, `100`.
- **A padlock.** A hotbar slot whose material has not been researched is dim and flat, with a small bronze padlock where its count would be (a count would only say none) and its block faint behind the lock, so a player can still tell the slots apart. Slots 2 to 4 start that way. The key still picks the slot: the ghost is the red of any refused placement and a right click sends nothing.
- **The words under the piece in hand.** Under the block's name, where the line says "Cost 1 Scrap" for a block the crew can pay for and "Not enough Scrap" for one it cannot, a block of a material not researched yet says "Needs research · 12 of 20": the points the crew has and the points that open it. The toast has a queue now (the Core falling and a wave coming use it too), so the second message waits for the first instead of replacing it; at most three wait.
- **The card under the crosshair.** A heap of anything but Scrap says what it gives: "Strip debris · Reinforced Composite · 2 strips left".
- **The tile.** The Outpost Overrun card has a sixth tile, "Research earned", between "Materials gathered" and "Respawns used". Six tiles lay out as two rows of three, not one row of six narrow ones.

Advanced Alloy is dark steel brushed in one direction, with an amber inlay running round each panel just inside its edge and a white lamp. The frame of a better heap takes some of its tint, and the low orange sun is taken less warm by a better heap, as the blocks of the same materials are, so that Composite does not go olive in it and the steel does not go to wood. The model is the same, so a better heap is the same size, has the same layers that come away with a strip and costs the same to draw; only the dress and the lamp differ. The lamp is the cue from across the valley: amber for Scrap, cyan for Composite, white for Alloy.

## Phase 5d1: the Research Terminal

The Research Terminal is the first functional module: a model drawn in code that stands in one cell, and the first thing the crew builds that is not a block. Nothing else on the screen is new. The hotbar slot, the line over it, the ghost and the outline are the ones building already draws; what is new is the thing they point at. ### The Terminal

- **Shape.** A field console on a steel pad, a little under a cell wide (0.8 of it) and about a cell tall with its masts. A foundation block and a rounded base carry a lighter cap and a cabinet at the back that stands to about 0.85 of a cell, with a lit screen on its front. A desk slopes away from the cabinet toward the player with a second screen laid in it and a row of five amber keys, and two thin masts stand on the cabinet's top, one with a steady amber cap and the other with a beacon that blinks. Each side of the cabinet has two amber bands and its back four upright cyan light bars, so the Terminal reads from every side: from the front it is a screen and keys, from behind four bars of light.
- **Steel and light.** The Core's palette: dark blue-grey, fairly metallic steel with a lighter trim, amber on what is worked (the keys, the bands, the beacon) and cyan on what is powered (the strip round the pad, the one along the desk's edge, the bars on the back and the lines of text). The golden sun gives the steel warm edges and the night makes it navy, as with the Core. The screens are dark teal glass with bright cyan lines on them: a first version with flat bright cyan screens read as blank panels, and the text is what makes them read as screens from across a clearing. There is no power in the game yet, so a Terminal is always on; the lights are the look the other modules take when they are.
- **It moves a little.** The screens shimmer, the strips and the readout breathe, and the beacon blinks every 2.2 seconds, all from the stage's animation clock, so a frozen picture shows the same Terminal at the same moment and a running page shows a console that is on.
- **It faces the player.** Placing it turns its front toward the player (screens and keys, so the first thing a player sees of their own Terminal is the front) and R turns it a quarter turn at a time, through four facings. The ghost is the model in the same turn, so what the player aims is what stands.
- **It stands in the ground.** The valley's ground is smooth and cuts a cell at any height, and a model on a cell's floor would hover over a slope or sink into a rise: the first picture of a Terminal in the cell the ground cut at 82 percent was a thin rod over the grass. The pad stands on a foundation block that reaches almost a cell below the floor, so where the Terminal's cell floats over the ground, the foundation is the plinth it stands on (up to three-quarters of a cell tall on a steep slope), and where the ground reaches a little into the cell, the foundation is buried and the pad sits on the grass. Where the ground would cover more than a quarter of the cell, the cursor puts the Terminal in the cell above. On a block, or on the upper half of a slab, the foundation is inside it and the pad sits on its top.

### Cost

A Terminal is six meshes (steel, trim, amber, cyan lights, screen glass and beacon), 1,544 triangles in all, built once and shared by every Terminal. Counted like the tables above (Balanced, every pass, software GL), from the crash clearing looking at open ground, a frame is 97 draws and 776.7 thousand triangles with nothing built. One Terminal in view takes it to 109 and 779.8 thousand, two to 121 and 782.9 thousand and three to 133 and 785.9 thousand: each adds 12 draws and 3,088 triangles, because it is drawn twice, once in the main view and once in the shadow pass. With the three behind the camera, the shadow pass still draws them: 18 more draws and 4.6 thousand triangles, 6 draws and 1,544 triangles each. The triangles are a small part of a frame (0.4 percent for each Terminal), but the draws are the number to watch: a dozen Terminals in view would add 144, more than the rest of that frame has. The lit material reads the model matrix, so a module cannot be instanced the way the heaps are. The first things to try if a real machine needs the draws back are letting the thin lit parts (the light strips, screens, keys and beacon) skip the shadow pass, which takes 4 of the 12 draws away, and instancing a module kind once the turret (5f) is built many times. Nothing here is measured on a real GPU.

### Known gaps in the Terminal

- **The plinth.** On a slope the foundation shows as a plinth under the pad, up to three-quarters of a cell tall where the ground cuts the cell's floor just under the cursor's threshold (a quarter of a cell: `MODULE_SINK`). A raised threshold (0.4 to 0.5) would put more Terminals in the ground and fewer on a tall plinth, and is a number to change if play prefers it. An upper slab carrying a Terminal shows a stub of foundation under the slab's half.
- **No power state.** The Terminal is always lit. A module that is off, or that is working, is for the generator and the node (5g), and amber for "working" is waiting for the turret.
- **Twelve draws a module in view.** Every Terminal is a group of six meshes sharing one model and one set of materials, drawn in the main view and in the shadow pass, and the lit material reads the model matrix, so it cannot be instanced. A base with a dozen of them adds 144 draws to a frame ([Cost](#cost-2)), which is the point where the turret (5f) will want a cheaper kind of its own.
- **No sound.** Placing it, the keys and the screens are silent. Sound is Phase 6.
- **One model, one level.** An upgraded Terminal (5e) is the same model until the upgrade's own look comes with it.
- Seen only in software-rendered pictures. The screens' glow and the cyan strips (which the bloom and the tone mapper change) are judged there; how they read on a real display is for the first look on a real GPU.

## Phase 5d2: the Terminal reads the points

Research now waits for a Terminal, and the Terminal gets a face. Three lines of the screen change their words (the pill, the line over the hotbar and the toast) and one panel is new, the ladder a Terminal opens. Nothing is a new asset: the panel is DOM drawn with the HUD's own parts (the torn dark frame, the lavender bar of the research pill, the block icons of the hotbar, the mint of the health bar), and the amber is the Terminal's own. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5d2-an-unlock-needs-a-terminal-2026-10-26).

### What the crew sees

- **Points that wait.** When the points the crew has earned reach a row of the ladder that no Terminal has read, the research pill turns amber and says "NEEDS TERMINAL" where it said "RESEARCH". The bar and the count are the same (`20/55`: the points over the next row's), only the label and the bar are amber, the amber of the Terminal's keys and beacon, so the pill reads as something waiting to be done. A crew with 12 points and no Terminal has nothing to be told and keeps the lavender pill.
- **The padlocks stay.** Slots 2 to 4 are as dim and flat as in 5c, with the same bronze padlock, and the ghost is the red of a refused placement. - **The toast.** At a dawn that pays, after "Research +12", a second bar says "Needs a Research Terminal" when the points reach a row no Terminal has read. When one does, the bar that follows is the 5c one, "Reinforced Composite unlocked", the padlock goes and the pill turns back to lavender.
- **The card under the crosshair.** With the cursor on a Terminal in reach, and no heap in front of it, the card says "E Research ladder" and, in the card's lighter type, the points the crew has ("20 points").
- **The ladder.** E opens a panel just left of the crosshair and as tall as its contents, so the Terminal stays in view beside it (the right of the screen is where Build Mode keeps its key list). It is the HUD's torn dark frame, 318 wide at 1280 by 720: the small spaced capitals "RESEARCH TERMINAL"; the points earned in large figures with "points earned" beside them; a lavender bar with the way to the next row ("35 to Advanced Alloy") and, once the ladder is climbed, "Ladder climbed"; a row for each material, with its block icon, its name, what opens it ("From the start", "20 points", "55 points", "100 points") and on the right where the crew stands; a line on how a night earns points ("Each night survived earns 10, and 2 more if the Core takes no damage"); and the key that shuts it. A row the crew has earned and no Terminal has read has an amber outline and says "Not read yet".

### Cost

Nothing is added to the draw, and the pill costs what it did. ### Known gaps in the ladder

- **Reading is automatic.** A standing Terminal reads the points; a player never uses it. The notes say points are "spent at Research Terminal", which would be a button and a choice, and there is nothing to choose yet ([decisions](decisions.md#phase-5d2-an-unlock-needs-a-terminal-2026-10-26)).
- **The amber "Not read yet" row cannot happen in play yet.** A standing Terminal reads in the same frame that carries the points, and the panel opens only at a standing Terminal. The state is drawn for a Terminal that needs power (5g) and for the debug page (`mp.setResearch(55, false)`).

A row is an icon, a name, a need, a state and a note, so a row for a module fits when it exists.
- **E does two things.** With a heap of debris in front of the cursor, E strips the heap, and a Terminal behind it cannot be opened until the heap is gone. The status list in Build Mode still says "E Strip debris" and does not mention the Terminal (the card under the crosshair and the pause screen do); from [5e](#phase-5e-upgrade-in-place) the lists say "Strip debris, upgrade", and E does a third thing.
- **No sound and no flash.** Opening the ladder and a Terminal reading its points are silent, and the Terminal's own screen does not change when it reads. Sound is Phase 6.
- Seen only in software-rendered pictures. The amber, the mint and the panel's frame over the bright sky are judged there; how they read on a real display is for the first look on a real GPU.

## Phase 5e: upgrade in place

A block the crew has built can now be made better where it stands. Nothing is a new asset: the card is the one a heap and a Terminal already use (a pale key cap, a bold line, a softer caption under the crosshair), a refusal is the warm red of a refused placement, and an upgrade is the better material's own tile on the same shape, drawn by the meshing every block already goes through. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5e-upgrade-in-place-2026-10-26).

### What the crew sees

- **The card.** With the cursor on a block the crew has built, in reach, and no heap of debris in front of it, the card under the crosshair says "E Upgrade to Reinforced Composite" in bold and, in its softer type, the price: "Cost 1 Reinforced Composite". The material is the one after the block's own (Scrap, Reinforced Composite, Advanced Alloy, Energy-Infused), and the shape is kept: a wedge offers a wedge.
- **When the answer is no.** In Build Mode the card also shows an upgrade the host would turn away, with its caption in the warm red of a refused placement: "Needs research · 0 of 20" (the points the crew has and how many open the material), "Needs a Research Terminal" (the points are earned and no standing Terminal has read them) or "Not enough Reinforced Composite" (the pool is short). Outside Build Mode the card is there only while the upgrade would go, so a crew with nothing to spend is not nagged by every wall it looks at; a press still says why it did nothing.

- **The press.** E swaps the block for the better one in the same cell, with the same shape, facing and half of the cell, and the pool pays the better material's price. The old block is used up and nothing is paid back for it. The card moves on to the next material up at once, so the next press goes one tier further if the crew has researched it and can pay.
- **A press that did nothing** says why on the line over the hotbar, in the words above, or "Out of reach", "Nothing to upgrade there", "Already fully upgraded".
- **The lists.** The Build Mode key list and the status list say "E Strip debris, upgrade", and the pause card says what E does: "Strip the heap of debris you are looking at, read the ladder of a Research Terminal, or upgrade a block to the next material and a turret to its next level" (the last words came with [5f2](#phase-5f2-turret-levels)).
- **The results card.** "Upgrades completed" follows "Blocks placed", and with the research tile there are seven tiles, laid out four above and three below.

### Cost

Nothing is added to the draw. An upgrade is a block changing state, so the chunk that holds it is meshed again as it is for any edit, and the better material is another tile on the same shape. On the screen the hands ask two small questions a frame while a block is aimed at (the better piece, a table lookup, and the reason it would be refused, which reads the economy's counts), and the card is rewritten only when its words change, as the heap's and the Terminal's are. On the host an upgrade is one edit and one payment, the same as a placement, and the wire carries a command of the size of a placement's and, in the results card's frame, one more number.

### Known gaps in upgrading

- **No flash and no sound.** The block changes tile and nothing else happens at the cell. A flash at the block is a small effect (the shot fx already draw sparks), but a screen only learns that a cell now holds the better piece, not what it held, so each screen would have to compare against its own world before it applies the event; it waits for the polish of Phase 6 with the sound.
- **The old block is not paid back,** and every material costs 1, so an upgrade costs one of the next material whatever the block was. The spec's "costs rise with tier" is as true as the table; the balance pass (5j) is where the numbers move. A refund of the old piece is the other fair rule and is one call on the host.
- **One press, one block, one material.** A press does not climb as far as the crew can afford, and a wall is raised a block at a time.
- **E does three things.** A heap in front of the cursor is stripped first, then a Terminal opens its ladder, and only then does the key upgrade, so a wall behind a heap cannot be upgraded until the heap is gone.
- **A module is not upgraded here.** E on a Terminal opens its ladder, and the levels of a module came with the first module that has more than one: [5f2](#phase-5f2-turret-levels) raises a turret with the same press.
- **A damaged block is not repaired,** because a block has no health to repair: a machine breaks it outright after its gnawing time, so an upgrade restarts the gnawing on a block that holds longer, which is all the repair there is.
- Seen only in software-rendered pictures. The card's warm red over the grass and the better materials' tiles in sunlight are judged there; how they read on a real display is for the first look on a real GPU.

## Phase 5f1: the Basic Turret

The Basic Turret is the first module that acts: a gun drawn in code that stands in one cell, turns its head to what it shoots and fires on its own. Little else on the screen is new. The hotbar slot, the line over it, the ghost and the outline are the ones the Terminal already uses, and the bolt is the blaster's streak drawn in amber; what is new is the thing they point at. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5f1-the-basic-turret-2026-10-26).

### The turret

- **Shape.** A gun on a steel pad, a little under a cell wide (0.72 of it) and about 0.9 of a cell tall. A foundation block and a flat pad with a raised corner at each corner carry a short column with a lighter cap, and on the column sits the head: a boxy housing with a round sleeve at its front and a thin barrel that runs out of the sleeve for 0.4 of a cell. The barrel ends in an amber ring, the housing has a small amber lamp on its top and a grey vent on each flank, and the whole head turns.
- **Steel and light.** The Terminal's palette: dark blue-grey steel with a lighter trim, cyan on what is powered (the strip round the pad, a plate on the front of the column, the visor across the front of the head and a panel on its back) and amber on what is worked (the ring on the barrel and the lamp). The golden sun gives the steel warm edges and the night makes it navy; at night what is seen of a turret is its cyan strip, its visor and the amber ring. There is no power in the game yet, so the lights are always on: they are the look the turret keeps when it has power (5g).
- **A head that turns.** The pad and the column stand still. The head turns about a pivot at 0.6 of the cell's height, first about the vertical and then a lift about its own side, so that a machine below the turret is shot from above and one up a wall from below. It snaps to the end of each shot and holds there until the next; it does not sweep. A turret that has not fired points the way it was placed.
- **It faces outward.** Placing a turret turns its barrel the way the player is looking, so that it points where they were aiming; a Terminal is turned the other way, so that its screens are what they see. R turns it a quarter turn at a time, through four facings, and the ghost is the model in the same turn, so what the player aims is what stands.
- **It stands in the ground.** The pad stands on a foundation block under it, with the Terminal's rule ([Phase 5d1](#phase-5d1-the-research-terminal)): where the cell floats over the ground the foundation is the plinth it stands on, where the ground reaches a little into the cell the foundation is buried, and where the ground would cover more than a quarter of the cell the cursor puts the turret in the cell above.
- **A turret that is aimed at** is outlined by its body box (0.72 by 0.78 by 0.72 of a cell), which is also what movement stands against.
- **The shot.** The blaster's streak (a bright head with a short trail) in amber, from the tip of the barrel to the machine, with a flash at the muzzle and sparks and a flash where it lands. It is drawn on every screen from the host's report, the host's own included, so a turret's bolt looks the same to the whole crew. Nobody fired it, so it has no hit marker; a kill bursts as a player's does.

A turret is seven meshes, three for the body that stands still (steel, trim and cyan lights) and four for the head that turns (steel, trim, amber and cyan lights), 1,460 triangles in all, built once and shared by every turret. Counted like the tables above (Balanced, every pass, software GL), from the crash clearing looking at open ground, a frame is 97 draws and 776.7 thousand triangles with nothing built. One turret in view takes it to 111 and 779.6 thousand, two to 125 and 782.5 thousand and three to 139 and 785.4 thousand: each adds 14 draws and 2,920 triangles, because it is drawn twice, once in the main view and once in the shadow pass. With the three behind the camera, the shadow pass still draws them: 21 more draws and 4.4 thousand triangles, 7 draws and 1,460 triangles each. A turret is two draws more than a Terminal. The triangles are a small part of a frame (0.4 percent for each turret), but the draws are the number to watch: a dozen turrets in view would add 168, more than the rest of that frame has. The lit material reads the model matrix, so a module cannot be instanced the way the heaps are. The first things to try if a real machine needs the draws back are the ones the Terminal's notes name: letting the thin lit parts (the strips, the visor, the ring and the lamp) skip the shadow pass, and instancing a kind of module now that there are two of them. The shot costs what a player's does, a streak, a flash and a few sparks for a moment from the effects' own pool, and a turret makes at most two a second. Nothing here is measured on a real GPU.

### Known gaps in the turret

- **Fourteen draws a turret in view.** A turret is seven meshes, three that stand still and four that turn, and each is drawn twice, in the main view and in the shadow pass, so each turret adds 14 draws ([Cost](#cost-5)). The lit material reads the model matrix, so a module cannot be instanced the way the heaps are, and a base with a dozen turrets adds 168 draws to a frame, more than the rest of one has. The two things to try first are the ones the Terminal's notes name: letting the thin lit parts skip the shadow pass, and drawing each kind of module as one instanced batch.
- **The head snaps.** It turns to the end of each shot in one frame and holds there. It does not sweep to a machine it has not fired at, so a turret that is about to fire is still pointing where it last fired. Following a target is a small change (the host's trace, the bolt and the model already share one line), but it needs a feel to be judged on a real display.
- **The turret itself does not react to its shot.** No recoil, no smoke and no glow at the ring: the flash at the muzzle and the bolt are the shot's own, drawn by the effects.
- **A blind spot at the foot of a tall pillar.** A turret on a pillar three blocks high cannot see a machine within about two and a half blocks of the pillar's foot, because the first block under its gun is the pillar. On a block or a two-high wall that ground is much smaller. It is the cost of a bolt that stops at the first block ([decisions](decisions.md#phase-5f1-the-basic-turret-2026-10-26)), and a second turret beside the first covers it.
- **The head dips into the column by up to 6 cm** at the steepest dip (70 degrees, measured in a unit test). It does not show from where a player stands.
- **One level, one model.** This section is the Basic Turret alone. The Auto Turret at Tier 2 and the Heavy Turret at Tier 3 have models and numbers of their own in [5f2](#phase-5f2-turret-levels); a turret's material byte is its level.
- **No power state and no sound.** The lights are always on until there is power (5g), and the shot, the turn of the head and the hit are silent until Phase 6.
- Seen only in software-rendered pictures. The cyan strips, the amber ring and lamp (which the bloom and the tone mapper change) and the bolt's brightness are judged there, a bolt photographed 1/30 of a second after it was fired; how they read on a real display is for the first look on a real GPU.

## Phase 5f2: turret levels

A turret the crew has built can now be made better where it stands. E on it raises the Basic Turret to the Auto Turret with Reinforced Composite and the Auto Turret to the Heavy Turret with Advanced Alloy, by the same press, the same card and the same command that upgrade a block ([Phase 5e](#phase-5e-upgrade-in-place)). What is new to look at is two more models, each in the paint of the material that pays for it, and a heavier bolt for the last. The choices and the numbers are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5f2-turret-levels-2026-10-26).

### The three turrets

- **One family.** All three stand on the pad and the short column of the Basic Turret ([Phase 5f1](#phase-5f1-the-basic-turret)), with the cyan strip down the front of the column that shows which way the turret faces, and carry a head that turns about the same pivot. A raised turret keeps its cell, its facing and the foundation under it, and is drawn as the better model from the next frame on; its head rests along its facing until it next fires, as a new turret's does. The body box that is outlined and walked against (0.72 by 0.78 by 0.72 of a cell) is the same at every level, so a raise changes nothing about where a player can stand or what the cursor catches.
- **Basic Turret** (Scrap). As in 5f1: blue-grey steel, one barrel with an amber ring at its tip, a cyan visor and strip.
- **Auto Turret** (Reinforced Composite). A rapid-fire gun. Two thin barrels lie side by side in an amber clamp at the front of a housing a little wider than the Basic Turret's, a drum of ammunition rides on one flank, and a crate of it stands behind the column with a small cyan light. It is painted as a block of Composite is: grey-green panels with pale trim, and its cyan lights glow harder than the Basic Turret's.
- **Heavy Turret** (Advanced Alloy). A cannon. A big housing with armoured cheeks carries one thick barrel in a recoil sleeve that ends in a muzzle brake glowing amber, and a block of sensors on top with a pale eye. The head is taller than the others and sits a little higher on its pivot, which keeps its back clear of the column when it lifts. A blast shield with an amber line stands along each side of the pad. It is painted as a block of Alloy is: brushed steel with amber inlay, so the strip round the pad is amber rather than cyan and the lamps are a pale warm white.
- **The paint** is the dress the heaps and blocks of the same material already wear ([Phase 5c](#phase-5c-research)), laid over the turret's looks: a better grade repaints the plates, the trim and the lights, and the looks it does not name (the amber of the Basic Turret's ring and lamp) stay as they are. A level is painted by the material that pays for it, so a level that came from another material would wear that one's dress.
- **At night** the steel is navy at every level and what is seen is the lights: the cyan strip and visor of the first two (the Auto Turret's a little brighter), and on the Heavy Turret the amber strip round the pad, the amber lines on its shields and the pale lamp.
- **The bolt.** The Auto Turret's bolt is the Basic Turret's, and it comes twice as often (every quarter of a second where the Basic Turret's comes every half). The Heavy Turret's is heavier: the table gives each level a `heft` (1, 1 and 2), a bolt is drawn that many times as wide, its colour is turned up by the square root of it (the additive light shows that as brighter) and so is the flash at its muzzle, and no heft is drawn past 2.5. Each screen reads the level of the turret that fired from its own copy of the world, so the host's report carries no new number for it.

### What the crew sees

- **The card.** With the cursor on a turret the crew has built, in reach, the card under the crosshair is the one a block gets ([Phase 5e](#phase-5e-upgrade-in-place)): "E Upgrade to Auto Turret" in bold and, in its softer type, the price, "Cost 10 Reinforced Composite". On an Auto Turret it says "Upgrade to Heavy Turret" and "Cost 10 Advanced Alloy".
- **When the answer is no.** In Build Mode the card also shows a raise the host would turn away, in the warm red of a refused placement: "Needs research · 0 of 20" (the points the crew has, over the points the material needs), "Needs a Research Terminal" or "Not enough Reinforced Composite". Outside Build Mode the card is there only while the raise would go. A turret has no research row of its own: the material it is paid in is the gate, so the Auto Turret opens with Reinforced Composite at 20 points and the Heavy Turret with Advanced Alloy at 55.
- **The press.** E raises the turret in place, in the same cell with the same facing, and the pool pays the price of the new level. What the turret cost before is used up: breaking a turret gives back the price of the level it stands at.
- **The top.** A Heavy Turret has nothing better, so there is no card, and E on it says "Already fully upgraded".
- **The lists.** The pause card says what E does ("… or upgrade a block to the next material and a turret to its next level"); the key lists in play and Build Mode ("E Strip debris, upgrade") are as they were. The hotbar still offers only the Basic Turret: a better turret is never placed, only raised.

## Phase 5g1: the Generator

The Generator is the first module that gives: a field reactor drawn in code that stands in one cell and gives Energy back to a player who stands near it, and a ring of light on the ground that shows how far its reach goes. The hotbar slot, the line over it, the ghost and the outline are the Terminal's and the turret's; what is new is the thing they point at, the padlock the module slot wears until the Power research is read, a row in the Terminal's ladder, and the ring. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5g1-the-generator-2026-10-27).

### The Generator

- **Shape.** A reactor on a steel pad, 0.76 of a cell wide and a little under a cell tall (0.97 to the tip of its beacon). A foundation block and a flat pad with a raised corner at each corner carry an upright drum, 0.47 of a cell across, that stands a little toward the back with a lighter cap and a mast on top. A control box (0.16 deep, 0.3 tall) sits on the pad's front with a lit gauge on its face, and three amber lamps under it, and a short pipe links it to the drum. A radiator hugs each flank: a backing plate on two brackets ribbed with five slats. The mast ends in an amber beacon that blinks, the Terminal's blink, and a stub with a collar sticks out of the drum's back.
- **Steel and light.** The Terminal's palette: dark blue-grey steel with lighter trim, cyan on what is running (the broad band round the drum, the strip round the pad and the gauge's bars and screen) and amber on what is worked (two thin rings above and below the band, the lamps and the beacon). The golden sun gives the steel warm edges and the night makes it navy; at night what is seen of a Generator is its cyan band and strip, its gauge and its amber lamps and rings. The band means it is running, which it always is: it burns nothing, and there is no off.
- **It faces the player.** Placing a Generator turns its control box toward the player, so the gauge and the lamps are what they see; R turns it a quarter turn at a time, through four facings, and the ghost is the model in the same turn. From behind it is the drum and its band, the radiators, the stub on the drum's back and the beacon, with nothing on the pad's back.
- **It stands in the ground.** The pad stands on a foundation block under it, with the Terminal's rule ([Phase 5d1](#phase-5d1-the-research-terminal)): where the cell floats over the ground the foundation is the plinth it stands on (a pale grey slab in the pictures where the ground falls away below the cell), where the ground reaches a little into the cell the foundation is buried, and where the ground would cover more than a quarter of the cell the cursor puts the Generator in the cell above.
- **A Generator that is aimed at** is outlined by its body box (0.76 by 0.85 by 0.76 of a cell), which is also what movement stands against.

### What the crew sees

### Cost

A Generator is six meshes (steel, trim, amber, cyan lights, screen glass and the beacon) and 1,704 triangles, built once and shared by every Generator. Counted like the tables above (Balanced, every pass, software GL), from the crash clearing looking at open ground, a frame is 97 draws and 776.7 thousand triangles with nothing built. One Generator in view takes it to 109 draws and 780.1 thousand triangles, two to 121 and 783.5 thousand and three to 133 and 786.9 thousand: each adds 12 draws and 3,408 triangles, because it is drawn twice, once in the main view and once in the shadow pass. With the three behind the camera the shadow pass still draws them, 18 more draws and 5.1 thousand triangles, 6 draws and 1,704 triangles each. A Generator is the Terminal's 12 draws and two fewer than a turret's 14. The ring is two meshes, the ribbon and the curtain, 384 triangles between them, and with it on screen a frame has 4 more draws and 768 more triangles across its passes; it is drawn in Build Mode only. The things to try first if a real machine needs the draws back are the ones the Terminal's notes name: letting the thin lit parts (the strips, the gauge and the beacon) skip the shadow pass, and drawing each kind of module as one instanced batch, which three kinds of module make more worth doing.

### Known gaps in the Generator

- **Twelve draws a Generator in view.** Six meshes, each drawn twice, as the Terminal's: a crew with a dozen Generators in view adds 144 draws to a frame that has 97 with nothing built ([Cost](#cost-7)). A Generator's field is six blocks, so a crew builds one or two beside a wall and not a field of them, which keeps this smaller than the turrets' ([5f1](#known-gaps-in-the-turret)).
- **The curtain is big near the edge.** From a player's eyes, standing inside the ring near its edge, the curtain is a translucent fence about as high as the player's chest running across the view (see the ghost and charging pictures). It is at most half opaque and fades out toward its top, but how strongly it reads, and whether the bloom on a real display makes it too bright, is a call for the first look on a real GPU. Its two numbers are the height `CURTAIN` and the opacity in the curtain's shader in `src/world/render/reach-ring.ts`.
- **The ring is Build Mode's.** It is drawn with the pointer taken and Build Mode on, round a Generator in hand or under the cursor. Nothing shows the reach in play, and the Core's own field has no ring: the "Charging" label is how a player in play learns they are in a field. A ring for the Core and one that comes on when a Generator is walked up to are both small changes.
- **The ring is drawn for a refused ghost too.** A crew short of Scrap or still behind the Power row, or aiming out of reach, sees the ring round a red ghost, so it can plan a spot it cannot build on yet.
- **No power state and no sound.** The band and the strip are always lit, and the Generator makes no sound and the ring none as it comes on: sound is Phase 6, and lights that dim without power are 5g2, which is the Node's work, since a Generator is the source and is never short of power.
- **A plinth where the ground falls away.** The foundation under the pad is a plain pale slab, as the Terminal's and the turret's are; where the ground falls away below the cell it shows as a grey block under the pad (the charging picture).
- Seen only in software-rendered pictures. The cyan band and strip, the amber rings and lamps (which the bloom and the tone mapper change) and the curtain's brightness are judged there; how they read on a real display is for the first look on a real GPU.

## Phase 5g2: the Power Node and power for turrets

The Power Node is the fourth module and the first one whose job is to pass something on: a relay pylon drawn in code that stands in one cell, takes the reach of a Generator (or of another Node) and carries it eight blocks farther. What is new to the eye is the pylon and one change to every machine that takes power: a turret or a Node with no power burns its lights low, so a base shows at a glance which of its machines are on, and a small tag under the crosshair says so in words. The hotbar slot, the line over it, the ghost, the outline and the ring are the Generator's ([Phase 5g1](#phase-5g1-the-generator)). The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5g2-the-power-node-and-power-for-turrets-2026-10-27).

### The Node

- **Shape.** A relay pylon on a small steel pad, 0.6 of a cell wide and 0.95 of a cell tall to the tip of its needle. A foundation block and a flat pad with a raised corner at each corner carry a collar and a slender column. Three disks that shrink toward the top (0.32, 0.26 and 0.2 of a cell across) sit on the column like the coils of a relay, with a band of amber insulator between each pair, a band of cyan over the top one and another over the collar. A bright cyan crystal, an octahedron stretched upward and turned to stand on a corner, rests in a small cradle on the top, and an amber needle above it blinks, the Terminal's blink. A console (0.14 deep, 0.18 tall) stands on the front of the pad with a lit screen and three amber lamps under it.
- **Steel and light.** The Terminal's and the Generator's palette: dark blue-grey steel with lighter trim, cyan on what carries power (the strip round the pad, the bands, the crystal and the screen) and amber on what is worked (the insulators, the lamps and the needle). ### What the crew sees

### Cost

A Bed is six meshes (steel, trim, amber, cyan lights, the status strip along its side and the beacon) and 900 triangles, built once and shared by every Bed. Counted like the tables above (Balanced, every pass, software GL), from the crash clearing looking at open ground, a frame is 97 draws and 776.7 thousand triangles with nothing built. One Bed in view takes it to 109 draws and 778.5 thousand triangles, two to 121 and 780.3 thousand and three to 133 and 782.1 thousand: each adds 12 draws and 1,800 triangles, because it is drawn twice, once in the main view and once in the shadow pass. With the three behind the camera the shadow pass still draws them, 18 more draws and 2.7 thousand triangles, 6 draws and 900 triangles each. A Bed is the Node's and the Storage's 12 draws with fewer triangles (900 against the Node's 1,304 and the Storage's 1,608), the lightest module to draw. It has no ring and takes no part in power, so nothing is swapped for it when the power changes. The rule that puts a player back at it is a walk of the module list when the Core brings someone back, once and on the host, so it adds nothing to a frame. The things to try first if a real machine needs the draws back are the ones the Terminal's notes name: letting the thin lit parts (the strips and the beacon) skip the shadow pass, and drawing each kind of module as one instanced batch, which six kinds of module make more worth doing.

### Known gaps in the Bed

- **Twelve draws a Bed in view.** Six meshes, each drawn twice, as the Node's and the Storage's: a crew with a dozen Beds in view adds 144 draws to a frame that has 97 with nothing built ([Cost](#cost-10)). A crew builds a few, one at the base and one where it fights, not a field of them.
- **Nothing marks where a player will come back.** A Bed looks the same whether it is the nearest to anyone or not: there is no marker on it, no line on the compass, and the message does not say where. A crew learns it by being felled once. A waypoint for the Bed the Core would choose is Phase 6 polish.
- **A player does not get up.** They stand, shaken, in front of the Bed the moment the Core brings them back, as they stood at the seat; there is no rising from the cot, no fade and no sound, which belong to Phase 6.

## Phase 5h3a: the Light

The Light is the seventh module and the third that takes power: a floodlight on a mast, drawn in code, that stands in one cell. What is new to the eye is the lamp itself, a seventh look for its lens, and that it is the one module whose light neither beats nor blinks. What a Light throws on the ground and on what stands round it is the next part ([5h3b](#phase-5h3b-the-light-a-light-throws)): here the lens glows and the world is as it was. The hotbar slot, the line over it, the ghost, the outline and the tag are the Terminal's, the Generator's, the Node's and the Storage's ([Phase 5d1](#phase-5d1-the-research-terminal), [Phase 5g1](#phase-5g1-the-generator), [Phase 5g2](#phase-5g2-the-power-node-and-power-for-turrets), [Phase 5h1](#phase-5h1-the-storage)). The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5h3a-the-light-2026-10-27).

### The Light

- **Shape.** A floodlight on a mast on a small steel pad, the slimmest of the modules: 0.5 of a cell across at the pad and 0.95 tall to the top of its head. A foundation block and a pad (0.5 across, 0.3 deep) carry a lower step, a plate of lighter steel and a collar (0.14 across) with a ring of amber round its top. A slender mast (0.056 across, 0.56 long) rises from the collar, with two small clamps on it and a cyan strip up its front. At the top, on a short bracket, the head leans about 14 degrees toward the ground in front of it: a shallow housing (0.34 by 0.34 and 0.15 deep, rounded) with a short visor over a lens in a frame. The lens, a pane 0.22 wide and 0.09 high on the front of the housing, is the lamp.

Without power it keeps its shape and its plates and burns low: the lamp is a dull amber pane (0.08 of its light, held still), the strip a dull teal and the ring a brown, as in a Node or a turret that has none.

## Phase 5h3b: the light a Light throws

A Light with power throws light. By night a warm cone falls from its lens on the ground, the blocks, the modules, the grass and trees, the machines and the fragments of a broken block in front of it, in the colour of the lens, so the glow on the mast and the pool on the ground are one light; by day it is a tenth of itself, a faint warmth. It is drawn by the shaders every lit thing already has, with no mesh, pass or draw call of its own, and it is a look and not a rule: nothing in the game reads it. The model, the slot, the ghost and the tag are 5h3a's ([Phase 5h3a](#phase-5h3a-the-light)). The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5h3b-the-light-a-light-throws-2026-10-27).

### The light

- **A cone from the lens.** The light leaves a few hundredths of a block in front of the middle of the lens and is aimed along its face, 14 degrees below level (the lean of the head), from about three quarters of a block above the cell's floor. Inside 43 degrees of that axis it is at full strength, and it fades over the next 20 degrees to a tenth of that, the spill, which is what lights the ground behind and beside a Light a little. On level ground the pool starts about half a block in front of the mast, is brightest two to four blocks out and has faded by eight to ten.
- **How bright.** The lamp's own colour, `#ffc778`, at a brightness of 7 at the lens, half of that 2.6 blocks out (the falloff is the plain inverse square with a soft core) and nothing past 15 blocks, ending smoothly from 8. The first guess, 9, burned the crates out (the brightness pictures below); 7 keeps the wall's tiles readable and still lights a pool that shows the cracks of the ground.
- **What a surface does with it.** Diffuse light with a half-wrap: a surface square to the lamp takes all of it, one at right angles a third and one facing more than 120 degrees away none, so a pool on flat ground reads as a pool and the back of a crate stays dark. It is the surface's own colour times the light, before the haze, so the lake's mist and the fog still lie over it, and in the terrain and the blocks the ambient occlusion that darkens a corner darkens the lamp's light in it by half as much.
- **By day.** The lamp's strength is a tenth of itself plus nine tenths of how dark the sky is (0 until the sun is within 2 degrees of the horizon, 1 once it is 14 below), so by day a Light is a faint warm patch on the ground in front of it and the sun is not fought.
- **Many Lights.** Pools add: three Lights in a row make one wash. The shaders take the nearest few lamps to the camera, 2 on Low, 6 on Balanced and 8 on Ultra, so the count in view is bounded however many a crew builds.

### What the crew sees

- **The Generator comes first.** A Light's pool comes up in the same frame as its lens when a Generator stands within reach, and goes out in the frame the Generator is broken, on every screen: the lens and the light are one thing, worked out from the same list of modules.
- **The Light does not light itself.** The mast, the pad and the housing are behind the lens and in the spill, so they stay dark navy and the lens reads as the source. The light falls on what is in front.
- **Everything lit takes it.** The ground, Scrap blocks and the other modules, the grass and the trees (a trunk goes the warm orange of its own bark, the tips of the grass a yellow-green), the machines and the fragments of a broken block (the same line is in their shader and draws without error, but the fragments are too small and quick to show in a still, so no picture of them is offered). The water, the distant hills and the intro film do not.
- **No shadows.** The light goes through what is in the way: the ground behind a wall of blocks is lit as though the wall were not there.

### Cost

The lamps add no mesh, pass or draw call. Counted as in the tables above (Balanced, every pass, software GL, at night, from the crash clearing looking at open ground), a frame with ten modules in view, eight of them Lights, all burning, is 184 draws and 798.0 thousand triangles, against 97 and 776.7 thousand with nothing built, and it is the same 184 and 798.0 thousand with the lamp limit at 0, 1, 2, 6 and 8: nothing is drawn for a lamp. What they add is in the shaders. Every lit fragment loops over the lamps in use (the loop ends at once when none burn, and a lamp past 15 blocks costs one distance test), so the work grows with the lamps in view and the pixels they reach. Software GL runs the shaders on the CPU and a frame takes seconds, so it can only say whether the work is large: alternating no lamps and all eight so that the machine's drift touched both alike, eight rounds of six frames at 640 by 360, a frame took 2.72 seconds with eight lamps against 2.63 with none (standard deviations 0.08 and 0.06), about 3 percent more, and at 1280 by 720 the frame times (4.7 to 5.3 seconds) did not order by the number of lamps at all. What a GPU feels is unmeasured: the owner's `?bench=1` on a real machine is the number to trust. If the lamps cost too much there, the knobs are the preset's lamp count (2, 6 and 8), the range (15 blocks), and skipping the loop for fragments farther than every lamp's range with one test against a sphere round the lamps.

### Known gaps in the lamp light

- **No shadows.** The light goes through blocks, walls and trees: the ground and what stands behind a wall of blocks takes it as though the wall were not there, and a Light behind a wall lights the room on the other side. A shadow per lamp would be a shadow map or a distance field each, which is the cost the lamps are made not to have; the sun's shadow is the world's only one.
- **Eight lamps at most, the nearest to the camera.** A base with a dozen Lights in view lights its nearest few (2, 6 or 8 by preset) and the rest are lens only until the camera comes nearer. Which lamps are nearest changes as the camera moves, so at the edge of the count a pool can switch on or off as the camera passes; at a few Lights it is never seen.
- **Diffuse only.** The lamp adds no highlight: the sun's shine on steel and gloss is unchanged, so the metal of a module takes the lamp as a flat warm tint.
- **Not everything takes it.** The lake, the backdrop of far hills and the intro film have shaders of their own and are not lit by lamps; a Light by the water does not light it.
- **No coming on.** The pool is on in the frame the power is, with no ramp or flicker and no sound, like the lens.
- **One colour and one size.** Every Light throws the same warm cone; nothing tints or widens it.
- **Nothing reads it.** The machines are neither drawn to a lit patch nor shy of it, and a player in the dark is not any worse off: it is what the base looks like, and the question of whether it should matter is for a later part.
- **A tenth by day.** The faint warmth at golden hour is deliberate (a floodlight does not fight the sun) and is one number, `day` in `LAMP_LIGHT`.
- **Brightness is a guess by eye.** 7 was chosen in software-rendered pictures, with a tone mapper and bloom that differ from a real display's, so its look on a real GPU and monitor is for the first run there to judge; it is one number in one table.
- Seen only in software-rendered pictures: the pictures are correct but there is no frame rate from them, and the cost of the lamp loop on a real GPU is unmeasured.

## Phase 5i1: the armored crawler

The first special machine is the crawler built heavier: a bigger hull under plates of rust-brown steel, with amber ribs across its back and hotter amber at the knees, so that in a wave at dusk it can be told from the pack by its shape before it is told by what it does. It is drawn from code like the crawler, in the crawler's palette made darker and warmer (no assets, so `CREDITS.md` is unchanged). Felled, it leaves Tier 4 cells in the pool, and the crew is told so in a message. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5i1-the-armored-crawler-and-the-cells-it-leaves-2026-10-27).

### The armored crawler

- **Shape.** The crawler's build bulked out. The hull is deeper and wider (0.78 blocks across and 0.38 deep, against 0.64 and 0.3) under two decks of plate with three amber ribs across the top, where the crawler has one plate and a thin spine. A plate runs down each flank over a skirt of steel, there is a plate over the engine behind and a brow plate over the head with a ram plate under it, the eye is a strip with a small lamp each side of it, the jaws are heavier, and the six legs are thicker, with amber knees. It is drawn 1.2 times its model, so it stands about a block high and spreads to 2.2 blocks across the legs, against the crawler's 0.7 and 1.7: the one machine in a wave that is the size of a player.
- **Steel and lights.** Its shell is a darker steel than the crawler's (`#1f252c` against `#262d35`) and its plate is a rust brown (`#5b4838`, where the crawler's is a blue-grey), so at golden hour the plates read orange-brown against the crawlers' grey and at night they go nearly black. The amber is brighter and glows more (0.9 against 0.7) and the eye is hotter (3.2 against 2.6), so what is left of it at night is the amber: three ribs across its back, the knees and the eye strip, larger and brighter than a crawler's.
- **Movement.** It walks 2 blocks a second against the crawler's 2.6, so the crawlers of its wave reach the base first and it arrives behind them. The legs swing in the same two tripods with a shorter stride for the ground covered (4 radians of gait to a block against 5) and full swing at a slower walk (1.3 blocks a second against 1.6), which makes a slow, heavy step. It climbs out of the ground in 0.6 seconds, lunges to bite and chews through walls as a crawler does, and a hit lights it white-hot as it does a crawler.
- **When it falls.** The same burst as any machine, then a message at the top of the screen, "A machine left 3 Energy-Infused in the pool", and the pool holds three more of Tier 4. While the 100-point row of the ladder is shut the hotbar's Tier 4 slot is padlocked and shows no count, so the message is the only sign of the cells until it opens.
- **How long it takes.** Thirteen blaster shots on the first night against a crawler's three, and fourteen against four on the fourth night, when the night's extra health is on it: 60 percent of every hit gets through, and nothing in the picture shows the rest being turned, so a crew learns the plating from how many shots it takes.
- **Cost.** A second instanced mesh, of 1,032 triangles a machine against the crawler's 720, drawn once in each of the three passes that draw it, as the heaps are in [Phase 5a](#phase-5a-salvage) (a module is drawn in two). A kind's mesh is drawn only while there is a machine of that kind, so a night of crawlers alone costs what it did and a night with both adds three draws. Counted like the tables above (Balanced, every pass, software GL), from beside the road looking out over the forest with the run held, a frame is 117 draws and 844.0 thousand triangles with no machine. One crawler takes it to 120 and 846.2 thousand, one armored crawler to 120 and 847.1 thousand and one of each to 123 and 849.3 thousand. The machines are not culled (one bounding sphere would have to cover the valley), so a field costs the same wherever the camera looks: 100 crawlers are 120 draws and 1,060.0 thousand triangles, 100 armored crawlers 120 and 1,153.6 thousand and 80 crawlers with 20 armored 123 and 1,078.7 thousand, which is 216, 310 and 235 thousand triangles more than the empty frame, or 26, 37 and 28 percent. The waves never send that many armored crawlers (the plan has one on night 4, two on night 7 and three on night 10), so what one costs in practice is 3,096 triangles, 0.4 percent of the frame. The crawler's own count in [Phase 4d](#phase-4d-the-crawler) took two passes for it; it is three, and that text is corrected. Nothing here is measured on a real GPU.

### Known gaps in the armored crawler

- **The plating shows nothing.** A hit that the plates turn lights the machine white-hot exactly as a crawler's does, with no spark, glint or sound to say that most of it was turned, so a crew learns the plating from how many shots it takes. A deflect spark is the first thing to add; the sound is Phase 6.
- **No health bar.** The only sign of how an armored crawler is doing is the flicker of its lights as its health fails. With thirteen shots to a kill that is a long time to go on without a sign, and a small bar over a hurt machine is the thing to try if play shows it matters.
- **The cells are behind a padlock.** While the 100-point row is shut the hotbar's Tier 4 slot shows a padlock and no count, so the message is the only sign of the cells and it is gone in a moment. Whether the slot should show what is in the pool while it is locked is for the first play to say.
- **The colour is judged in software.** The plates read orange-brown at golden hour (a rust was meant) and the amber knees read a pure yellow, louder than the crawler's. At night the plates go nearly black, so the form of the plating is lost and only the amber is seen. How the glow sits with the bloom of a real display is for the first look on a real GPU.
- **One message for each kill.** A night with several armored crawlers, felled quickly, may make this loud.
- **The body stays level on a slope**, as the crawler's does, so the feet of the larger machine can sink into a steep slope or hover over it by a few tenths of a block.
- **The breacher** has a body of its own since [Phase 5i3](#phase-5i3-the-breacher), as the flying skimmer has since [Phase 5i2](#phase-5i2-the-flying-skimmer).
- Seen only in software-rendered pictures, which have no frame rate: the cost of the second mesh on a real GPU is unmeasured, and a full field of armored crawlers is the number to watch.

## Phase 5i2: the flying skimmer

The second special machine is the first that does not walk: a drone of blue-grey steel that hangs 2.6 blocks over the ground on four spinning rotors, comes straight at the crew over water, walls and cliffs, and zaps from above. It is drawn from code like the others, in a cooler palette than the walkers' so that it reads against the sky as they read against the ground (no assets, so `CREDITS.md` is unchanged). Felled, it leaves Energy-Infused cells in the pool and the crew is told, as for the armored crawler. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5i2-the-flying-skimmer-2026-10-27).

### The skimmer

- **Shape.** A flat hull 0.64 blocks across and 0.86 long, with a deck of plate and an amber spine along the top, a nose with a red eye strip across its face, a fin behind and an amber strip down each flank. Four pods on short arms carry the rotors, each a pair of crossed blades that never stops (34 radians a second, about a tenth of a turn from one frame to the next at 60 a second, so they flicker rather than blur, and a still shows them wherever they were), the two on a diagonal turning the same way and the crosses set a little apart so that the four do not flash in step. Under the belly is the emitter: a lens in a bezel of plate. It is drawn 1.25 times its model, so it is 1.5 blocks long, 1.8 across the rotors and half a block tall, and it hangs 2.6 blocks over what is under it, which puts its belly just over the head of a player standing below it (1.8 blocks).
- **Steel and lights.** The shell is a cooler, bluer steel than the walkers' (`#2b3644`) with a lighter plate on its deck (`#53657a`), the amber is the crawler's yellowed a little further (`#b97b22`, glowing at 0.8 against the crawler's 0.7), the eye is the same red as theirs, and the blades are a pale steel (`#b9c3cf`). At night the hull is nearly black and what is left of the skimmer is its amber strips, the orange of the eye, the hubs of the rotors and the lens under the belly.
- **Movement.** It leans into a flight as far as its speed takes it (0.3 radians at full speed), bobs by a few hundredths of a block on a beat of two seconds and dips its nose by up to 0.35 radians to zap, all of it in the vertex shader from the machine's speed and phase, so a hundred of them are still one draw. The rotors turn at one rate however fast it flies, and a hover is not a stop: the height eases as it passes over the rises of the ground and over a wall of the crew's.
- **The zap.** In the 0.4 seconds before it, the lens glows nearly seven times brighter than it does at rest and a thin beam grows out of it toward the ground (2.4 blocks long at its fullest, so over level ground its tip stops a little short of the grass), to be gone again when the zap lands. The beam hangs straight down whatever the zap is aimed at: it is the sign that a skimmer has begun, not a line to the player.
- **When it falls.** The same burst as any machine, then a message at the top of the screen, "A machine left 2 Energy-Infused in the pool", and the pool holds two more of Tier 4. Two blaster shots on the first night against a crawler's three: it takes every point of a hit, and has twenty points to a crawler's thirty.
- **Cost.** A third instanced mesh, of 1,248 triangles a machine against the crawler's 720 and the armored crawler's 1,032, drawn once in each of the three passes that draw it (a module is drawn in two). A kind's mesh is drawn only while there is a machine of that kind, so a night without skimmers costs what it did, and a night with all three kinds adds nine draws. Counted like the tables above (Balanced, every pass, software GL), from beside the road looking out over the forest with the run held, a frame is 117 draws and 844.0 thousand triangles with no machine. One skimmer takes it to 120 and 847.7 thousand, and one machine of each kind to 126 and 853.0 thousand. The machines are not culled (one bounding sphere would have to cover the valley), so a field costs the same wherever the camera looks: 100 skimmers are 120 draws and 1,218.4 thousand triangles, which is 374 thousand more than the empty frame, or 44 percent, and 70 crawlers with 15 armored crawlers and 15 skimmers are 126 and 1,097.8 thousand, 254 thousand more, or 30 percent. The waves never send that many skimmers (the plan has one on night 5, two on night 9 and three on night 13), so what one costs in practice is 3,744 triangles, 0.4 percent of the frame. ## Phase 5i3: the breacher

The last special machine is the first whose job is the base and not the crew: a ram of dark iron under dull yellow plate, with a cell in a cage on its back, that walks the crawlers' way to the first wall of the crew's on its road (or to the Core), stands facing it, shudders and charges for 1.2 seconds, and goes off, once: a flash and a fan of fire, a hole of about three blocks wide and two high in the wall, a wound for whoever is standing near and a bite out of the Core. It is drawn from code like the others, in a palette of its own (no assets, so `CREDITS.md` is unchanged), and it is the machine whose picture is built up most by its charge. Felled before it goes off, it leaves Energy-Infused cells in the pool and the crew is told, as for the other two. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-5i3-the-breacher-2026-10-27).

### The breacher

- **Shape.** A broad, low hull of near-black iron 0.84 blocks across and 0.86 long, a deck of plate over it, plate down each flank with an amber strip of light, a lower engine behind it carrying two stacks capped with plate, and a blunt head with a brow over it. The head's face is a ram plate across its whole width, with wings of plate running back along its sides, a red eye strip across it, a grille of four amber bars below the strip and three pale teeth at its foot. On its back, over the deck, is the cell: a small orb in a cage of four posts on a base of plate with a frame over them. It stands on four stout legs, two rows of two, with a block of plate at each hip and each knee. It is drawn 1.3 times its model, so it is 1.3 blocks tall to the top of its cage, about 2.2 long, 1.1 across its hull and 1.7 across its feet, and its hitbox is 1.7 across and 1.3 tall.
- **Steel and lights.** The shell is a near-black iron (`#201e1c`) and the plate a dull demolition yellow (`#6e5b2e`), well off the crawler's yellow joints so that it reads as another trade; the grille and the flank strips are a dull orange (`#c4701a`) that glows at 0.45, the lowest of the machines, so that it has room to burn up; the eye is the red of the others and the teeth a pale steel (`#a9a398`). The cell is a dark ember (`#4a1a07`) glowing at 0.04 of its full rating at rest. At night the hull is nearly black and what is left of the breacher is the plate catching the sky down its flanks, the orange of its grille and the dull ember on its back.
- **Movement.** It walks slowly: 1.8 blocks a second against a crawler's 2.6, on legs that swing through a short stride (3.2 radians a block, at their fullest at 1.4 blocks a second), rocking a little as it goes. It has no lunge and no jaws.
- **The charge.** Over the 1.2 seconds the host calls it charging (the flag a bite has, so a guest builds it up on its own clock with nothing new on the wire), all of it shudders (by about 0.04 blocks, off the beat of its own phase so that a line of them does not shake in step) and sinks about 0.16 blocks on its legs, the orange of its grille and strips burns up to four times as bright, and the cell swells by 30 percent about its middle, until it strains its cage, burning from the dull ember to white (fifty times as bright as at rest). The charge is the only warning a crew has that a wall is about to go: there is no sound or message for it.
- **The blast.** It is an event with a flash and no words: a warm flash 3.4 blocks across that lasts 0.4 seconds, a white core in it 1.9 across for 0.2 seconds, and a fan of 64 sparks thrown every way, where the host says it went off (to a tenth of a block, 0.9 blocks ahead of the machine). Every screen draws it, in the same two meshes as the blaster's light, so it costs no draw call of its own.
- **When it falls.** Felled before it goes off, it is the same burst as any machine, then a message at the top of the screen, "A machine left 4 Energy-Infused in the pool", and the pool holds four more of Tier 4: the most of any machine, because it is the one a good crew should kill. Nine blaster shots on the first night against a crawler's three: it has ninety points and takes every point of a hit. Felled in the middle of its charge it bursts like any machine, with nothing blown away.
- **Cost.** A fourth instanced mesh, of 1,208 triangles a machine against the crawler's 720, the armored crawler's 1,032 and the skimmer's 1,248, drawn once in each of the three passes that draw it (a module is drawn in two). A kind's mesh is drawn only while there is a machine of that kind, so a night without breachers costs what it did, and a night with all four kinds adds twelve draws. Counted like the tables above (Balanced, every pass, software GL), from beside the road looking out over the forest with the run held, a frame is 117 draws and 844.0 thousand triangles with no machine. One breacher takes it to 120 and 847.6 thousand, and one machine of each kind to 129 and 856.6 thousand. The machines are not culled (one bounding sphere would have to cover the valley), so a field costs the same wherever the camera looks: 100 breachers are 120 draws and 1,206.4 thousand triangles, which is 362 thousand more than the empty frame, or 43 percent, and 55 crawlers with 15 armored crawlers, 15 skimmers and 15 breachers are 129 and 1,119.8 thousand, 276 thousand more, or 33 percent. The waves never send that many breachers (the plan has one on night 9, two on night 13 and three on night 17), so what one costs in practice is 3,624 triangles, 0.4 percent of the frame. Nothing here is measured on a real GPU.

### Known gaps in the breacher

- **The charge is the only warning.** A crew learns that a wall is about to go from the build-up on the machine (the shudder, the sinking, the amber burning up, the cell swelling and going white) and from nothing else: no sound, no message, no shake of the screen and no mark on the wall it is facing. Whether it should be named or sounded is for play to say, and the sound is Phase 6.
- **The blast is a flash and nothing else.** No scorch on the blocks left standing, no debris thrown from the broken ones (they go as chewed blocks do), no shake and no sound. The flash is drawn in the same two meshes as the blaster's light, so the size it can reach on the screen is capped as theirs is: a breacher going off beside the camera is no bigger on the screen than one a few blocks off.
- **It wounds through walls and breaks modules as freely as blocks.** The host checks the distance to the blast and not the line to it, so a player behind a wall is wounded, and a Turret, a Light or a Bed in the cells it clears goes with its block.
- **It goes off at the first wall its route meets.** It takes the crawlers' route, so a wall with a way round it is walked round, and where the way is closed it goes off at whatever wall it reaches first, not at the weakest.
- **No health bar**, as for the others. With nine shots to a kill it matters most here, and a small bar over a hurt machine is the thing to try if play shows it does.
- **The colour is judged in software.** The plate reads orange-brown at golden hour where a dull yellow was meant (as the armored crawler's rust does), the iron reads dark brown in the light, and at night the hull is nearly black. At full charge the cell is a flat white disc in a software frame, which the bloom of a real display may spread or tame.
- **The numbers are first guesses.** Ninety points, 1.8 blocks a second, a 1.2 second charge, a radius of 1.8 blocks, 35 off a player within 3 blocks, 150 off the Core within 2 and four cells dropped are all in one table, for the balance simulator (5j) to tune.
- Seen only in software-rendered pictures, which have no frame rate: the cost of the fourth mesh on a real GPU is unmeasured, and a full field of breachers is the number to watch.

## Phase 6c1: the sound of the valley

The valley has sound. The wind and the Core's hum run all day, the low note of the night comes up with the dark, a sparse music plays over them, a heartbeat and drums join it when the machines draw near, and ten seconds before each wave a stinger climbs toward it. SPEC section 11 asks for this ("soft industrial hum + wind/debris during day; lower, tenser drone at night", "a short rising stinger approximately 10 seconds before a wave", "sparse music that shifts from calmer (day) to more urgent (night)", from "free CC0 sources or simple synthesis"), and it is all synthesis, made of the film's own voices and beds, so there is still no audio file to license or ship and `CREDITS.md` does not change. The effects the spec lists next to it (place and break sounds, turret fire, enemy hits, power) are Phase 6c2, in two parts: building came first ([the sound of building](#phase-6c2a-the-sound-of-building)) and the fight is 6c2b. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-6c1-the-sound-of-the-valley-2026-10-27). **None of it has been heard**: the sandbox the game is built in has no sound device, so what follows is measured from a night drawn to samples by the page's own code, and how it sounds as music is for Austin's first listen.

### What the valley sounds like

The sound follows the run the way the sky does: once a frame it reads the clock, the nearest machine and the Core, and it listens for what the host announces (nightfall, a wave coming, the dawn), so a guest hears the same words the host's player hears, with the tension of their own corner of the valley. The music is in D, as the film's is: a D major pentatonic by day and the minor one by night.

- **By day.** The wind, a band of noise at 620 Hz that swells and sways, and the Core's hum, the film's chord of pure tones with its third at F sharp, which is in full within 10 blocks of the Core and falls to under a third of itself beyond 70. Every 7 to 15 seconds a bell note is struck (D4 to F sharp 5), now and then answered a step up or down, 3 dB softer, a breath later; every 55 to 85 seconds a D major chord swells over four seconds and fades over five. The first note comes six seconds into play and the first chord at twenty-four, so that the film's last chord has died.
- **Night falls.** At the host's word the hull groans (a saw sliding from 95 Hz down to 52 over four and a half seconds) and 0.6 seconds later a D minor chord swells. The sound follows the sky through its forty seconds of dusk: the wind drops to half its level and to 360 Hz, the third of the Core's chord turns from F sharp to F, and under everything comes the low note of the night (the film's D and A with two sawtooth D's through a lowpass), which is nothing by day and grows with the square of the dark. The bells move to the minor (D3 to F4), a little quieter and slower, every 10 to 20 seconds.
- **A wave, ten seconds out.** A rising noise starts 0.2 seconds after the host's word and climbs from 240 Hz to 3.6 kHz over 3.6 seconds, and a drum at 62 Hz is struck at the top of the climb. The wave comes out of the ground about six seconds after that. Each wave of a night hits a little harder than the one before, by up to 3 dB from the first to the last.
- **The machines close in.** With any machine alive a heartbeat starts under the night (once the sky is more dark than light, twenty seconds into the dusk, whatever came out of the ground before): two beats, the second 4 dB softer, a pair every 1.5 seconds at -18 dB with the machines far off and every 0.72 seconds at -9 dB at the walls. The low note brightens (its filter opens from 180 to 420 Hz) and swells by about a third. Within about 42 blocks drums join, one every 3.2 to 5.5 seconds, from -22 dB to -12. The tension follows a machine coming out of the ground over about 0.8 seconds and lets go over about 4 when the last is felled, as a pulse does.
- **The dawn.** The sky has been lightening, and the sound with it, for the forty seconds before; the host's word is a glass note (D5) and, 0.4 seconds later, a D major chord.
- **Held and over.** While the mouse is free and the run's clock waits (the pause card), and once the run is over, the music stops where it is: no new bell, chord, heartbeat or drum, while the wind, the hum and the low note go on.
- **With the film.** The film has its own score. While it plays the valley is silent; a film that begins over a sounding valley takes it away in 0.6 seconds, and the valley comes up under the film's last chord when it ends.

### How it is made

- **A plan with nothing of the browser in it.** `src/shared/game-sound.ts` holds the levels of the long sounds (`MIX`), the music (its notes, its pace and its levels: `MUSIC` and `Composer`), the stinger and the three words of the hours (`waveStinger`, `nightfallCues`, `dawnCues`, and `cuesForEvent`, which turns one of the host's events into the sound it makes) and the scene the sound follows each frame (`SceneTracker`: how far into the night the sky is, how tense it is, how near the Core). The music is a function of its seed and of the scenes it is shown, so a unit test listens to a whole night in a moment, and the words are seeded by the world, so every screen of a room plays the same groan and the same drums.
- **The film's voices, steered by the clock.** `src/engine/audio/live.ts` runs the film's wind, Core hum and drone beds all the while, each eased toward the level and tone the scene asks for (a level follows a change over 2.2 seconds and a tone over 4; the targets are rewritten ten times a second, and only once they have moved), and strikes the film's `pluck`, `pad`, `heart`, `timp`, `riser`, `groan` and `ping` voices as the music and the host's words call for them, a twentieth of a second ahead of the audio clock. It has its own noise and room, goes through the same output chain as the film (the limiter, then the master volume the player set) and comes up over three seconds when it starts.
- **Wired to the game.** `src/app/attach-sound.ts` loads on its own once the game is up, as a lazy chunk. A browser keeps a page silent until it has been clicked, so the first click or key press on the page makes the audio context, whatever it was for: the valley is silent on the title, and the wind comes up with the first click. A page told to be silent (`?sound=off`) or a volume at nothing makes no context; turn the slider up and click again, and it builds.
- **Behind the film.** The sound's room and noise take about a tenth of a second to make, so it is built as soon as there is an audio context, behind the film when there is one, and starts nothing until the film is over. Building it on the frame that hands the run over was a hitch of that size.
- **It never needs to work.** A browser that cannot make something the sound needs plays the game in silence and says so once in the console, and a recipe that fails is counted, told once and passed over.
- **Drawn as well as played.** `window.__ashbound.audio.renderValley({ night, seed, leadIn, seconds, ... })` on a `?debug` page (`src/app/render-valley-sound.ts`) draws a night to samples with no one listening: the same plan, voices and output chain on an offline context, with a script for the player and the crew. The player stands 8 blocks from the Core; the night's own waves (`planNight`) come out of the ring the game spawns them on, walk in at a crawler's pace to 13 blocks of the player and are fought there for 22 seconds; and the host's words fall due when the plan says. It is how the mix below was set and measured, and what the next sounds can be measured against.

### What the sound costs

The page's first load carries none of it: the first-load script is 4.8 kB smaller than main's (663.8 kB against 668.5, 192.9 against 194.8 gzipped), since the code the film and the valley share now lives in chunks of its own. The valley's sound arrives on its own once the game is up, as four small chunks: `attach-sound` (1.6 kB), `live` (6.3 kB), `reverb` (11.7 kB, the room, which the film shares) and `audio` (0.45 kB), 20.0 kB in all (7.9 kB gzipped). The film's own chunk fell from 21.4 kB to 9.9 kB. The tool that draws a night to a file is 1.4 kB and loads only on a `?debug` page when asked. No audio file is fetched, and no audio context is made before a click.

Played in real time in the sandbox's headless Chromium (44.1 kHz, a four-core machine with no sound device, a page with nothing else running), with the valley as busy as it gets, a siege at midnight (a machine at 18 blocks, the player 12 from the Core, a wave's stinger every 20 seconds, 182 sounds started in 60 seconds): setting the sound up, which makes the noise banks and the room's impulse response, takes 123 ms on the main thread, once, behind the film when there is one; telling it where the valley stands, once a frame, takes 0.018 ms on average (0.3 ms at the 99th percentile and 4.2 ms at the worst call). The browser as a whole used 0.134 of a core against 0.029 with an empty audio context running, so the synthesis costs about a tenth of one core at the walls (the offline render that draws a night takes 24.4 seconds for its 315, a little under that). This browser does not report the audio thread's own load, so the figure is the whole browser's, with the page idle: the picture is not in it.

The noise banks (three four-second buffers) and the room (3.2 seconds in each ear) are about 3.3 MB of samples, made when the sound is built and freed when it is let go. The valley builds its own, so for the length of the film, behind which it waits, two sets are alive. A tab that is hidden puts the sound to sleep.

### Known gaps in the valley's sound

- **It has not been heard.** The sandbox has no sound device. The mix is measured and the plan is checked in tests, but whether the bells are pleasant, the heartbeat too much, the riser too shy or the low note too heavy is for Austin's first listen, which is why a listening copy of night 4 came with the pull request. The low note's root is a D at 37 Hz, under what a laptop's speakers play, so on them it is heard through its upper parts.
- **The music is mine.** The spec gives no music direction, so the scales, the chords, the bell notes and their pace are my reading of "sparse music that shifts from calmer to more urgent". It is one place to change: `SCALES`, `CHORDS` and `MUSIC` in `src/shared/game-sound.ts`.
- **The title is silent.** A browser allows no sound before a click, so the valley is silent until the first click on the page, and the wind comes up with it.
- **Only the ambience, the music and the three words.** Building and breaking and power came with Phase 6c2a ([the sound of building](#phase-6c2a-the-sound-of-building)). Steps, shots, hits, the machines themselves, the Core's hits, a player going down and coming back, a drop, a breacher's charge and its blast, and the loss are Phase 6c2b. The host's events for them (`down`, `back`, `drop`, `blast`, `overrun`) have no sound yet.
- **One volume.** No separate music and effects, no mute key (both want the menus of Phase 6a), and the music is on by default for every player.
- **In the head, not in the world.** The sound is in the middle with only the bell notes panned, nearly the same in both ears. A machine's drum does not come from where it stands, and the heartbeat does not pan.
- **The tension is the player's own.** Each screen steers the sound by its own player's distance to the machines and the Core, and by its own clock; the words are the host's. Two players hear one night, each with their own tension.
- **The measured night is a script.** One player who stands still, one wave at a time. A real night has several machines at once from several sides and a player who runs, which the tension does not tell apart.
- **The numbers are first guesses.** Every level, gap and range is in `MIX` and `MUSIC`, for the first listen to move.

## Phase 6c2a: the sound of building

What the crew does with its hands now makes a sound. A block set down knocks, a block broken crackles and falls, a piece raised whirs and chimes, a module set down says what it is, a module that comes on winds up, a heap stripped snaps and rings, and a click the game turned away is answered in the player's head. SPEC section 11 lists "place/break sounds" and "power" among the effects, "from free CC0 sources or simple synthesis", and all of it is synthesis, made of the film's own voices, so there is still no audio file to license or ship and `CREDITS.md` does not change. The sounds of the fight (turret and blaster fire, hits, the machines, the Core's hits) are Phase 6c2b. The choices are in [decisions](decisions.md#phase-6c2a-the-sound-of-building-2026-10-27). **None of it has been heard**: the sandbox the game is built in has no sound device, so what follows is measured from effects drawn to samples by the page's own code, and how it sounds as sound design is for Austin's first listen.

### What building sounds like

Every screen works out the same sound for the same word of the host and plays it from where its own player stands, so a crew hears one base being built, each from where they are. The voices are the film's (`step` a dull knock, `thud` a collapse, `crackle` a snap of noise, `tick` a woody tap, `blip` a rising chirp, `pluck` a bell, `servo` a motor sliding between two pitches, `whoom`, `steam`, `rumble`), at levels set against the valley's own sound.

A cell that did not change makes no sound, and neither does a world being loaded (it is a snapshot, not a list of events). Of the modules that come on or go dark together (a Generator set down lights a whole base) only the nearest of each is heard.

### Where it is heard from

Each sound is placed against the player's eyes and the way the view faces, from the middle of its cell (a heap's middle for a strip; nowhere, in the head, for a refusal).

| Blocks away | Within 3 | 8   | 16    | 32  | 48  | Beyond 48 |
| ----------- | -------- | --- | ----- | --- | --- | --------- |
| Loss (dB)   | 0        | -6  | -12.5 | -21 | -29 | not made  |

- **The sides.** Panned up to 0.85 of the way to one ear, and drawn toward the middle within 3 blocks of the player, so that a block set at the feet is not in one ear. A sound dead ahead or behind is in both ears alike, each 3 dB under what the near ear of a sound hard to one side gets (the panner keeps the total power), which is why ahead and behind read lower in the tables below.
- **Far and behind are dull.** A lowpass from 16 kHz (open) to 600 Hz (shut) on a log scale: up to 0.8 of the way at 48 blocks (1.2 kHz there) beginning at 8 blocks, and 0.3 of the way for a sound directly behind the player. The two add and are held at 1.
- **Far is roomy.** Up to 0.3 is added to the share of a far sound that goes to the reverb, since the echo of the valley is most of what is left of it.
- **The same word, a different sound.** The seed of an effect is the cell and what is in it, so every screen makes the same knock; where it is, how loud and on which side, is each screen's own.

### How many may sound

Every kind of effect has a gap (the least time between two from one source), a priority and a life (how long its voices keep the budget busy):

| Kind                       | Priority | Gap (s) | Life (s) | Share of the 28 it may fill |
| -------------------------- | -------- | ------- | -------- | --------------------------- |
| A block set down or broken | 1        | 0.045   | 0.45     | 65%                         |
| A heap stripped            | 1        | 0.07    | 0.4      | 65%                         |
| A piece raised, a module   | 2        | 0.12    | 1.2, 0.9 | 80%                         |
| A module's power           | 2        | 0.2     | 0.9      | 80%                         |
| A click turned away        | 3        | 0.2     | 0.3      | all                         |

A source is a cell and what was done to it, a heap, or a turret. The budget is 28 voices, each costing what it works for (a held note 1, a knock 0.7, a tick or a blip 0.4, a snap of crackle 0.2), and 4 more are kept for priority 4, which the fight will use. A crowd turns away the least important first. In practice a held click, a wall being gnawed and a flurry of refusals all sound, and a thousand blocks that appear in one frame sound as thirteen.

### How it is made

- **A plan with nothing of the browser in it.** `src/shared/effect-sound.ts` holds where a sound is heard from (`hearAt`, `EARSHOT`, `dropFor` and `SPOT`), how many may sound (`KINDS`, `BUDGET` and the `Governor`) and the effects themselves (`effectForEdit`, `effectsForPower`, `effectForRefusal` and `effectForHarvest`), each a list of the film's cues. A unit test can listen to a whole wall being built in a moment.
- **The film's voices, placed.** `src/engine/audio/live.ts` has `listen(pose)`, which each frame says where the player's head is, and `effect(made)`, which places an effect against it, asks the governor, and strikes the voices a moment ahead of the audio clock through a bus of its own into the same room, so one place sets how loud all of it is next to the valley. A cue may now be dull (`muffle`) or roomy (`room`), and the voices' last stage (`output` in `voices.ts`) does it with a lowpass and more of the reverb send. The trace the tests read says which kind of effect each cue belongs to, where it was placed and at what level.
- **Wired to the game.** `src/app/attach-sound.ts` listens to what the host's world does: `session.onEvent(listener(event, previous))` for the cells, `economy.debris.onHarvest` for the heaps, `PowerWatcher.onFlips` for the power and `BuildControls.onRefused` for the clicks that were turned away, and tells the sound where the player's head is (eye height, the camera's yaw) before the effects of each frame. It makes no effect while the film plays.
- **What a cell held before.** `onEvent` on a session (`HostSession`, `GuestSession` and the hub that joins them) now hands its listeners what the cell held until the event, read from the screen's own copy of the world just before the event is applied. It never travels: nothing on the wire changed.
- **Which modules came on.** `PowerFlips` (`src/systems/survival/power-flips.ts`) compares the host's own `PowerGrid`, which every screen runs over its copy of the world, from one look to the next. Only a module that stood at both looks counts, so one just set down is not a module that came on.
- **A turned-away click.** The hands' one `setNotice` (which has the toast) calls a new `refused` port, which `createBuildControls` offers as `onRefused`; a refusal that the host sent back is a notice too.
- **Drawn as well as played.** `window.__ashbound.audio.renderEffects({ script, seconds, scene, volume, seed })` on a `?debug` page (`src/app/render-effect-sound.ts`) draws a script of effects and moves of the listener to samples over the valley's quiet morning, on an offline context stopped every six audio blocks the way the game calls the sound once a frame. It is how every level below was set, and how a listening copy was made.

### The levels, measured

The first levels were set from how loud each voice is called. They were wrong in a way that only a measurement showed: the voices differ by 15 to 30 dB in how much a nominal level says (a crackle at a nominal -10 dB came out 20 dB under the wind of a quiet morning, and a 100 Hz knock 13 dB over it), and the knocks live under 200 Hz, where a laptop's speakers have nothing, and where loudness as the ear weighs it (A-weighted) says they are no louder than the wind. So each effect was drawn alone and with the quiet morning (the wind and the Core's hum, -34.8 LUFS integrated) under it, and its loudest 100 ms (Hann window, centred on its energy, the louder ear) was read in each octave against the morning's own power in that octave. Two numbers say whether it is heard: `body`, the most it stands over the wind anywhere from 180 Hz to 5.6 kHz, and `small`, the most from 355 Hz to 2.8 kHz, where even a tiny speaker is not short of power. The targets were 13 to 20 dB for `body` and 6 to 16 for `small`, and each effect came to:

The valley's other hours are easier for the effects than the morning in the bands that carry them, since the wind falls to half at night, and the same effects stand further over it: an Alloy cube set down has 22.6 dB over a night's bed and 22.6 over a siege's in its 2 kHz octave (against 12.8 by day), and a Generator set down has 16.5 and 16.2 over them in its 1 kHz one (against 7.9). In a siege the heartbeat and the drums (-26.6 and -28.7 dB in the 63 and 125 Hz octaves, against -59.3 and -49.9 in the morning) take the bottom of a knock, which leaves its body at 250 Hz 13.3 dB over the siege.

The effects add at most 2 LUFS to a quiet morning (the busy hour does; the others add 1 or less). The busy hour peaks at -10 dBFS, 10 dB under the top and 4.5 dB under the third wave's drum (-5.5), so building never reaches the limiter. The siege (-23.9 LUFS on its own) is 11 dB louder than the morning; the effects were not set against it, because that is when the fight's own sounds come in (6c2b).

The busy hour was also run through the governor alone: 61 effects in 6.5 seconds (the click at six a second, a wall at three, six refusals and a Generator), none turned away, the load of the sounds alive peaking at 11.3 against the 18.2 that building may fill and the 28 in all. Run in a browser for 30 seconds in real time there were 262 effects and none dropped.

### What the sound costs

The first-load script is 4.0 kB larger than main's (667.7 kB against 663.8; 194.3 against 192.9 gzipped), and the multiplayer chunk is 2.0 kB smaller (148.5 kB against 150.5), since code that only the multiplayer chunk carried is now shared with the sound and so lives in the first-load script. The page downloads 2.0 kB more in all (0.6 gzipped) before a click. The sound's own chunks arrive once the game is up and grew from 20.0 kB to 27.6 kB (7.9 to 11.0 gzipped): `live` from 6.3 to 13.1 kB (the effects, the governor and the placement), `attach-sound` from 1.6 to 2.2 kB, `reverb` from 11.65 to 11.81, `audio` as it was. The tool that draws effects to a file is 0.7 kB and loads only on a `?debug` page when asked. No audio file is fetched.

Played in real time in the sandbox's headless Chromium (44.1 kHz, a four-core machine with no sound device, a page with nothing else running) with a busy hour on a loop (the click at six a second, a machine gnawing a wall at three, a refusal every 1.5 seconds: 262 effects and 1032 voices in 30 seconds), the browser as a whole used 0.33 of a core against 0.14 for the valley's own sound alone, so building costs about 0.19 of a core at its busiest. Almost all of it is the audio thread, which makes the sounds: starting an effect on the main thread (placing it, asking the governor, making and connecting its voices) took 0.63 ms on average (1.5 ms at the 99th percentile, 1.7 at worst), and the once-a-frame update 0.013 ms on average, so the main thread spends about half of one percent of a core on it. A burst the budget lets through (thirteen blocks in one frame) is about 8 ms of that frame; more than that is turned away.

The sound's memory is the valley's (the noise banks and the room) and nothing besides: a voice's nodes are let go as it ends, and the governor, once it holds more than 512 sources, forgets those older than five seconds.

### Known gaps in the sound of building

- **It has not been heard.** The sandbox has no sound device. The levels are measured against the wind and the plan is checked in tests, but whether a block set down feels set down, whether the break's crackle is too sharp, whether the chimes of a raise and the chirps of the modules are pleasant, and whether the held click at six blocks a second tires, are for Austin's first listen, which is why a listening copy came with the pull request.
- **The sounds are mine.** The spec gives no direction beyond "place/break sounds" and "power", so the knocks, the signature of each module, the chimes (fifths and octaves of D, so that they sit in the day's major and the night's minor) and the power chirps are my reading of it. They are one place to change: the cue lists in `src/shared/effect-sound.ts`.
- **Only the building.** The fight (shots, turret fire, hits, kills, a machine rising and biting, the Core hit, a player down and back, the breacher's blast, a drop, the loss) is 6c2b. The player's own steps, jumps and landings, the clicks of the hotbar and the menus, a research row opening, a Storage used and a Bed slept in have no sound yet, and a base is built in silence between the knocks.
- **A guest hears its own block one round trip late.** Every sound follows the host's word, as the block does: a guest sees and hears its block together, when the host's word for it comes back. There is no prediction of an edit.
- **Placed by distance and side only.** A wall between a block and the player does not dull it, nothing is higher or lower than anything else, and the room is the same however much is built.
- **One reach.** Every effect carries 48 blocks. A turret across a base and a blast want a longer one, which 6c2b will give each kind.
- **A burst.** A thousand blocks that change in one frame sound as thirteen, which is the aim, and cost about 8 ms of that frame.
- **The measured hour is the morning.** One listener who stands still, one cell at a time, over the quiet morning. A siege covers the lowest octaves of a knock, and the effects stand far enough over it in the middle bands, but a heavy night's sounds against them is for the play to say.
- **The numbers are first guesses.** Every level is in the cue lists, and the loss, the pan, the dullness, the room, the gaps and the budget are `FALL`, `SPOT`, `KINDS` and `BUDGET` in `src/shared/effect-sound.ts`, for the first listen to move.
