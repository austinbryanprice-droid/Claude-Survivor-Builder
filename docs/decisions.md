## Defaults (approved with the above)

- The Research Terminal and Basic Turret cost Scrap only (about 40 and 25), because the notes price them in Composite, which the terminal itself unlocks. Composite first appears with Tier 2 and pays for upgrades.
- Research points are cumulative thresholds that are never spent: 10, 20, 40, 55 and 100, unlocking the Generator and Power Node; Tier 2 Composite with the reinforced wall and auto-turret; module upgrades; Tier 3 Alloy with the electrified wall and heavy turret; and the Tier 4 Energy-Infused capstones.
- Tier 4 cells drop only from special enemies and rare anomaly nodes.
- Ration Packs refill Energy, and Energy regenerates slowly near a powered generator. Storage raises the shared pool cap. The player starts with a multi-tool and a pulse blaster.
- Enemies are the crawler, the armored crawler, the flying skimmer and the base-targeting breacher.
- Power works by radius from a generator or node, with no wiring.
- Waves follow the notes for nights 1 to 4 (6, 8, 10, then 12 with one armored). From night 5 each night adds 2 enemies and about 8 percent health, flyers arrive on night 5 and breachers on night 9.
- One basic block costs 1 Scrap, so a 3 by 3 "Basic Scrap Wall" costs 9.
- Clients own their movement and the host sanity-checks it. Each browser keeps a random player token, and the host save stores inventories by token so a resumed session can restore hotbars.
- Terrain is static in version 1: players place and break structure blocks, not ground.

## Hardware target (2026-10-20)

After the first look-spike benchmark Austin wrote: "it's expected that any computer to run this game is expected to have dedicated graphics, not integrated only." What that changes:

- Integrated-only graphics is not a target. Section 12's "integrated graphics acceptable for modest scenes" no longer applies, and the art direction no longer budgets for it.
- The 60 FPS target in sections 1 and 12 (a stable 60 FPS at 1080p on mid-range laptops from the last 4 to 5 years) reads as mid-range laptops with a dedicated GPU, unless Austin says otherwise.
- The first real benchmark, an RTX 4090 Laptop at a 200 Hz display, held 60 in every preset and stands as the look spike's real-hardware result ([numbers](art-direction.md#first-real-measurement)). No mid-range dedicated GPU has been measured, so Balanced holding 60 on one is still unproven.

## Phase 1: engine and camera

- **The local player simulates at 60 Hz, separately from the host's 20 Hz world tick.** The loop takes at most five ticks per frame and drops any backlog, so a stalled tab never spirals. The camera draws the blend between the last two ticks. Mouse look is applied every rendered frame, not per tick, so aiming never waits for a tick.
- **Movement and collision live in `src/shared`** against a `SolidQuery` over unit cells, so they run unchanged under Vitest and in the future host. The player box is 0.6 by 1.8 blocks with the eyes at 1.62, and one block is one meter.
- **Feel numbers** (all in `MOVEMENT`, `src/shared/movement.ts`): walk 4.4 and sprint 7.2 blocks per second; a standing jump rises 1.25 blocks, which clears one block but not two; gravity 30; a jump still works for 0.1 s after walking off an edge and a press is remembered for 0.12 s before landing. Space is held, not tapped: holding it hops again on every landing. A sprinting jump travels about 4.2 blocks and a walking one about 2.6.
- **Step-up came with Phase 2e.** Phase 1 collided with whole blocks only, so a one-block rise took a jump. Slabs and wedge roofs brought sub-block shapes, and the rule is under Phase 2e below: a grounded player walks up a ledge of up to 0.6 blocks, which is every slab and every step of a ramp, and a whole block still takes a jump.
- **Collision is swept per axis against unit cells** (vertical first, then the longer horizontal axis), so no speed can tunnel through a block. A body that already overlaps a block may walk out of it instead of being stuck.
- **The camera key is V or F5 (spec section 5).** F5 normally reloads the page, so while the mouse is taken the game swallows it and uses it to switch the view. With the mouse free it is left alone, and Ctrl, Cmd and Alt combinations always pass through to the browser.
- **Third person is over the right shoulder**, 3.4 blocks back. A clearance probe pulls the camera in front of walls (it snaps in and eases out) and hides the body when the camera is closer than 0.9 blocks.
- **Preferences** (mouse sensitivity, field of view, inverted look) live in `localStorage` under `ashbound.settings.v1`, and bad or missing values fall back to the defaults.
- **`?bench=1` is the frame-rate check.** It plays a fixed route counted in simulation ticks, so a slow machine flies exactly the same route as a fast one, after two unmeasured warm-up seconds. The report counts the frame rate as holding 60 when the average is at least 57 FPS and the 95th percentile frame time is at most 18.5 ms, which allows timer jitter around a 60 Hz refresh. Headless runs use software WebGL, so they test that the flow works and say nothing about speed.
- **The proving ground was a throwaway.** The walled deck (stairs, jumping gaps, doorways, tunnels, a tower, pillars) existed to test movement and went in Phase 2b when the valley moved into the game page. Its layout lives on as a helper for the unit tests (`tests/unit/helpers/proving-ground.ts`), so movement and camera tests still run on it. How terrain collision works was settled by the look spike and is built in [Phase 2b](#phase-2b-the-valley-in-the-game-page).

## Look spike (2026-10-20)

The spike lived in `look.html` and `src/spike/`, on top of the engine and world modules, and went in Phase 2b, which moved the valley and its URL switches into the game page (see [the app README](../src/app/README.md)). The spike scene was throwaway, but the engine, terrain, scatter and HUD modules it exercised were written to be kept, and are.

- **Terrain mesher: surface nets.** The seeded density field is baked to a 1 m grid (197 by 73 by 197 nodes) and meshed in 36 chunks of 32 m. The smoothed-heightfield fallback was built too (`?terrain=heightfield`) so the two could be measured on the same field. Surface nets cost 123k triangles and about 170 ms to mesh; the heightfield costs 74k triangles and 60 to 85 ms. Surface nets therefore adds about 50k triangles for each pass that draws the terrain, against a Balanced frame of 0.7 to 0.9 million, and the extra mesh time is a one-off cost at load. The heightfield cannot show rock overhangs: every outcrop and cliff turns into one stretched slope with smeared texture, which is the opposite of the smooth landforms in reference 06 and the rocks in reference 07. Terrain is static in version 1, so remeshing speed does not matter. Decision: surface nets, with the heightfield kept behind the switch as a fallback if a real machine shows the extra 50k triangles matter.
- **Terrain collision: spheres against the baked density grid.** A body is a vertical stack of three spheres (ankle, waist, chest) pushed out of the ground along the field gradient (`src/world/terrain/collision.ts`), so what is drawn is what is stood on, overhangs included. A normal facing up by more than 0.55 counts as ground. Structure blocks stay cell-based: Phase 1's movement already collides with unit cells through `SolidQuery` (`src/shared/solid.ts`), and that stays the query for blocks. Movement therefore needs two queries, one for terrain and one for blocks. `src/shared` sits below `src/world`, so Phase 2 hands the terrain query to the movement code through an interface beside `SolidQuery` instead of importing `resolveTerrain` there.
- **Presets and the frame governor.** Balanced draws at the canvas size (capped at 2.1 million pixels) with one filtered shadow map, half-resolution bloom, FXAA and a planar reflection at 0.3 scale. Low and Balanced adapt their internal resolution (floors 0.6 and 0.7) with a governor that steps down when more than a fifth of recent frames run long and probes back up after a calm spell, backing off when a probe fails. Ultra is fixed at full resolution with 4x MSAA, two shadow cascades and light shafts. The budgets are in [art-direction.md](art-direction.md#look-spike-results).
- **One benchmark.** `?bench=1` on the look page was the game page's benchmark (`src/engine/bench.ts`) flown through the valley instead of the proving ground (Phase 2b made it the only one, see below): the same two unmeasured warm-up seconds, the same route counted in simulation ticks, the same 30 second default (`?benchSeconds=`), the same report format and the same pass mark, at least 57 FPS on average and a 95th percentile of at most 18.5 ms. Phase 1's thresholds are unchanged (`src/shared/bench-report.ts`). The look page ran it once per preset with the governor off and added GPU time where the browser has timer queries, plus draw calls and triangles; the B key started it and Esc stopped it. The cloud sandbox has no GPU, so Austin's hardware gives the real numbers. The first real run (2026-10-20, an RTX 4090 Laptop at a 200 Hz display) held 60 in every preset but sat at the display cap, and no mid-range dedicated GPU has been measured yet. The numbers are in [art-direction.md](art-direction.md#first-real-measurement).
- **Day cycle.** The sun stays between 12 and 45 degrees by day and sinks to minus 26 at midnight; the curve is continuous at every seam (a unit test caught a 12 degree jump at dawn). `src/shared/daycycle.ts` uses trigonometry, so it is visual only: gameplay reads the phase and the cycle constants, never the derived values.
- **Textures.** Detailed, non-pixelated ground, rock, bark and leaf textures baked at load from seeded noise (spec section 4). References 01, 02, 03 and 08 use pixel-cube textures; Austin chose smooth, detailed textures over them on 2026-10-20 (on a decision card), so spec section 4 stands and Phase 2's structure materials follow it.
- **HUD.** An HTML, CSS and SVG component (`src/ui/player-hud/`) with original icons, laid out from reference 07 at 1280 by 720 and scaled with the window: torn dark frames, mint health in three segments, a blue stamina bar, round status icons with a timed buff, a compass strip with an orange waypoint diamond, a slate objective banner, and a frosted hotbar. It is a pure view driven by one state object. The spike drives it with made-up numbers; the survival and economy phases will feed it real ones. This replaces the earlier generic description of the HUD.

## Phase 2a: the voxel world

- **A cell is a 16-bit state: the shape in the low byte and the material in the high byte** (`src/shared/blocks.ts`). 0 is empty. A material upgrade is a new material on the same shape, so Phase 5 swaps the state and re-meshes the chunk. Shape and material ids go into saves and onto the wire, so they never change once shipped. Phase 2a defines the cube and the four tiers of spec section 9; slabs and wedges arrive in 2e as new shape ids.
- **Chunks are 16 by 16 by 16 cells**, stored sparsely in a Map keyed by chunk coordinates, and a chunk is dropped when its last block is broken. Terrain meshes are 32 m (`CHUNK_SIZE` in the terrain renderer); the two are unrelated partitions.
- **The buildable area** is the valley minus a margin of four cells at the edge (x and z from -92 to 91), and y from 0 to 95, which clears the highest terrain. Reads outside it return empty, and writes outside it are ignored.
- **Block edits go through `setBlock`, which is idempotent and cheap.** It marks the chunk dirty, and also every neighbor chunk whose mesh reads the cell (a mesh looks one cell past its own edge for hidden faces, ambient occlusion and chamfers, so a cell on a chunk face, edge or corner dirties 1, 3 or 7 neighbors). It never remeshes: the renderer drains the dirty list once per frame with a time budget.
- **Placement legality is world-state only** (`checkPlace`): a known piece, inside the buildable area, an empty cell, not buried (a block with all eight corners inside the ground is invisible and would let a player wall themselves into a hill), and supported, meaning it touches a block or its lowest corner is within 0.05 m of the terrain surface. There is no structural integrity (spec section 14), so a block that touches any other block stays. Reach, cost, "not inside a player" and rate limits belong to the host's command path in `src/net`, on top of this. `checkBreak` allows a break wherever a block is: terrain cannot be broken.
- **The net layer builds on `BlockWorld`, not on the voxel classes.** The "Multiplayer foundation" thread owns the command path (`src/net`: a host session that validates commands and emits events, a loopback transport for solo play, a PeerJS transport). Commands are place and break; events are idempotent `set { x, y, z, state, by }`; the session applies events with `setBlock`. This replaces the roadmap's plan for a separate command and event layer in Phase 2d: the game's input code calls `session.sendCommand`. A joiner learns the seed from the host and replays `forEachEdit`, so a snapshot is the seed plus the edit list (terrain never travels).

## Phase 2b: the valley in the game page

- **One page and one benchmark.** The look page, the proving ground and the Phase 1 flythrough are gone. The game opens in the valley at the lake shore (the `lake` viewpoint, facing the low sun across the water), and `?bench=1` flies the valley once for each preset with the adaptive governor off: the same state machine (`src/engine/bench.ts`), the same two warm-up seconds, 30 seconds a preset by default, and the same mark (at least 57 FPS on average, 95th percentile at most 18.5 ms). The card shows a row per preset and a report to copy; Esc stops a run. There is no B key: the URL switch starts it and the card's Run again repeats it.
- **The route no longer dips under hills.** The look spike's flight path passed about 0.3 m under a hill near four fifths of the way, which a camera that no longer ghosts through terrain cannot do. The route now stays at least 1.2 m above the ground (a smooth maximum over 0.8 m, so it bends instead of kinking). The views are the same ones; they are not identical to the spike's, so the first real numbers are not comparable frame for frame, and the cost of the scene is the same.
- **Stage and Game are two things.** `Stage` owns the renderer, sky, sun, presets and governor; `Game` owns the fixed-step loop, the local player, the camera and the benchmark. A world is a `GameWorld` (collision, spawn, route, build timings, ground height) that `src/app` builds and `Game.loadWorld(seed)` swaps. This is the seam the net layer works through: `teleport`, `loadWorld`, `onTick` and `onBeforeRender`.
- **Terrain collision is a `GroundQuery`** next to the cell `SolidQuery` (`src/shared/ground.ts`; `CollisionWorld` carries both). The body is the same three spheres (radius 0.3, centers 0.3, 0.9 and 1.5 above the feet) and the adapter pushes them out of the baked density grid. Ground facing up by more than 0.55 is walkable: the body is lifted straight up, so standing on a slope does not creep and walking uphill costs no speed. Steeper ground and ceilings push the body along the surface normal and cancel the velocity into it, so rock faces slide rather than stick. `groundSnap` (0.4 m) keeps the feet on the ground when walking downhill, but never while rising, so a jump is not cancelled. Each tick moves the body through the cells first (the Phase 1 swept box) and then pushes it out of the ground. An invisible wall stands one meter inside the edge of the terrain. Trees do not block yet; they are backdrop until a gameplay reason arrives. Falling below y = -10 returns the player to the spawn point.
- **The third-person camera treats terrain as a sphere of 0.32** around the boom, so it pulls in before a hill the way it does before a wall.
- **Photo mode.** `?shot=<name>` holds the camera at a named viewpoint (`src/world/terrain/viewpoints.ts`) with its own time of day, for screenshots that reproduce from a URL. The player still exists and simulates.
- **Graphics is a saved setting.** `Settings.quality` joins sensitivity, field of view and inverted look. The pause panel's Graphics choice and the P key change and save it, and `?quality=` overrides it for one visit without saving. The pause panel also has a Time of day choice (golden hour, the other moments, or the day and night cycle) until the clock arrives in Phase 4; golden hour is the default and is what the screenshots show.
- **The HUD is the reference's, with placeholder numbers.** The player HUD from the look spike is on the game page. The compass follows the view and stamina drains while sprinting; health, status icons, the objective and the tools on the hotbar are made up until the survival and economy phases feed it. The number keys and the wheel pick hotbar slots while the mouse is taken; since Phase 2d the first four slots hold the four block materials (see below).
- **Seed and mode switches.** `?seed=` builds another valley and `?terrain=heightfield` meshes it as the fallback. Both are for measurement and tests, not for players.

## Phase 2c: block meshing and materials

- **Every block is a chamfered cube, meshed per face.** The chamfer is 0.08 m (`BEVEL` in `src/world/voxels/mesher.ts`; the spec allows 0.05 to 0.12). A block is up to four kinds of piece: a panel for each exposed face, pulled in by the bevel; a strip along each exposed edge; a triangle across each exposed corner; and a small cap where four blocks meet along an edge. Faces and chamfers that a neighbor hides are dropped, so a wall is one closed surface with a groove between its blocks, and nothing is ever merged into a bigger face (no greedy merging, as decided). The caps were found by the tests: without them, four chamfered blocks around an edge leave a diamond shaft open to the air. `tests/unit/voxel-mesher.test.ts` welds the vertices of random worlds and checks that every edge is used exactly twice, in opposite directions, that no triangle is degenerate, and that winding agrees with the normals, across chunk borders too.
- **Meshing is pure.** `meshChunk(world, cx, cy, cz)` reads cells through `BlockWorld.getBlock` (a block shows what the 26 cells around it say, which is why `setBlock` dirties neighbor chunks) and returns typed arrays. Only `src/world/render/voxel-render.ts` turns them into three.js geometry, so the mesher can move into a Worker unchanged if Phase 6 needs it. The hot path allocates nothing; a chunk that holds a hollow 16 by 8 by 16 room (28 thousand triangles, a very dense chunk) meshes in about 7 ms on the sandbox CPU, a typical base chunk (a few hundred blocks, 10 thousand triangles or fewer) in 1 to 3 ms.
- **Vertex data.** Position and normal in world space (the meshes sit at the origin, like the terrain's), tile coordinates, and four bytes: ambient occlusion, material id, primitive kind (low two bits) with the face's tile layout above it, and the block's random seed. Ambient occlusion is per vertex from the three cells beside and diagonal to each face corner (four levels: 0.46, 0.64, 0.82 and 1.0); chamfers on a hidden edge and the shafts between blocks take a groove value of 0.58, which is what draws the seam between two blocks. Each quad splits along the diagonal joining its brighter corners, so a gradient does not run along a crease.
- **One material for every block, one tile per face.** A block's material id picks a layer of the baked tile arrays, so a Phase 5 upgrade is a new number in the cell and a re-mesh, never a different mesh or material. The tiles are baked on the GPU at load into array render targets (`StructureTextures` in `src/engine/texture-bake.ts`): per material tier, `TILE_LAYOUTS` (4) different layouts of albedo with height in alpha, and surface data (gloss, metalness, glow). Each face of each block picks a layout from its cell's hash and turns it one of eight ways, so a wall is never one stamp repeated. The outer 0.08 of a tile is what the chamfers show and stays plain, a dark edge that makes the groove read. Tile coordinates come from projecting each piece onto its owner face, so strips, corners and caps land in that plain band. The tiles are smooth and detailed, never pixel cubes (spec section 4). They are baked the first time a block is meshed (`Stage.structure`, or `VoxelRenderer.warmUp`), not at start-up: 16 layers at 512 by 512 (256 on Low), twice over with mipmaps, are about 43 MB of video memory (11 MB on Low), and under software rendering the bake takes about 9 seconds at Balanced, so a world with no blocks pays nothing. A real GPU does it in a few milliseconds. Phase 2d calls `warmUp` when the player enters Build Mode, or three seconds after first taking the mouse if that comes first, so the bake and the shader's first compile do not land on the frame of the first block.
- **The four tiers.** Scrap: patchwork plates of salvaged metal in rust, teal paint and grey primer, riveted, with run-off streaks, a corrugated sheet and a bolted repair plate in some layouts. Reinforced Composite: laminated grey-green panels (bands, twill weave, ribs, a honeycomb window) with a cyan trim. Advanced Alloy: brushed steel with amber markings, a vent grille and hazard stripes. Energy-Infused: dark graphite with cyan veins and conduits that pulse. Everything is generated from the shared noise helpers; nothing is imported (`CREDITS.md` has no new entries).
- **Lighting.** The structure shader takes the terrain's sun, shadow, haze and ambient (the same `ATMOSPHERE_GLSL` and `sunShadow`), multiplies direct and ambient light by the vertex occlusion, adds a specular and a rough environment term for the metals (a rough surface sees `ambientLight` of the reflection vector, a polished one the sky above and the ground below), a little drift of brightness across a structure and per block, and lets the Energy tier glow and pulse into the bloom. Two things went wrong with Advanced Alloy at golden hour, the game's default light. The first version reflected `skyGradient` on every metal, which washed it out to cream; the rough environment term fixed that. Then the deep orange key (3.5, 1.6, 0.5) turned steel tan, and a wall of it read as wooden crates. A polished surface has little diffuse color of its own and shows what it reflects, so a metallic tile's diffuse term now takes the key only part of the way (up to 60 percent toward its grey), while glints keep the full color. The 2e lighting pass is where the look gets checked against references 04 and 05.
- **The remesh budget.** `VoxelRenderer.update` takes the world's dirty chunks into a `RemeshQueue`, orders them nearest the camera first (ties by chunk key, so the order never depends on insertion), and meshes until 3 ms of a frame are spent, always at least one chunk. A late-join snapshot or a whole outpost therefore spreads over frames, while the block a player has just placed is in the chunk nearest the camera and so is meshed first. One draw call per chunk that holds a block.
- **Plants give way to blocks.** Trees and grass are generated from the seed alone and cached, so they cannot know about player edits. When the vegetation renderer assembles what to draw (each time the camera moves a meter, and when `VoxelWorld.revision` says a block changed), it skips grass rooted inside or on top of a block and trees whose trunk a block stands in (`rootBuried` and `trunkBlocked` in `src/world/scatter.ts`). Trees still do not block movement or building.
- **`?demo=outpost` and `?demo=swatch`** raise a structure before the first frame, for looking at the blocks until players can build: a walled outpost with a gate, windows, a courtyard roof, four towers, a core, a stair and lamps (338 blocks, 10 thousand triangles), and four walls with a cube in front of each, one per tier. Each demo places its blocks the way a player would, one at a time and only where `checkPlace` allows, so it is also a check of the placement rules (the unit tests replay the edits on a fresh world and count zero refusals). A bare `?demo` raises the outpost; a name that is not a demo does nothing. The viewpoints `outpost`, `gate` (golden hour), `swatch` and `closeup` photograph them.

## Phase 2d: building by hand

- **The spec's controls.** Right click places the selected block, left click breaks the block under the cursor (in the block slots: with the pulse blaster in hand, from Phase 4e on, left click fires it instead), and both work with or without Build Mode ("Outside Build Mode, right-click still places if a block is selected", spec section 8). B turns Build Mode on and off; it adds what the spec lists for it, the ghost of the piece, its name and cost, and the valid or invalid highlight, plus the key list in the status panel. Holding a button repeats after 0.35 s and then every 0.15 s, and the same command is not sent twice within half a second, because the first answer may still be on its way. The mouse buttons, the number keys and the wheel count only while the mouse is taken, and giving the mouse back lets go of every held button. The wheel moves one slot for each 60 pixels of travel (a notch of a wheel, or a decent swipe on a trackpad) and wraps. The hotbar has the reference's eight slots, so the 9 key does nothing (spec section 8 says 1 to 9).
- **A click is a command and nothing else.** `src/app/build-controller.ts` never changes the world. A click becomes `place` or `break` for `session.sendCommand`, in solo play too; the host validates it, and the block appears when its event comes back. A refusal comes back through `onRejected` and stays on the screen for 1.8 s. The network code loads a moment after the game (and never with `?bench=1`), so a click before that is answered with "Still connecting".
- **Aiming.** The cursor's ray starts level with the player's eyes, not at the camera, so in third person nothing between the camera and the player can be hit or built on. It finds blocks cell by cell and the terrain by marching along the ground's signed distance (`src/shared/raycast.ts`). A block goes on the face that was hit; on terrain it goes in the cell the surface passes through, which is where the support rule wants it. Reach is 6 blocks from the eyes to the middle of the cell, the host's own number (`NET.editReach`, which the host loosens by 3 for lag); the cursor looks 2 blocks farther, so a spot just out of reach says "Out of reach" instead of going quiet. `src/shared/build-aim.ts` applies the rules in the host's order, reach, then the world's (`checkPlace`), then nobody standing in the cell, so a click that passes on the screen passes at the host. A player counts as the controller's box made 0.05 bigger on every side, the local one and every remote one from the session's `sampleRemotes`; the host repeats the check with positions it trusts.
- **What the screen says.** In Build Mode a translucent copy of the beveled block (the mesher's own cube, so it shows the shape that will appear) sits on the cell a click would fill, cyan when the click would work and red when it would not, with a slow breath so it reads as a preview, and a white outline marks the block a click would break (orange when that is refused). The crosshair gets a cyan ring over a surface a block can go on, in Build Mode or out of it, and a red ring in Build Mode when the click would be refused. The caption above the hotbar names the piece in hand with its price ("Scrap Block", "Cost 1 Scrap"), in Build Mode and for 1.6 s after a new choice, and becomes the reason in red when a click is refused ("Out of reach", "A player is in the way", "Needs ground or a block to rest on"). The words for every reason are in `src/ui/build-hud.ts`; the host's own words pass through capitalized. Build Mode also swaps the status panel's key hints for its own list; a separate legend sat behind that panel at the bottom right.
- **The hotbar.** Slots 1 to 4 hold one block of each material in tier order (Scrap, Reinforced Composite, Advanced Alloy, Energy-Infused), drawn as original icons on the cyan grid of placeable items; slots 5 to 8 keep the placeholder tools until the inventory arrives. A slot with no block places nothing (interacting with what is aimed at comes with the modules). Slabs and wedges are chosen with Q, which changes the shape of every block slot (see Phase 2e): eight slots cannot hold every shape in every material.
- **The price is shown, not charged.** The caption reads the material table's `cost` (1 of the material for every piece, for now). Nothing is deducted: there is no inventory until Phase 5, and the host's command path has no cost rule yet. The README says so.
- **Getting the first block ready.** A page's first block needs the tile arrays baked and its shader compiled, a few milliseconds of bake and a driver compile on a real GPU and many seconds under software rendering. `warmUp` does both when Build Mode starts, or three seconds after the mouse is first taken, whichever comes first, and again for a valley that a joining player's room has replaced. The compile uses a real one-block mesh as its primer: three.js picks a program by the geometry's attributes as well as the material (normals, tile coordinates, the extra vertex bytes), and a bare stand-in compiled a different program, so the first real block still compiled a second one. The browser test counts the programs and expects the first block to add none. The ghost block's own shader is compiled with it.
- **The benchmark has no hands.** `?bench=1` never loads the network code, the building keys and clicks are ignored during a sweep, nothing is baked or compiled for blocks it never draws, and the ghost stays hidden, so it measures the game alone (a browser test covers it).
- **Left alone.** Trees still do not block building. `window.__ashbound.build` (`aim()`, `hands()`, `ghost()`) shows the browser tests what the building hands see.

## Phase 2e: slabs and wedges

2e is built in steps, each a pull request of its own: the shapes and how they are drawn, then walking on them, then choosing and placing them by hand, then the look pass.

- **Three kinds of piece, one number each.** A shape id names the kind and its turn, so a placed piece is still one 16-bit state and nothing else has to travel with it (`src/shared/blocks.ts`). The cube stays 1. A slab is 8 for the lower half of its cell and 9 for the upper half. A wedge is 16 plus the way it rises (0 toward +x, 1 toward +z, 2 toward -x, 3 toward -z), plus 4 when it is turned upside down, which puts the flat face on top and the slope underneath for an eave or an overhang. A wedge is the cell cut along its diagonal, so its slope is 45 degrees and rises one block over one block; a roof of wedges has the steep pitch of the roofs in reference 05. These ids never change once shipped, like the others.
- **One piece to a cell.** No double slab (two slabs are a cube), no half wedge, no stair or corner shapes. A roof is a row of wedges, a floor a row of slabs. Every piece comes in all four materials, and the material still sits in the high byte, so a Phase 5 upgrade is a new number on the same shape. Placement checks (support, buried, bounds) treat every shape as an occupied cell.
- **A shape is data** (`src/shared/shapes.ts`). Each is a convex solid given by its corners and the loops of its faces, and three things are worked out from it: the faces the mesher draws, the boxes movement collides with, and, for each side of the cell, the rectangle the shape fills exactly (`cover`) and the rectangle where its face lies (`reach`). A neighbor hides its face against a shape only when `cover` contains that face's `reach`, so a cube beside a slab still shows the half of its side that the slab does not hide, and the back of a wedge hides the face behind it.
- **A shape is drawn the way a cube is.** `buildTemplate` (`src/world/voxels/shape-template.ts`) turns a shape into the same three kinds of piece: a panel for each face, pulled in from every edge by the bevel (0.08), a strip along each edge, and a corner for each vertex. Slopes and slab sides therefore get the same chamfer and the same groove as cubes. A panel that a neighbor covers is dropped. Strips and corners are always drawn: they close the surface even where the panel is hidden, and where two shapes meet only in part, the open ring that is left lies inside another drawn panel. The cube keeps its own, unchanged code path (no greedy merging, as decided). `tests/unit/voxel-mesher.test.ts` and `tests/unit/voxel-shapes-mesh.test.ts` weld the vertices of random worlds of mixed shapes, chunk borders included, and check that every edge is used exactly twice, in opposite directions, and that winding agrees with the normals.
- **Tiles are fitted to the face.** A panel's tile is stretched over the face's bounding rectangle, so the side of a slab shows a whole tile squeezed to half its height and a slope shows it stretched by the diagonal (1.41 times). Strips and corners read the plain dark band at the tile's rim, as the cube's do. Ambient occlusion comes from the cells around the piece, and the top of a slab reads the cells level with it. Whether the squeeze reads well is for the look pass (2e-4) to judge.
- **`?demo=hall`** raises a gabled hall for looking at the shapes: a platform with a ramp of wedges, composite walls with an arched door and slab sills, a lamp post either side of the ramp, and a roof of wedges four rows deep with eaves and gable overhangs, 416 pieces (298 cubes, 12 slabs, 106 wedges, about 12 thousand triangles). `?shot=hall` photographs it from the south.
- **Walking on shapes.** Movement reads a cell that is not a whole cube as boxes (`SolidQuery.boxesAt`, optional, so a world of whole cubes needs nothing new): a slab is one box, a wedge is a stair of 32 thin boxes, each as high as the highest point of its slice, so a body never sinks into the surface that is drawn. The swept test (`src/shared/collision.ts`) takes the nearest face among those boxes instead of the cell's boundary; a whole cube still uses the boundary and costs nothing extra.
- **Step-up and step-down.** `MOVEMENT.stepHeight` is 0.6. A body that stood on the ground at the start of a tick and is blocked sideways is lifted by up to that much, tries the move again and comes back down, and keeps the climb if it got further than the plain slide (the step slide of Quake). 0.6 clears a slab (0.5) and every step of a 45 degree ramp, but not a whole block, so a block still takes a jump (1.25 high), and a body in the air steps up nothing. Walking down is the other half: a body that stood on cells is set back on the next one when it is within `groundSnap` (0.4) below, every tick, because a roof of wedges falls away faster than gravity pulls. Done only on ticks that began in the air, it made a descent jerk down every other tick.
- **What the body feels like on a slope.** A flat-bottomed box meets a 45 degree slope at its uphill edge, so on a roof of wedges the middle of the feet floats up to 0.3 above the surface (half the body's width). The terrain avoids this with spheres. It is small in first person; if it shows on the avatar in third person, the fix is a rounder foot for cells, left for now. A climb is a string of steps of 1/32 block, so the eye rises in small even steps (at walking speed 0.06 or 0.09 a tick).
- **The host's checks.** The guest move guard's test for "inside a wall" asks whether the middle of the body is inside the part of a cell that is filled (`pointSolid`), so standing on a slab or beside a ramp is not "buried". "A player is in the way" of a placement, and the aim's version of it, stay whole-cell tests: a slab is refused in a cell that a body overlaps even when only the empty half does. That is the safe direction, and changing the host's rules is not part of this step.
- **Choosing a shape: Q and R.** Slots 1 to 4 stay the four materials, and the shape is a second choice that applies to whichever material is in hand: Q goes block, slab, wedge, block, and R turns the piece. Eight slots cannot hold three shapes in four materials, and a player building a roof stays on one material and changes the shape often, so one key that changes every slot is quicker than a longer hotbar or a menu. Both keys sit by the movement keys, work with or without Build Mode (like the number keys) and do nothing during `?bench=1`. Choosing a new shape starts the turn over. The hotbar draws its four block slots as the piece they would make, the caption names it ("Scrap Wedge", "Cost 1 Scrap"), and the key list of Build Mode lists Q and R.
- **The cursor turns the piece; R adjusts.** A player should not have to turn every ramp by hand, so the aim chooses the orientation (`src/shared/placement.ts`, pure, so the preview and the click cannot disagree) and R is an offset from it. A slab lies low on the top of a block and hangs high under one; against a wall, and on the ground, it goes in the half of the cell the cursor is on. The ground cuts through its cell at any height, and a slab in the half under the ground would never show: the first browser test of this step built exactly that, a slab buried in the hill that the cursor could not find. A wedge rises away from the player (the look direction, taken to the nearest of the four axes), so a row of them placed while walking forward is a ramp to walk up, and under a ceiling it is turned upside down. R turns a wedge a quarter at a time and, after four steps, flips it and turns again (eight positions); it flips a slab; a block has one position. The offset is from the view, so it means the same thing whichever way the player faces.
- **The ray meets the shape.** The ray that aims, breaks and places used to see a slab or a wedge as a whole cell. It now meets the shape where it is, by testing the ray against the shape's face planes (every shape is convex), so the cursor goes on past the empty half of a lower slab and lands on a wedge's slope where the slope is; a unit test checks it against an independent triangle test for thousands of random rays at a world of mixed shapes. A block goes in the cell across the face that was hit when that face lies on the edge of its cell. For a face inside the cell (the top of a lower slab, the underside of an upper one) or a slanting one (the slope of a wedge) it goes in the neighbor along the axis the face points along most, and along the vertical on a tie, so a block put on a slope goes above it. The break outline and the ghost follow the same shapes.
- **Stacking.** One piece to a cell means a block put on a lower slab goes in the cell above, half a block over the slab's top, and two slabs stacked in a column leave a gap the cursor can see into but a block cannot go in ("Something is already there"). A stair is a row of slabs one cell apart. Letting a block replace a slab, or two pieces share a cell, would break the one number per cell that the save and the wire format rest on, so that is left to a later decision if it proves wrong in play.
- **The ghost and the outline are the real shape.** The ghost is the mesher's own skin for the oriented shape (so a wedge shows its slope and its chamfers), and the break outline is the edges of the shape of the block it marks, both made on first use and kept. The debug hooks report the shape each is drawn in (`build.ghost()`), and `build.aim()` reports the piece a click would place.
- **Hotbar icons.** Each of the four materials has a slab and a wedge icon beside the block's. They are not new art: the block's own paint, bevel panels and marks are carried onto each face of the shape by an exact transform (the slab squeezes the side faces to half their height, the wedge stretches the top into the slope and keeps the lower half of the right face as its side), so the rust, the cyan trim, the hazard band and the veins all show.
- **The lighting pass measured first (2e-4).** Before touching a light I compared crops of our structures with references 04 and 05 for brightness and saturation (the table is in `docs/art-direction.md`). Overall brightness was already inside the references' range, so this is not a pass to brighten the structures: a structure's median brightness was 0.14 to 0.34 against 0.16 to 0.45 in the references. What fell outside was the shade and one hue. The darkest 5 percent of ours was lower than the references' (0.06 to 0.09 against 0.08 to 0.21), the fill in shade was the violet of the golden-hour sky, which turns dark steel to plum where the references' stone and timber stay neutral, and Composite went yellow-olive in the low sun where the references' sunlit materials stay close to their own hue.
- **The changes are in the structure shader, none in the terrain or the sky.** The look spike's light was approved, so only what players build changes (`src/world/render/structure-material.ts`, constants at the top of the shader). The sky's fill on a structure is pulled 45 percent toward a cool grey and made 30 percent stronger. The low sun's orange is pulled 30 percent of the way toward grey on every surface, plus 45 percent times how metallic it is (before it was 60 percent times how metallic and nothing for a plain surface), so grey-green Composite stays grey-green and steel stays steel. A polished surface shows more of the sky face on (0.55 of its reflection rather than 0.3) and metal reflects at full strength rather than 0.85, so a steel roof is steel and not a dark slab. Measured on the same viewpoints, the darkest 5 percent rises by 0.01 to 0.02, the median by 0.01 to 0.03, saturation falls by 0.02 to 0.06, and Composite's saturation in the sun at the gate falls from 0.50 to 0.33. Noon barely moves (the swatch's mean brightness goes from 0.504 to 0.510).
- **Shade where a wall meets the ground.** The corner shading between blocks cannot see the terrain, so a wall met the ground like a toy on a table. The mesher now takes the terrain's height (`GroundHeight`, optional) and darkens the occlusion of each vertex by its height above the ground under it: half as bright on the ground, easing to nothing at a meter (`CONTACT_BRIGHTNESS`, `CONTACT_HEIGHT`). It is per vertex, so it fades up a wall, adds no geometry and no shader work, and the terrain never changes so no chunk is meshed again for it. A first try at a third the brightness over 1.2 meters was visible but heavy: the whole one-block platform of the hall went dark.
- **Left alone on purpose.** The tile art and its density (approved in 2c), the bevel and the groove, and the stretch of a tile over a slope (1.41 times) and its squeeze on a slab's side. I looked at the squeeze and the stretch in the Balanced shots of the hall: they read as long panels on a roof and thin plates on a sill, and the amber marks keep their shape well enough, so they stay in the known gaps rather than being fixed with a second set of tile coordinates. The tiles remain busier than the references' calm stone and timber; that is a decision about the art, not the light, and it is Austin's.

## Phase 3: multiplayer foundation (2026-10-21)

How it works, how to try it and what the tests prove are in [multiplayer.md](multiplayer.md). This records the choices.

- **Owner decisions applied.** Clients own their movement and the host sanity-checks it; each browser keeps a random player token; the host owns blocks, as in the defaults above. For friends on strict networks Austin chose, on a decision card (2026-10-21), to keep PeerJS's free shared relay: the library's own list of ICE servers (a Google STUN server and the PeerJS project's TURN servers in the EU and the US, best effort). Nothing is set up or paid for, and a friend who cannot connect sees "Could not connect to the host" with the likely reason. The address already takes relays of his own (`?ice=`), so revisit this if it happens to him.
- **Host-authoritative, one command path, solo included.** Single-player is the host session with nobody in the room, so placing a block is the same call alone or in a room of four. The in-memory transport that Phase 2 planned for single-player became the test network instead: whole rooms run on virtual time with latency, jitter, loss, reordering and dead links.
- **Rooms.** A code is five characters from 32 symbols (no I, O, 0 or 1), 33.5 million of them, registered as the PeerJS id `ashbound-CODE` and redrawn up to eight times if the matchmaking server says it is taken. Up to four players; a fifth is told the room is full. Case, spaces, dashes and a pasted invite link all work when typing a code.
- **Two lanes per guest, both raw.** `ctl` is reliable and ordered and carries JSON control frames of at most 20,000 characters, with snapshots in frames of at most 1,000 edits. `fast` is `reliable: false` and carries binary state: 31 bytes from a guest, 27 per player in the host's relay. Both use PeerJS's `raw` serialization, so the game owns the format and checks everything from the other side. `reliable: false` is unordered but Chromium still retransmits, so a stale packet is dropped by its sequence number rather than lost (architecture rule 6).
- **Joining replaces the page's world.** A guest builds the terrain from the host's seed and applies the host's edits, which is the whole snapshot (terrain never travels). Blocks the guest built alone are discarded, and the start card says so. A valley with the same seed and nothing built on it is kept instead of rebuilt, because a rebuild stalls the page for a second or two.
- **Movement.** Sent about 20 times a second, relayed at most that often, drawn 100 ms behind the newest sample, carried forward for at most 250 ms when samples run late, and drawn as a teleport past 6 blocks or on a reset flag. The host's check allows 1.35 times the controller's top speeds with a bank of 1.5 s of movement, and moves a guest back after 600 ms inside a block. A correction bumps the guest's epoch so stale packets are ignored, and it does not refill the bank. A reset flag (the respawn after falling out of the world) is believed only when the last accepted position was below y = -1: the lowest ground in the seeds tried is 2.1 to 3.4, and the game returns a player to the start below -10, but the report before that can be a few blocks higher. A scripted sprint-and-hop tour of the real valley (hills, lake floor, edge wall, a 60 m fall) passes the check even with a slack of 1.0, so 1.35 leaves a margin. It is a sanity check against glitches and honest mistakes, not anti-cheat; a determined player can still dive under the world and claim a respawn anywhere. Version 1 plays with friends.
- **Tokens and seats.** The token lives in the tab's session storage, so a second tab is a second player. The host remembers the last 16 guests who left: the same tab returning gets its player id, colour and last safe position back; a live member heard from in the last 3 s means the newcomer is a new player. Names live in local storage. Tokens that survive a browser restart arrive with saves in Phase 6.
- **Host leaves, session ends (spec sections 6 and 14).** Guests get a full-screen notice, "The host left the game" for a closed room or tab and "Lost the connection to the host" after 10 s of silence, with Play alone (the world as it was) or join another room. The start card, the room card and the README all say there is no host migration and no automatic reconnection. A host closing the tab while others are in is asked first. A page that was itself blocked for more than two seconds does not hold the silence against the other side (the packets that arrived meanwhile are read after the timer), so a frozen browser tab does not end the room when it wakes; the 10 s limit counts again from the stall's end.
- **Seats start beside the spawn point,** 1.8 blocks apart in a row, above anything built there (`src/app/seat-spawn.ts`), so nobody starts inside a wall or on top of another player.
- **Network switches.** Rooms go through the public PeerJS server by default. `?broker=`, `?ice=`, `?join=` and `?name=` change that for tests and private servers; an invite link keeps the network switches and drops the rest, and the room card says so when the network is not the usual one, because a link can point the game at another server.
- **Multiplayer loads on demand.** `src/app/multiplayer.ts` takes four narrow views of the game (the player, the world, the scene and the mouse) and is updated once a frame; `src/app/attach-multiplayer.ts` is the only file that knows `Game`. The glue loads after the game has started and PeerJS loads when a room is first opened, so `?bench=1` loads neither and the benchmark measures the game alone.
- **Blocks sync as data; the voxel thread draws and places them.** Place and break commands travel and change every player's world, and since 2c every world draws them. The build controls (2d, which call `session.sendCommand`) belong to the voxel thread and are in; the browser tests still place blocks through the `?debug` hook (`window.__ashbound.mp`) when they need one without a mouse. A page opened with `?demo=` raises its structure in every valley it builds, so the glue empties the valley a room gives it before the host's snapshot arrives: a room's world is the host's blocks and nothing else.
- **Accepted limits.** A hidden or covered host tab slows the room, because browsers throttle its timers (the card tells the host to keep the tab visible; the host Worker in Phase 4 will fix most of it). A wrong room code takes about five seconds to report, because the matchmaking server holds the introduction that long. There is no kick, chat or spectator mode. A host and a guest on different protocol versions refuse each other and say why.

## Phase 4a: the day and night clock (2026-10-21)

Phase 4 (spec section 15: a day and night timer with a lighting shift, Health and Energy, an enemy that paths toward the base, escalating waves, soft respawn and a results screen) is six small PRs in a row instead of the roadmap's three, because each of them shows something and none of them is large: (a) the clock, (b) the Outpost Core and the vitals, (c) pathing and the crawler, (d) the pulse blaster, (e) the waves, (f) respawn charges and the Outpost Overrun screen. Owner decisions that bind the phase (day 8 and night 4 minutes; an Outpost Core that ends the run at 0 health plus shared respawn charges; models and audio generated in code; the look of the references, HUD included) are applied as written.

- **The clock is the host's, counted in steps.** `SurvivalSim` (`src/systems/survival/sim.ts`) counts whole 20 Hz steps, so the clock is exact however frames and timers fall, and a Worker, a test and the browser play the same run. Day 1 comes first, then night 1, then day 2: a night is numbered like the day before it. Events (nightfall, dawn) fire when a step crosses the boundary; a run that starts at night (`?night=N`) does not announce the night it starts in. The day and night lengths stay in `src/shared/daycycle.ts` because the sky reads them too.
- **The sky follows the run.** The sun rises at the start of the day, the long golden afternoon lasts the day, dusk takes the first 40 seconds of the night, and the dawn the last 40, so the light turns before the enemies of the later parts would. A stage with no moment held asks the game's clock for the sky (`Stage.setSkyDriver`); `?time=`, a named `?shot=` and the compressed demo `?clock` still keep the sky to themselves, and the benchmark page, which never loads the run, stays at golden hour. The pause card's Time of day choice, a stand-in until the clock existed, is now a development preview behind `?debug`.
- **The run waits for the player, alone.** A solo player can pause with Esc, so their clock stops while the mouse is free (and before the first click on Play); with a guest in the room nothing waits, because the others are playing, and the host's pause card does not stop their night. The pause card says so ("the clock waits while you are away"). A page that was blocked or throttled catches up by at most two seconds a tick and drops the rest, so a night is never replayed in the second a tab comes back.
- **Protocol version 2.** `sv` (the host's step count and whether the clock runs) goes out about twice a second, at once on a change and right after the snapshot for a newcomer; `se` announces a change of light. A guest carries the last word forward for at most three seconds and never lets a countdown tick backwards. The sim lives in the host session and the guests hold a mirror; the screen reads both through one `SurvivalView`.
- **The indicator is small.** The spec asks for a "subtle day/night or wave indicator", so it is a pill under the compass (sun or moon, "Day 2", the time left, a thin progress bar, a slow breath for the last 30 seconds), and the objective banner says what the hour asks ("Build up before nightfall", "Hold the outpost until dawn") in place of the placeholder. A toast marks the change of light.
- **The host Worker is not in this part.** Spec section 6 asks for a host that owns the enemies, the waves and the health, not for a Worker, and nothing in 4a needs one: the clock keeps real time in a hidden tab because it catches up. What a hidden tab still costs is the relay of players, and later the machines' movement, at one tick a second. The simulation is pure and fed elapsed time, so a Worker that only keeps the metronome (a timer a browser does not throttle) can drive `HostSession.step` without changing the simulation. That is a late, optional PR once enemies exist and the cost can be measured; this entry is where the outcome goes.
- **For trying it.** `?night=3` starts at nightfall of night 3 and `window.__ashbound.mp.warp(seconds)` (with `?debug`) jumps the clock ahead, taking every step on the way.

## Phase 4b: the Outpost Core (2026-10-21)

Phase 4 is seven small PRs now, not six: (a) the clock, (b) the Outpost Core (this part), (c) Health and Energy in the top-left vitals, (d) flow-field pathing and the crawler, (e) the pulse blaster and damage, (f) the wave director, (g) down, respawn charges and the Outpost Overrun screen. Health and Energy left the Core's PR because each of them needs its own host state, wire frame and pictures; [roadmap.md](roadmap.md) has the new list. This is the Outpost Core that Austin approved on 2026-10-20: the run ends when its health reaches 0.

- **The Core is part of the valley, not made of blocks.** It is a box of 3 by 3 cells and 4 high at the middle of the crash site, with its base at the middle height of the ground under it, because the crater floor is not level (`src/world/outpost.ts`; on the default seed the ground under it runs from 9.5 to 10.3, so it fills y 10 to 14). A little of the plinth is buried where the ground is high, and its drawn foundation reaches a block below the base so there is no gap where the ground is low; only the box is solid. Sinking it to the lowest ground, as a first version did, buried much of the plinth on the high sides, because the bowl rises round the Core. `VoxelWorld` holds it as an optional `Outpost` and answers for it: `isSolid` is true inside the box, so movement, the host's checks and the enemies of 4d see a wall, `checkPlace` refuses a block in it with `reserved` ("The Outpost Core needs that room"), and a block against its face counts as touching something, so a wall can be built up to it and along it. The ground the cursor and the camera measure (`withOutpost`) knows it too, so the crosshair stops on its faces and the ghost goes against them.
- **Why not blocks.** Breakable blocks would make the Core's health a sum of cells and let a player dig it out of the ground, and they would put it into every save and snapshot. As a feature of the valley it costs nothing on the wire (every peer works the cells out from the same seed's ground, like the lake), it is never an edit, and it has one health number with one place to draw it.
- **Health and mending are data** (`SURVIVAL.core`): 1000 points, which later phases scale with the Core's upgrades. Nothing mends at night. By day it mends 1000 points in 240 seconds, one step at a time, and a step that begins in daylight mends, so the step that lands on the dawn is not lost: a bad night is repaired over the day that follows, a Core on its last points is not. Until the enemies of 4d, the `?debug` hook `mp.hurtCore(n)` is the only thing that can hurt it.
- **When it falls, the run is over.** The sim reports `over` at 0 health, the clock stops for good (with guests in the room too: nothing is left to wait for), the host announces one `overrun` event with the cycle it fell in, and nothing mends it again. Nights survived is the cycle it fell in, less one (a Core that falls on night 2 survived one night), counted by `nightsSurvived` for the results screen. 4b shows the end on the HUD and the pause card only; the Outpost Overrun screen with Retry and New Run is 4h, so the card says what has happened and offers nothing else yet.
- **Protocol version 3.** `sv` now carries `core: [health, max]` and `over`. It goes out as before (about twice a second, at once on a change), and also when the Core's health has moved and 200 milliseconds (`NET.coreIntervalMs`) have passed since the last frame, so a hit shows on every screen within a fifth of a second without a frame per hit. The guests' mirror keeps the last word like it keeps the clock's.
- **The gauge on the HUD.** A second pill under the clock: a crystal icon, "Core", a bar and the number. By night it is always up, by day only once the Core is hurt (a whole Core by day needs no attention), and it turns amber below half, red below a quarter and grey when the Core has fallen. A hit flashes the pill, and the red one breathes. It is drawn from the same readings as the clock, in the same frame.
- **The reactor is drawn from code.** A dark steel plinth with amber trim, four pylons with cyan caps and a frame joining them, a cyan crystal that turns and bobs in the cage with a thin ring about it, and a plate of light under it: the cyan of the accents in the art direction, on the amber-trimmed steel of the blocks. It is six meshes, four merged by material plus the crystal and the ring; in view it adds 11 draw calls and 24 thousand triangles to a Balanced frame of about 105 draws and 747 thousand (every pass counted, measured below the cost table in the art direction). The crystal is the health gauge in the world: it is cyan while sound, amber when worn and red near the end (the same marks as the HUD), beats faster as the Core weakens, flickers below 40 percent, flashes white when it is hit, and sits dark, grey and tilted when the Core has fallen. A small `facets` option on the shared lit material dims the glow on faces turned away from the viewer, so the crystal reads as cut facets instead of a flat shape.
- **Left for later on purpose.** The wreckage and the scorched patch round the Core (art direction), the Core's upgrades and repair by hand (Phase 5), and any light cast by the crystal on the ground: the scene has no point lights, the glow blooms and that is all. The benchmark page draws the Core, because it flies the valley as it is played.
- **For trying it.** With `?debug`, `window.__ashbound.mp.hurtCore(300)` wounds the Core by 300 points (a host or a single player can), `mp.clock()` now reads `coreHealth`, `coreMax` and `over` as well as the time, and `mp.warp(480)` takes you to nightfall. `?shot=core` and `?shot=reactor` photograph it from the rim and from a few steps away (add `&time=night` for the night).

## Phase 4c: Health and Energy (2026-10-23)

Austin lifted the pause on 2026-10-23 ("Continue.") and the phase went on where it had stopped. The numbers are all in `VITALS` (`src/systems/survival/balance.ts`). Everything here is the host's to decide and to keep; the guests hear only their own.

- **Two meters per player, kept by the host.** `PlayerVitals` (`src/systems/survival/vitals.ts`) is pure and the survival simulation steps one for every player in the room with the clock, so a Worker, a test and the browser play the same run. Health and Energy are each out of 100 and kept as fractions of a point; what is read out and sent is whole points, rounded up, so a bar with a sliver left shows 1 and 0 means truly empty. A player who leaves takes their meters with them: the seat is forgotten, and a returning tab starts again at full. That is a way to wash off a wound by reopening the tab; with friends it is tolerated, and saves (Phase 6) are where it gets closed.
- **Energy is what the spec's section 9 asks for.** It "drains slowly over time and faster during combat or heavy building", and low Energy "reduces movement and interaction speed". Alive costs 0.08 a second (a full bar lasts about twenty minutes of standing about), sprinting adds 0.5 (about three minutes of running), a wound adds 0.4 for the next six seconds, every block placed or broken that the host accepts costs 0.25 (a held click repeats about six times a second, so heavy building is about 1.5 a second), and a shot of the pulse blaster will cost 0.6 (4e). A refused command costs nothing, and the host charges the player who sent it. The host decides that a player sprints from the speed it last saw them at, measured against their own slowed walking pace, so being tired is not charged as sprinting.
- **Low Energy slows the legs and the hands, and nothing else.** Under a quarter of the bar, walking and sprinting slow in a straight line to 65 percent of normal with none left, and the repeat of a held click to half as fast; a single click always acts at once, and jumping, falling and swimming are not touched. There is no starvation: an empty bar hurts nothing, it only stops Health from mending. The slow-down is each player's own machine's business: `followVitals` (`src/app/vitals-effects.ts`) reads their meters once a frame and hands the scale to `Game.setSpeedScale` (a slower tuning for the same movement function, so the host's check of a guest still works: a slowed guest is only ever slower than it allows) and to `BuildController.setHandScale`. A guest's screen works the scale out from the whole points it was told, by the same rule as the simulation, so no speed travels and every screen slows by the same amount. The scale never goes below a quarter, however it is set.
- **Health mends by itself.** Eight seconds after the last wound it comes back at 1.5 a second, for as long as there is any Energy. Until the crawlers of 4d, only the `?debug` hooks can hurt a player, and 0 Health only empties the bar: going down and the shared respawn charges are 4g, and the Outpost Overrun screen is 4h, so that a half-built "down" does not have to be taken apart.
- **The Core's charge field.** Within 8 blocks of the Core's walls, measured from the middle of the player's body, a standing Core charges Energy at 2.5 a second, five times what sprinting costs and more than building costs. It is why the crew works near the base and why a trip away has a price. A fallen Core charges nobody.
- **Protocol version 4.** One new frame, `vt`: `{ hp: [points, max], en: [points, max], chg }`, a few dozen bytes. The host sends it to a newcomer with the snapshot, then within 250 milliseconds (`NET.vitalsIntervalMs`) of a change in the whole points or the field and not more often than that, and only to the guest whose meters they are: nobody else's Health is drawn anywhere yet, and the room list does not show it. The host's own player reads their meters straight from the simulation.
- **The corner is real.** The three mint segments are the player's Health, emptying from the right, and the blue bar with the white cap is their Energy; both turn their tone near the end (Health red and breathing below a quarter, Energy amber below a quarter, with the frame breathing at none). The round icons under them are two statuses, "Charging" in the Core's field and "Low energy" or "Out of energy", each with a short caption; a wound flashes the bar and reddens the edges of the screen for a moment (two animations take turns, because one that is already running cannot restart, and `prefers-reduced-motion` turns the breathing and the flashes into a steady colour). The flask, mushroom and shield icons and the "Rested" buff the look spike drew are gone: they promised things that do not exist, and beds and rest are Phase 5. The hotbar's tools stay placeholders until then.
- **The numbers here are guesses for playtesting,** like the day and the night: a night is four minutes, and a player who works the base stays charged, while one who runs a loop of the valley is dry in three. If it feels like a chore, `VITALS` is the one table to change.
- **For trying it.** With `?debug`: `mp.vitals()` reads the local player's meters as the screen has them, `mp.hurtPlayer(n, id?)` wounds a player (the id of a guest from `mp.session().localId` on its page), and `mp.setVitals({ health, energy }, id?)` sets them outright. All three act on the host or the single player only. Walking to the Core with the mouse taken shows Energy climb and "Charging" come up.

## Phase 4d: the crawler (2026-10-23)

The first machine and the ground it walks. A crawler comes in from a ring round the Core, goes round or chews through what the players have built, bites whoever is near and the Core, and is drawn on every screen. Nothing can hurt one yet (the pulse blaster is 4e) and nothing sends them by itself (the nights' waves are 4f), so for now the `?debug` hooks bring them. Every number is in `ENEMIES` (`src/systems/survival/balance.ts`) and is a guess for playtesting.

- **The map is a flow field.** `NavGrid` (`src/systems/survival/nav.ts`) cuts the ground within 64 blocks of the Core into 129 by 129 columns a block wide and finds, with one Dijkstra run outward from the Core, the cost of the cheapest walk from each. A column is open, blocked (deep water, the edge of the valley, a step taller than a crawler climbs), walled (a block of the players' stands in the way of its body) or the Core. An enemy steps to the neighbour with the lowest cost and looks up to eight steps down the route for a spot it can walk to in a straight line, so it cuts corners instead of tracing columns. The ground is the one the player stands on, the density grid read by `createSurfaceQuery` (`src/world/terrain/surface.ts`, rock tops included), so the lake, the cliffs and the slope of the crater mean the same to both. On the default seed the map takes about 50 ms to build, once, when the run starts, and about 5 ms to solve again.
- **Walls buy time and do not end a night.** A walled column stays on the map at the price of the chewing: the seconds a crawler takes over its lowest block (scrap 2, composite 3.5, alloy 6, energy-infused 9), turned into blocks of walking at its speed. A wall that costs less to chew than the way round is chewed, so a Core walled in on every side is still reached, and a gap costs a crawler only the walk to it. After a block changes the routes are worked out again at most four times a second. Chewing is the world's own change: the host breaks the block as nobody (`NO_PLAYER`, so no Energy is charged to anyone), every screen hears it as it hears any block break, and several crawlers at one block add their time; chewing that stops wears off at a fifth of a block a second. The times are guesses, to be tuned with the economy in Phase 5.
- **They walk on the ground and do not climb.** A player's block in the way of a crawler's body is a wall to chew, however low; anything above head height does not matter, so they walk under a bridge; and a player standing more than 2.5 blocks above them is not chased. Height is a way out of a chase, not a way to save the Core.
- **What a crawler does.** Each 20 Hz step it does the first of four things that applies. It bites, if a player (the nearest) or else the Core is within reach: 1.4 blocks of a player who is at most 1.5 above or below it, 0.95 from the side of the Core. It chases a player within 7 blocks that it can walk to in a straight line, and keeps on until the player is 1.3 times farther. It chews the wall on its route when chewing is cheaper than going round. Otherwise it marches. A bite winds up for 0.4 seconds and lands only if the same victim is still in reach, so a step back dodges it; the next may begin a second after the last began. A bite takes 6 points off a player and 5 off the Core, so six crawlers at the Core wear it down at 30 points a second and a full field of 100 would finish it in two seconds: the pulse blaster comes before the waves for a reason.
- **Where they come from.** From a ring 46 blocks out from the middle of the Core, give or take 4, at angles drawn from the run's seed, so the same seed plays the same spawns. A spot in the lake, on a cliff or walled in is skipped (24 tries a crawler), and at most 100 are alive at once, however they were sent. They stand still when the run does: alone with the mouse free the whole field is held, so Esc costs the Core nothing.
- **Protocol version 5.** One new frame on the fast lane, the one that may lose a packet: the list of machines, with 8 bytes of header and 12 per machine (an id, a kind, flags for attacking and hurt, x y z in sixteenths of a block, the way it faces, and its health as a fraction of 255). The cap of 128 makes the largest packet 1,544 bytes. The host sends the whole list ten times a second (`NET.enemyIntervalMs`), only when someone is there to hear it, and a guest drops any list that arrives after a newer one. The lane can lose the packet that says the field is empty, so that one is said again every second (`NET.enemyKeepAliveMs`) until something is back; without it a guest would keep a ghost on its screen for good.
- **Drawn a little in the past, the same way on every screen.** `EnemyMirror` (`src/net/enemy-mirror.ts`) keeps the last eight reports of each machine and draws it between two of them, 150 ms behind the host for a guest and 70 ms for the host itself, whose bites land at the truth. A hop of more than six blocks is a jump and not a glide, a missing report is bridged by carrying on at the last speed for at most a quarter second and then holding, and a machine is drawn from the second report that names it. The host's page and a guest's run the same mirror with different delays, so a crowd moves the same way on every screen.
- **The picture.** A low six-legged walker built from boxes in code, with no assets: a hull of dark blue steel with a plate and a thin amber spine on its back, the engine behind, a head in front with a red eye and a pair of pale jaws, and amber joints on six legs that step in two tripods. It is 720 triangles. Everything that moves is done in the vertex shader from a few numbers per machine: the stride follows how far it has walked, the legs ease in and out of a walk with its speed, a bite is a lunge of the body and a snap of the jaws, a hit lights it white-hot, the lights flicker as its health fails, and on arrival it rises out of the ground over 0.6 seconds. So the whole field is one instanced mesh, one draw call and one more into the sun's shadow, however many there are; at the cap of 100 that is 72 thousand triangles a pass. Sizes differ by up to 5 percent from the id, so a crowd is not a stamp. [Art direction](art-direction.md#phase-4d-the-crawler) has the pictures.
- **What the machines cost the host,** which is the measure that 4a said the Worker question waits for. On the idle sandbox CPU 100 crawlers marching and crowding take 0.35 ms a step on average (2.2 ms at the 99th percentile, 5 ms at the worst), 100 chewing through a wall with the routes worked out again take 0.2 ms on average (4 ms at the worst), and writing the list for the wire takes 0.02 ms. A hidden tab that wakes once a second has 20 steps to play, about 7 ms, and the cap of two seconds of catch-up is about 14. So the simulation is no reason for a Worker. What a hidden host tab still costs is the timer: its machines move in one-second jumps on the guests' screens, and a Worker that only keeps the metronome (4i) is what would fix that. It stays optional and waits for a play-test over a real network.
- **Left for later on purpose.** The pulse blaster and hurting a machine (4e; the wire already carries their health, and a hit already flashes), the waves (4f; the armored, flying and breacher machines stand in as crawlers until Phase 5 gives them their own bodies), being downed and the respawn charges (4g), and sound (Phase 6). A save that is loaded (Phase 6) must put its blocks into the world before the run is created, because the map is built from the world when the run starts and learns of changes only afterwards.
- **For trying it.** With `?debug` (and `?night=1` for the light they come in): `mp.spawnCrawlers(n)` sends n in from the ring and returns how many came, `mp.spawnCrawlerAt(x, z)` puts one on open ground and returns its id or null, `mp.clearEnemies()` takes them all away, and `mp.enemies()` lists what is on screen with health as a fraction of the bar. The first three act on the host or the single player only. With the mouse free the field is held, so spawn a crowd, set up the view and click Play.

## Phase 4e: the pulse blaster (2026-10-23)

The first weapon and the first way to hurt a machine: a bolt from the crosshair that the host judges and every screen draws. The numbers are in `BLASTER` (`src/systems/survival/balance.ts`) and `VITALS.energy.perShot`, and are guesses for playtesting: a hit does 10 points (three shots fell a crawler, which has 30), the blaster is ready again 0.35 seconds after a shot and banks two, a bolt flies 40 blocks, and a shot costs 0.6 Energy.

- **In hand on key 5.** The fifth slot of the hotbar holds the blaster, the first of the tools after the four blocks (slots 6 to 8 stay placeholders until Phase 5). With it selected, left click fires (hold to keep firing, at the blaster's pace) and right click does nothing; with a block selected, left click still breaks and right click still places. That changes one line of Phase 2d, "left click breaks the block under the cursor": it breaks in the block slots. The sight becomes a white ring with a tick at each quarter, so it reads as a weapon and not as the build cursor; the caption above the hotbar says "Pulse Blaster" and "Uses 0.6 Energy a shot"; and a hit on a machine flashes four ticks round the sight, white for a hit and larger and amber for a kill. Low Energy slows the blaster as it slows every hand (to half the pace with none left, as 4c has it) and never locks it.
- **A shot is a ray.** `traceShot` (`src/shared/shot.ts`, pure geometry that the host and every screen share) starts at the camera, runs straight with no spread and no drop, and ends at the first block, hill or Core in its way (the ray the aim uses, slabs and wedges by their faces), at the first machine's hitbox before that, or after 40 blocks. It hurts the one machine it ends on, if any. A crawler's hitbox is a box 1.2 blocks wide and one tall standing on its feet, a little more than its body (the legs count), so a shot that looks true is one. It also means a level shot from the eyes (1.62 up) passes over a crawler's back: you aim down at them. Other players are not targets; a bolt flies through them.
- **The host decides, and the shooter's eyes win.** A guest sends `{ t: 'fire', o, d, at }`: where its camera is, the unit vector it looks along, and the moment of the host's clock its screen was drawing machines at (150 ms behind, like every guest's screen). The host judges the bolt against the machines as they were then (`EnemyMirror.sample`, never further back than half a second, `NET.maxRewindMs`; for its own player 70 ms back), hurts the machine by id as it is now, and answers everyone with one `sh` frame: who fired, where the bolt ended, the `SHOT_FLAG` bits (struck a machine, felled it, stopped by the world) and the machine's id. A shot aimed at what the player saw is therefore the shot that lands, whatever the ping, at the price that a machine can be hit a moment after it stepped out of the way on the host. The host trusts a guest's camera only near where the guest stands (6 blocks, `NET.shotOriginSlack`; from farther the bolt starts at the guest's eyes), ignores a shot with no direction, and answers nothing for a shot the blaster is not ready for: each player has a bank of two shots that fills at one every 0.35 seconds, kept by `PlayerVitals.takeShot`, so a stream of clicks is no faster than the blaster.
- **Protocol version 6.** Two new frames of a few dozen bytes each, `fire` (guest to host) and `sh` (host to everyone, the shooter and the host's own page included). Both ride the reliable lane, because a shot is a command and not a position.
- **What each screen draws.** The player's own bolt is drawn the moment they click, from what their screen shows (the machines where they are drawn, the blocks and ground of their world), by the same `traceShot`, so the gun answers at once whatever the network is doing. The host's word on it adds only what the click could not know: that a machine fell (the burst) and the hit marker. Everyone else's bolts are drawn when the host's report arrives, from the shooter's avatar to the place the host says. A bolt wears its shooter's seat colour, and its strike is amber on a machine and the shooter's colour on the world. The head flies at 170 blocks a second and what the bolt does where it lands waits for it, so a shot at 40 blocks takes a quarter of a second to arrive; a burst the host reports waits for the bolt that felled the machine too.
- **What it costs to draw.** Two instanced meshes (the bolts and the sparks, flashes included) and so two draw calls however big the firefight, added into the HDR target above the bloom's threshold, so they glow and nothing is lit by them or casts a shadow. Everything an effect does while it plays (the streak flying, the flash swelling, a spark's flight and fall) is worked out in the vertex shader from the moment it was born, so the CPU writes a few numbers when something happens and nothing while it plays out. The buffers are rings of 64 bolts and 768 sparks. The meshes are in the scene from the start, drawing nothing, so the first shot of a session builds no programs. No assets: a bolt is a ribbon turned to the camera, a spark a small square with a round (or, for a flash, four-pointed) face. [Art direction](art-direction.md#phase-4e-the-blasters-light) has the pictures.
- **Left for later on purpose.** A gun in the player's hands or on their avatar (first person shows only the muzzle flash, and third person the flash at the avatar's chest; there is no recoil and the avatars do not raise an arm), sound (Phase 6), other weapons and upgrades (Phase 5), and damage numbers.
- **For trying it.** With `?debug`: `mp.fire()` fires from the player's eyes along their view as a click does, and works with the mouse free, which holds the run; `mp.fireAtMachine()` aims at the middle of the nearest machine as this screen has it and returns its id (or null, and fires nothing); `mp.shots()` lists the last two dozen shots drawn on this screen (who, what ended them, where, and whether it was the click or the host's report) with how much light is playing. The blaster has a browser spec of its own (`tests/e2e/blaster.spec.ts`), and a guest's shots at the host's crawler are in the multiplayer spec.

## Phase 4f: the waves (2026-10-23)

The nights get their machines. Each night's crawlers come in a few waves spread over the first three quarters of it, each from one side of the ring round the Core, and the crew is told ten seconds before each. The plan is plain arithmetic on the night's number (`planNight`, `src/systems/survival/waves.ts`), so a test, the balance simulator of Phase 5 and the host all read the same one. The numbers are in `WAVES` (`balance.ts`), and every one of them is a first guess for playtesting.

- **How many, and of what.** The spec's notes give the first four nights: 6, 8 and 10 drones, then 12 and an armored one. That is `totals: [6, 8, 10, 13]`, and each night after the fourth adds two. The special kinds take their places inside the night's total, last in its order: an armored one from night 4 and one more every three nights, a flyer from night 5 and one more every four, a breacher (the kind that goes for the base) from night 9 and one more every four. Until Phase 5 gives them bodies they all walk, are drawn and are shot as crawlers; the armored one has twice a crawler's Health (`WAVES.kinds`).
- **How tough.** From night 3 a machine has a tenth more Health (33 points, so the blaster takes four shots where it took three), and from night 5 each night's machines have 8 percent more than the last night's: 1.19 times a crawler's on night 5, 1.75 on night 10, 3.8 on night 20. It compounds, so the climb is gentle at first and steep late. Four shots to a kill from night 3 on is a feel to judge in the hands.
- **How they come.** About six to a wave and no more than six waves a night, shared out as evenly as it goes with the larger waves last, so that the armored one comes with the night's last wave. The first wave comes 14 seconds after nightfall; the last no later than 170 seconds, so that its walk of about 18 seconds (the ring is 46 blocks out and a crawler walks 2.6 blocks a second) is over well before dawn at 240; no two are more than 70 seconds apart. Night 1 is one wave of six at 14 seconds, night 2 two of four at 14 and 84, night 4 three (4, 4 and 5) at 14, 84 and 154, night 10 five of five. Inside a wave a machine comes out of the ground every 0.4 seconds, so a wave of six is out in two.
- **From one side.** A wave keeps to a stretch of the ring: a bearing drawn from the world's seed and the number of the night and of the wave (a stream of its own, `hash32(seed ^ night * 0x9e3779b1 ^ wave * 0x85ebca6b)`, whose first draw is the bearing and whose others place the machines), and 36 degrees either side of it (`WAVES.arc`). The first sixteen of a machine's 24 tries stay in that stretch; where it has no ground to stand on (the lake, a cliff) the last eight take any of the ring, so a wave that faces the water still comes, from wherever it can. Because the waves draw from streams of their own and not from the simulation's, the same seed plays the same waves however the crew played the nights before, which is what Retry in 4h needs.
- **Told ten seconds ahead.** A `wave` event, `{ k: 'wave', n, i, of, c }` (the night, which wave of the night it is, how many waves the night has and how many machines this one holds), rides the `se` frame like nightfall and dawn, so the host's screen and every guest's hear it once, and the HUD says it in the toast: "Wave 1 of 3: 4 machines in ten seconds". The codec refuses a wave whose place is not inside its night. Protocol version 7. The spec's rising stinger ten seconds before a wave is sound, so it is Phase 6, and so is a wave indicator that stays on the HUD: for now the toast, a line that lives 1.6 seconds, is all there is.
- **At dawn.** The machines still standing at first light fall back, so that every night begins on an empty field; the Core mends as before. The director keeps no clock of its own: the survival simulation calls it once a step with the step it has reached, and each check is of one step in particular, so a jump of the clock (`warp`, the debug hook) plays through the waves it passes, and a run that opens in the middle of a night skips the waves whose time has gone.
- **The crew's size does not change the nights yet.** A player alone and a crew of four meet the same machines. The notes scale waves by the night and nothing else, and what a crew of four needs is a question for playtesting.
- **Left for later on purpose.** A machine appears at the edge of the valley with no effect (a flash or dust where it comes up is polish for Phase 6), the armored, flying and base-targeting kinds drawn and fighting as themselves (Phase 5), the stinger and a standing wave indicator (Phase 6), and numbers tuned by the balance simulator (Phase 5).
- **For trying it.** The waves are on by default, so `?debug&night=4` has them, and with `?debug` `__ashbound.mp.warp(4)` and then `warp(10)` jump to the warning and the first machines. `?waves=0` keeps the nights empty, for pictures and for the browser specs that go through a night for the sake of something else (the blaster, the crawlers, the Core, the clock, the multiplayer rooms), and the unit-test arena keeps them quiet too (`waves: false`). The waves have a browser spec of their own (`tests/e2e/waves.spec.ts`), and unit tests alone and through a room of a host and two guests.

## Phase 4g: going down and coming back (2026-10-23)

A player at no Health is down, not out. They lie where they fell, the Core brings them back at the base a few seconds later for one of the crew's respawn charges, and a crew that is all down with the pool empty has lost the run. That is SPEC section 8 ("individual death is soft: respawn at the base... with a temporary energy and damage debuff"; a full wipe "ends the run") and decision 5 of 2026-10-20 (the charges are shared) built as written. The numbers are in `RESPAWN` (`src/systems/survival/balance.ts`) and are first guesses for playtesting.

- **The phase is split once more.** The roadmap's 4g held this and the Outpost Overrun results screen with Retry and New Run. The screen is a new piece of UI with a flow of its own (a results card, a new seed, the room starting over), so it is **4h**, and the host metronome Worker that 4d and 4f called 4h is **4i**: still optional, still waiting for a play-test over a real network. Until 4h a run that is lost ends as in 4b: the clock stops, the banner and the pause card say how, and the crew can close the room.
- **Down is 0 Health, and it holds the player still.** `PlayerVitals` has had a Health of 0 since 4c; now it means something. A downed player's meters stop (nothing mends and nothing drains), the machines leave them be (prey is chosen among players who are standing), the host refuses their block commands with "you are down" (`REFUSAL.down`) and ignores their shots, and on their own screen `followVitals` holds the legs (`Game.setFrozen`: no walking, sprinting or jumping, though gravity and the view go on working) and idles the hands (`BuildController.setDown`: the buttons are let go and do nothing). A refusal costs nothing, as ever.
- **The charges are the crew's, not the player's.** Every player brings two to the run (`startPerPlayer`), once: the host remembers who has brought theirs, so leaving and coming back adds nothing. Every dawn adds one for each player in the room (`dawnPerPlayer`), up to three for each (`capPerPlayer`), and a player who leaves leaves their charges in the pool. A lone player has two and the dawn tops them up to three; a crew of four has eight, gains four at dawn and holds twelve at most. Any fall spends from the same pool, so the crew decides who the charges are for by who falls.
- **Coming back.** A downed player lies for six seconds (`delaySeconds`), the first to fall first. Then, if the pool has a charge, the Core spends it and the player stands at their seat at the base (beside the world's spawn point, a step along for each seat, as when they joined; not where they fell), with full Health, half a bar of Energy (`energy: 0.5`), a full bank of blaster shots and **shaken** for 45 seconds: Energy drains one and a half times as fast and the blaster hits at six tenths of its damage (`shaken`). With no charge in the pool the player stays down and the countdown is replaced by "the dawn brings more": at first light the dawn adds its charges and the same step brings the queue back, first down, first up. A pool that is short for the whole queue brings back as many as it can, and the rest wait for the next dawn.
- **The host moves them.** Bringing a player back is the host's word, so it moves the guest the way it moves a guest who broke the movement check: a `fix` frame (a new epoch, so the packets the guest sent from where it fell are dropped), `guard.place` at the seat, the host's idea of the player's position and the room's idea of their avatar set at once (`HostSession.placeAtBase`), and the machines' idea too. The host's own player is moved by the same correction, now also for the host.
- **A lost run has two causes, and says which.** The Core falling is `overrun` with `why: 'core'` as before. When every player in the room is down and the pool is empty the run is over with `why: 'crew'`: the clock stops for the room, the Core stands as it was, and the HUD says "The whole crew is down" in the banner and a toast, and "Outpost overrun" over the downed screens. One player standing keeps the run going; a player who leaves does not leave the others waiting for them; a player who is down with a charge in the pool is not a wipe, because the Core is about to bring them back. A newcomer to a room whose run is over is told so and why.
- **What the screens show.** A pill under the Core's gauge counts the crew's charges as pips and a number, hidden by day while the pool is full, and on screen at night, whenever one is spent (it flashes red) and once the run is over; amber at one and red at none. A player who is down sees the edges of the screen darken, "You are down" and, under it, "The Core brings you back in" over a big count of seconds (6 down to 1), or "The crew is out of respawns. The dawn brings more" with no count, or, once the run is over, "Outpost overrun" and why. The sight and the caption over the hotbar are hidden and the hotbar dims. The toasts are "You are down" and "Ada is down", "The Core brought you back, shaken · 2 respawns left" and "Ada is back, shaken · 2 respawns left", and "The whole crew is down". After coming back the corner shows a "Shaken" status with the seconds left. [Art direction](art-direction.md#phase-4g-going-down-and-coming-back) has the pictures.
- **Protocol version 8.** `se` carries two new events, `down` (`{ n, p }`: the night or day, and the player) and `back` (`{ n, p, c }`: and the charges left), and `overrun` gains `why`; `sv` gains `rc: [charges, most]` and, once the run is over, `why`; `vt` gains `rt` (the whole seconds until the Core brings that guest back, 0 for a guest who is up or must wait for the dawn) and `sk` (the seconds it stays shaken). The codec refuses a `why` that is not `core` or `crew`, a charge count above the most the crew can hold, and a countdown outside a byte. A host and a guest on different versions refuse each other and say why.
- **Reopening the tab washes it off,** as in 4c: a guest who is down can leave and join again to stand up at full. The meters and the debuff are forgotten with the player; the charges it brought are remembered. With friends it is tolerated, and saves (Phase 6) are where it gets closed.
- **Left for later on purpose.** The Outpost Overrun screen, Retry and New Run (4h). A downed player's avatar is drawn on the other screens as it always is, standing where they fell: a lying-down pose or a marker over them is polish for Phase 6, and so is the sound. Reviving a teammate on the spot is not in the spec. Nothing protects a player the moment the Core has brought them back (no grace period, no shield), so a crowd at the seat can put them down again at once and spend another charge; whether a breath of safety is needed is for playtesting. The charges do not scale with the night, only with the crew, and whether six seconds and two charges feel right is for playtesting.

## The intro film (2026-10-27)

Austin asked for "an intro cinematic trailer that shows the beginning and how the player got there to the opening scene of the game when it starts." If he would rather not have it, it is off by one switch for a player (the pause card's checkbox) and by `?intro=off` for a page, and all of its code sits in one lazily loaded chunk that can be deleted whole.

- **What it shows.** About 43 seconds in six scenes, drawn by the game's own engine in the game's own valley, sky and light: the station's reactor falls out of the dusk sky and swings in over the hills; it bursts through the trees, ploughs a furrow and slides into the crater; the Core wakes in the wreck and sends a ring of cyan light across the ground; the machines of the wreck, the crawlers, rise out of the ground at the edge of the clearing in the night; the sun comes up across the lake over a title card; and the camera comes down to the shore, where the player stands, and becomes the player's view. Three captions in the letterbox say what happened. **The words are mine, not the spec's** (the spec gives no story): the station's name, "Vesper", is the one constant `STATION_NAME` in `src/shared/intro.ts`, and the three captions and the title card's line, "Scavenge by day. Hold the line by night.", are `CAPTIONS` and `INTRO_TITLE` in the same file.
- **It ends where the run begins.** The film's sky is the game's own day cycle (`sunElevationAt`, `sunBearingAt`, `skyStateAt`) run from dusk through night to the morning in a few seconds, handed to the stage with `Stage.setSkyOverride` and given back when it stops, and it ends on exactly the sky the run opens with. The last 1.6 seconds fly the camera into the player's own view at the spawn, so the hand-over is not a cut. The Core and the heaps of wreckage the crew will salvage are hidden while the film runs, and the Core stands where the hull lay when it stops.
- **It is a function of its clock.** The camera, the wreck's path (a fall from far out that swings in over the rim of the valley and ploughs the furrow `FURROW` the terrain already carries to the crater), the sky, the fire, the dust and the machines' rise are all worked out from the film's time alone, so a held moment is the same every time. `?introAt=T` holds the film at `T` seconds, which is how its pictures are taken and how the browser tests step through it. It touches nothing of the run: no command, no event, nothing on the wire, no change to the protocol, and no change to what a valley is.
- **When it plays.** At the first request for the mouse of a page, for a fresh run, when the player is on their own. The film starts inside the click that asks for the mouse, so the mouse arrives in hand: the game holds its input deaf while the film plays (`Input.hold`), the run's clock, which only runs while the mouse is captured, does not start, and when the film ends the same mouse is the player's, with no second click. It is not shown to a guest, or to a host with a room open, because the run they are in is already going; not to a page that is already playing; not on a page driven by a script or a test, or whose URL asks for something other than the plain start (`?debug`, `?bench`, `?shot`, `?time`, `?night`, `?stock`, `?research`, `?demo`, `?clock`, no HUD); (a player whose system asks for reduced motion is not on that list: they get a calmer film, below). `?intro=on` forces it, and `?intro=off` forbids it. **Phase 6b's resume must not offer it either**: a saved run is not a fresh one, and the controller offers the film once, at the page's first request for the mouse.
- **Skipping.** Any key or click skips it, after the first 0.8 seconds (the click that began it can arrive twice): the picture dips to black and the player is in the run. Esc, which lets go of the mouse, ends it at once and leaves the pause card up, and the next Play is the run itself. The pause card has a "Watch the intro" button to see it again and a checkbox, "Play the intro when a new game starts" (on by default, kept with the other settings). A prompt, "Any key to skip", shows from 1.6 seconds in and dims after six more.
- **Reduced motion gets a calmer film, not none (changed 2026-10-27).** The first version left the film out for a system that asks for reduced motion. Austin then found that Play went straight into the run and only the pause card's "Watch the intro" showed it ("when you hit play to start the game it automatically plays and you can hit ESC to skip it"), and a player cannot see that their system said so. It reproduces with the browser's reduced-motion setting, which a computer can report without its player having chosen it (Windows with "Animation effects" off, for one). Now the film plays for them too, with the camera held steady (`IntroOptions.calm`, no shake) and the flashes at a third of their strength (`.intro-flash` in `ui/intro-overlay.css`). The ways to turn it off are the pause card's checkbox and `?intro=off`. Esc is the browser's own key for letting go of the mouse, so on Esc the page is told only that the mouse has gone: the film ends and the pause card is up, and one click on Play starts the run; any other key skips straight into the run with the mouse kept.
- **How it is drawn.** Every effect is made in code at the start of the film and played back by the GPU from the film's clock, so the CPU writes nothing while it plays: particles are instanced quads whose motion is a closed form of their age (`engine/intro/particles.ts`), the wreck is a lathe of a profile with ribs, fins and spars and a shader of plating and heat, the lights are camera-facing glows, and the machines are the game's own crawler renderer. There are no models, textures or audio files, so `CREDITS.md` does not change ([art direction](art-direction.md#the-intro-film)). Its shaders are compiled as it is made, while it opens out of black.
- **Left for later on purpose.** An opening in space, with the station breaking up, which would want a model of the station; a film for the Overrun results or a warning for the first night; and captions in other languages. (Its music and sound effects came with [the film's sound](#the-films-sound-2026-10-27).)
- **Calls to look at.** The length, 43 seconds, for a film that is on by default for every new game (the skip is always there); the name, the captions and the title card's line; the film being on by default at all; and the carve-out itself.
- **For trying it.** `?intro=on` forces the film on a plain page, where the first Play starts it. With `?debug`, `window.__ashbound.intro.playback` is the film that plays (`time`, `seek(seconds)`, `skip()`), `?introAt=T` holds it at `T` seconds with no mouse needed, and `intro.replay()` is the pause card's button. The browser spec is `tests/e2e/intro.spec.ts` (the film from the click on Play, held and deaf, with the run's clock stopped, then a key skipping it to a run with the mouse in hand; Esc ending it at the pause card; the film offered once and watched again from the card; a plain page under automation and `?intro=off` showing none; and a moment from every scene drawn with no console error and the caption and the title where the script says). `tests/unit/intro-script.test.ts`, `intro-camera.test.ts`, `intro-wreck.test.ts`, `intro-tracks.test.ts`, `intro-director.test.ts` and `input-hold.test.ts` hold the script, the camera, the wreck's path (clear of the hills in three valleys), the tracks, the film's director and the input's hold.

## The film's sound (2026-10-27)

The third of Austin's three asks about the film: "make sure the trailer has music and sound effects". The film and the title screen's Play now start a soundtrack, **made entirely in code**. No recording, sample or audio file ships, so `CREDITS.md` does not change and decision 6 of the owner's list ("All audio is synthesized") holds to the letter. **I have not heard it**: the sandbox the game is built in has no sound device. What is checked is measured, not listened to (below, and in [art direction](art-direction.md#the-films-sound)), and the music itself, which is mine, is the first of the calls for Austin.

- **What it is.** About 47 seconds of sound under the film's 43.5, in D: minor while the night lasts, major with the morning. The fall has a low drone and a dark chord swelling under hull groans and shed plates, the air screaming past, and a pulse that quickens; the whole mix drops away for a fifth of a second, and the touchdown is a great boom and a clash of metal that is the loudest moment of the film. The wreck grinds to a stop and vents steam, and the Core's hum comes up with a low swell and a ping at each ring of its light. The crater is nearly silent: a held chord and seven bell notes. Each machine that rises in the eyes scene has a rumble of the ground, a servo and a blip for its eye, and then footsteps, over a heartbeat that quickens into timpani and a riser to the cut. At the dawn the key turns to D major (G, A, D) as the title card arrives, on a boom, timpani and a flourish of bells, with bells in eighth notes over D, A, B minor, G and D. For the waking there is the survivor's own heartbeat and breath, and the whole mix is heard through a lowpass that follows the picture's eyelids, a wall at 450 Hz while they are shut that clears as they open. The last chord rings into the first moments of play.
- **It keeps to the picture's script.** The moments the picture cares about (the touchdown, the stop, the Core's rings of light, each machine's rise, the title card, the eyelids) are exported from `src/shared/intro.ts` (`REVEAL_AT`, `CORE_PULSES`, `CRAWLER_RISES`, `BURN_UNTIL`, `coreGlowAt`, `lidsAt`), and the picture's layers and the score both read them, so retiming the film retimes its sound. Unit tests check each of those moments against the score's cues.
- **The film's clock is the only clock.** Each frame the director tells the sound where the film is; whatever starts within the next fifth of a second is handed to the audio thread for exactly the matching moment, and the rest is not made yet (a long sound is built when it first falls due). A frame that stalls holds the sound back with the picture, a slow machine is never more than that fifth of a second out of step, and a film that is moved on (a seek in a test) drops what it has passed instead of playing a burst of it. The same score is drawn to samples by `renderIntroSound` in step with a clock the way a film plays, which is how it is measured here.
- **It starts in the click.** A browser keeps a page silent until it has been clicked or a key pressed, so the audio context is not made at page load: `AudioHub.unlock()` makes it inside the click on Play (the title's, the pause card's "Watch the intro" or the Play after Esc, or the first request for the mouse on a page with no title). **The title screen itself is therefore silent**, which is the browser's rule and not a choice; the sound begins with the click and the fade to black. A page told to be silent (`?sound=off`) and a player who has turned the volume to nothing never make a context (turn it up and click again and the next film has its sound). A film held at a second for a picture (`?introAt`) has none, since no time passes for a sound to follow.
- **Skip, cut and end.** A key or click that skips the film fades the sound over 0.45 seconds with the picture's dip to black. Esc, which lets go of the mouse and ends the film at once, fades it over a quarter of a second. When the film runs out, what is left is handed over and left to ring: the last chord and the wind carry on for 3.6 seconds into play, with the last 0.4 of them faded so that taking the sound apart cannot click. A tab that is hidden puts the sound to sleep and wakes it with the tab; the hub only wakes a context it put to sleep itself.
- **The film does not wait on its sound.** A browser whose audio cannot make what the sound is set up with (the room, the noise, a node) plays the film in silence, and a sound that fails while the film plays is told once in the console and the film goes on without it. Whatever the sound throws, the picture, the skip, and the hand-over of the run and the mouse go ahead (`tellSound` in the director, `startSound` in the film's controller). A browser test makes the browser refuse the room and plays the film; without the guard the film does not start at all.
- **Volume.** A "Volume" slider on the pause card, 0 to 100 percent in steps of five, saved with the other settings, **100 percent by default** because the mix is set against full volume: it peaks a little under the top of the range, with the touchdown the loudest moment and the crater and the waking the quietest, so the slider only turns it down. The slider is squared into a gain (half way is a quarter, about 12 decibels down) and applied after a limiter, so turning it down never changes the mix; it follows the slider as it moves, over three hundredths of a second. The limiter is a compressor set to catch only the loudest moments (threshold -14 dB, knee 12, ratio 6, attack 3 ms, release 0.25 s), so that a dozen sounds landing together are squeezed together instead of clipped.
- **How it is made, and what it costs.** Oscillators and filtered noise from a seeded generator, shaped by envelopes; a reverb made from a generated impulse response (3.2 seconds, darker as it ages); a mixing board with a fader for each kind of sound; a limiter and the master volume. The page's first load carries only the small hub (`engine/audio/hub.ts`), the volume setting and the unlock, about 2.5 kB of JavaScript more than before (1.4 kB gzipped); the synthesizer and the score load with the film's chunk (21.4 kB, 7.8 kB gzipped), and the offline renderer (0.65 kB) only on a `?debug` page. Played in real time in the sandbox's browser, the browser as a whole uses about a fifth of a core more while the film's sound plays (0.24 of a core against 0.02 with an empty audio context); how it runs on a real machine is for Austin's first listen.
- **Measured, not heard.** `window.__ashbound.audio.render()` (with `?debug`) draws the soundtrack to samples, and the browser spec measures it: silent for the first quarter second and fading to nothing at the end, the loudest moment at the touchdown with the dip of a held breath before it, the skid the loudest scene and the crater, the eyes and the waking quieter, the closed eyes muffled and the open ones clear, two ears' worth of stereo, no sample that is not a number, and the same sound every time it is drawn (to a hundred-thousandth of the top of the range: the browser's wave shaper rounds the last digit of a sample differently from one drawing to the next).
- **Left for later on purpose.** The game's own sounds (building and breaking, steps, shots and hits, the machines, the rising stinger ten seconds before a wave, a sound for the loss), which are Phase 6c and can be built from the voices and beds written here; a score for the title screen and for the run itself; separate volumes for music and effects and a mute key; sound placed in the world (everything here is heard from inside the head, in stereo); and a recording of the film's sound as a file for the page (it would be a second copy of what the code makes).
- **Calls to look at.** The music: its key and mood (a dark D minor that turns to a bright D major), the bell motif of the crater, the heartbeat that turns into drums, the length of the quiet before the touchdown. The mix: how loud the touchdown is, how quiet the crater is, and whether 100 percent is the right default. That the film has sound on by default for every player (the volume slider and `?sound=off` are the ways out). That the title is silent. And how long the last chord rings into play.
- **For trying it.** Play the film with the volume up: the click on the title's Play, or `?intro=on` on a page with no title. A listening copy came with the pull request: the same soundtrack, drawn by the page's own code and saved as an MP3. With `?debug`: `window.__ashbound.audio.hub` is the hub (`context`, `volume`, `setVolume`), `audio.render({ volume, seconds })` the soundtrack as samples, and `intro.sound.stats` the film's own count of what it has started (`voices`, `beds`, `skipped`, `failed`, `state`). The browser spec is `tests/e2e/intro-sound.spec.ts` (the soundtrack measured; the click that unlocks it and a key that fades it; the mouse let go of and the film started from the pause card; a page told to be silent making no context; the volume at nothing and then turned up; a browser whose audio refuses the room, which plays the film in silence), with the volume slider added to `tests/e2e/play.spec.ts`. `tests/unit/audio.test.ts`, `cue-cursor.test.ts`, `audio-voices.test.ts`, `intro-sound.test.ts`, `intro-sound-play.test.ts` and `audio-hub.test.ts` hold the sound arithmetic, the cursor, every voice and bed against a stand-in audio context that refuses what a browser's would, the score, the sound's director and the hub, and `intro-script.test.ts`, `intro-director.test.ts`, `settings.test.ts` and `view-flags.test.ts` the shared moments, the director's sound port, the volume setting and `?sound=`.

This is SPEC section 8 ("scavenge debris for materials... debris nodes are finite and deplete. New nodes appear slowly or in slightly farther areas") and the first sentence of section 9's economy rules ("shared resource pool for all base construction and upgrades"), with the example yield of `notes/economy_and_waves.md` (8 to 12 Scrap a strip, 2 to 4 strips a heap). Nothing is charged yet: placing a block is still free until 5b ([Phase 5b](#phase-5b-costs-2026-10-24)), so the pool fills and does nothing. The numbers are in `DEBRIS` and `POOL` (`src/systems/economy/balance.ts`) and are first guesses for playtesting and, from 5j, for the balance simulator.

- **The phase is split into ten.** The roadmap's three Phase 5 PRs each held several parts that need their own review, so the serial draft PRs are: **5a** salvage (this); **5b** costs (placing a block spends from the pool); **5c** research (points for the nights survived, and the material tiers they open); **5d** the Research Terminal, the first module; **5e** upgrade in place with a visual change; **5f** the basic turret; **5g** power by radius (generator and node); **5h** storage, the bed and the light; **5i** the armored, flying and breaching machines; **5j** the balance simulator and its report. (The plan began with nine parts and 5c as "the Research Terminal, research points and the tier gates"; building 5c showed that to be two parts, so every letter after 5c moved by one: [Phase 5c](#phase-5c-research-2026-10-24). 5d was then built as two pull requests, 5d1 for the modules and the Terminal standing in the valley and 5d2 for the unlock that needs it, with the letters after it unchanged: [Phase 5d1](#phase-5d1-modules-and-the-research-terminal-2026-10-26).) The generated glTF pipeline the roadmap names for the modules is folded into 5d and 5f to 5h, one model at a time, drawn in code like every model so far.
- **One field of heaps for the run, drawn from the seed.** `debrisField(seed, ground, core)` (`src/systems/economy/field.ts`) is a pure function of the seed, the ground the machines walk and the Core's place, so a Retry plays the same heaps and every heap can be checked in a test. The whole run's field is drawn up front: 22 heaps (`initial`) lie out when the run begins, between 16 and 40 blocks from the Core's middle, and 12 batches of 4 (`perDawn`, `dawns`) are uncovered one at a dawn, in bands that start 1.5 blocks farther out each time and end by 62 blocks (`limit`), which is past the 46 blocks the machines come in from, so the later heaps are the ones that cost a risk to fetch by night. A heap needs dry, level ground (`flatness`, `dry`), no rock's top or ledge under it, 6 blocks (`gap`) between it and the next and 10 (`edge`) from the valley's rim; the generator tries 60 times (`attempts`) for each and gives up on one it cannot place, so a field can be a heap or two short on an odd seed (the seeds the tests and the pictures use give all 70). A run that opens at a later night (`?night=N`) has had the dawns before it. The heaps are the host's list, sent to the guests ([multiplayer](multiplayer.md#salvage-and-the-crews-pool)), and written to a hundredth of a block.
- **What a heap gives.** It can be stripped 2 to 4 times (`harvests`), and each strip gives 8 to 12 of the heap's material (`yield`): a number the seed, the heap and the strip's number decide, so a Retry gets the same Scrap from the same strip. Every heap gives Scrap in 5a (`grade` is on the wire so that research can make some of them give more), and a heap is drawn 0.85 to 1.25 times the size of its model (`size`). That is about 30 Scrap a heap and about 660 for the 22 that lie out, against a starting pool of 30 (`POOL.start`) and a ceiling of 400 of each material (`POOL.cap`, which Storage raises in 5h): enough that a crew that spends the first day gathering is limited by its hands and the walk, not by the field.
- **E is the interact key, and aim decides what it strips.** SPEC section 9 says an upgrade is "aiming and pressing the interact key", so E (`KeyE`, the new `interact` action) is that key from the start, and it strips the heap under the cursor now and will upgrade the block under it in 5e. A heap is a ball for aiming (`DEBRIS.radius` 1.5 blocks times its size, round a point `DEBRIS.lift` 0.75 up) and the cursor a ray (`aimAtDebris`, pure, `src/systems/economy/aim.ts`): the nearest ball the ray enters, within reach of the player's eyes, and in front of the first block or hill the view meets, so a heap behind a wall cannot be stripped. Reach is 4.5 blocks (`reach`), measured by the host as the screen measures it from the player's eyes to the middle of the heap, with the same slack block commands have. The hands wait 0.6 seconds between strips (`pace`, longer under low Energy, as the blaster is) and the host makes a player wait 0.5 (`cooldown`), the difference being the room for a network's jitter, so the host never refuses a strip the hands were slow enough to ask for.
- **Everything goes through the host, as a command.** A strip is a `harvest` command naming the cell the heap's middle lies in, checked by the host's rules and answered with an event to every screen (`hd`). The pool is the host's and a guest holds a mirror; the screens never add to it themselves. A pool with room for part of a strip takes what fits (the toast says "the pool is full"), and one with no room refuses it. The refusals have words ([multiplayer](multiplayer.md#salvage-and-the-crews-pool)) and a refusal costs nothing, as ever. Protocol version 10.
- **What the screens show.** Heaps are drawn as one instanced mesh (`src/engine/debris-render.ts`, built in code, 828 triangles a heap and one draw call in each pass that draws the world, which is three at Balanced and about 55 thousand triangles for the 22 that lie out, [measured](art-direction.md#phase-5a-salvage)): a buckled hull plate of patchwork Scrap, rust, teal paint and grey primer, a rib of the station's frame in an arc, bent beams, scorched rubble with embers that have not gone out, and one of three dressings on top (hazard-striped crates with a beacon, barrels and a pipe, or a broken console with its screen still lit) chosen by the heap's place in the list. The look is the Scrap blocks' own palette, so a player who has stripped a heap knows what they were given. A mast that leans out of every heap carries an amber lamp that breathes, so a heap can be found across the valley, where the grass stands higher than a block and a low pile is lost in it (the first pass of the model was a block tall and was, which is why the model is drawn at 1.5 times the size it is written at). The picture says how much is left: the model is made in layers, the top dressing goes first (below 80 percent of the strips left), then the rest of the pile (60, then 40), and the frame and the mast go with the last strip, each layer's pieces shrinking away and thrown a little outward. A strip flashes the whole heap white-hot and throws sparks of scrap (`ShotFx.strip`); a heap that is stripped bare sinks into the ground in about half a second; one uncovered at dawn rises out of it in about a second; the heap under the cursor glows at its edges. A heap that has none left is not drawn and cannot be aimed at.
- **What the HUD shows.** Slots 1 to 4 of the hotbar tell the pool's count of Scrap, Composite, Alloy and Energy-Infused (a stack of none is dimmed, and a count shows at 0 and 1, where a stack's would not). With the cursor on a heap in reach a small card under the crosshair says "E Strip debris" and how many strips are left ("3 strips left", "last strip"). A player's own strips raise a toast ("+9 Scrap", then " · the pool is full" or " · that heap is stripped bare" when either is so); nobody else is told, because they did not get it, they only see the pool grow. The Outpost Overrun card's row of tiles gains "Materials gathered", the first of the three counts section 8 asks for that Phase 5 was to add (the research follows in 5c and the upgrades in 5e). E is in the Build Mode key list and the controls on the pause card.
- **Left for later on purpose.** Costs (5b). A block built in a heap's cell: nothing reserves the cell yet, so a block placed there buries the heap, which is then out of aim (the ray stops at the block) until the block is taken away; the field keeps heaps clear of each other and of the rim, not of what the crew builds, and a reservation belongs with the placement rules in 5b (done: [Phase 5b](#phase-5b-costs-2026-10-24)). Heaps are not saved (Phase 6 saves the run). Heaps that give anything but Scrap (5c). A sound for a strip and a sound for a lamp (Phase 6). Collision for the heaps: a player walks through one, as through a machine, because a step-up rule for an odd shape is not worth it for a pile that is gone in a few strips. Whether a heap should give more per strip the farther it lies is a balance question for 5i.
- **For trying it.** With `?debug`: `mp.heaps()` lists every heap with its place, strips and the dawn that uncovers it, `mp.heapsShown()` the heaps drawn now with how far up out of the ground each is, the share of strips the picture shows, the flash of a strip and the glow of the cursor, `mp.pool()` the pool, `mp.harvest(i)` asks the host to strip heap `i` as E does with no aim and no pace (the host's rules, reach included, still apply), `mp.setPool(material, amount)` sets the pool, `build.salvage()` says which heap the cursor is on, and `mp.clock()` carries `gathered`. The browser specs are `tests/e2e/salvage.spec.ts` (one player: aiming, the prompt, a strip, the pool and the toast, a pool at its ceiling, the last strip, the refusals, and a dawn uncovering heaps) and a room of a host, a guest and a latecomer in `tests/e2e/multiplayer.spec.ts`.

## Phase 5b: costs (2026-10-24)

The second third of the economy: the crew's pool pays for the crew's building, and a heap of wreckage keeps its ground. SPEC section 9 says there is a "shared resource pool for all base construction and upgrades", section 10 asks for the "material cost" on the ghost and the HUD, and section 11 puts "resource costs" among the things the host decides. Until now placing a block was free; from now on it takes the piece's price from the pool, breaking it puts the price back, and a block cannot go where it would bury a heap (5a left that open on purpose). The numbers are in `MATERIALS` (`src/shared/blocks.ts`) and `DEBRIS.keepClear` (`src/systems/economy/balance.ts`) and are first guesses for playtesting and, from 5j, for the balance simulator.

- **Every piece costs one of its material, whatever its shape.** The table already said `cost: 1` for each of the four materials (the caption has said "Cost 1 Scrap" since 2d) and the pool now charges it: a block, a slab and a wedge of Scrap each take 1 Scrap. A slab and a wedge are each half a block's material, so a cheaper price for them is a fair thing to try; the rules ask only the table, so it is a number to change, not code. A run opens with 30 Scrap (5a) and none of the dearer materials, so the crew can place 30 blocks before it has to gather, a heap's strip gives 8 to 12, and Composite, Alloy and Energy-Infused cannot be placed at all until 5c gives the crew a way to get them: their hotbar slots show a dimmed 0 and the ghost says "Not enough Reinforced Composite".
- **Breaking gives the whole price back.** A block the crew breaks goes back into the pool at the price of the material that stood in the cell, as far as the ceiling (400 of each) allows; a pool that is full takes what fits and loses the rest. A full refund means nobody is punished for a misplaced block, and building and breaking cost only the Energy they always did (0.25 an edit, 4c). It is the cell's current material that is refunded, which is the right sum until 5e lets a block be upgraded in place: what an upgraded block gives back is for 5e to say. A block the machines chew through is not broken by anybody and is not refunded.
- **The host decides, and the screen asks the same questions first.** The host asks, after reach, the world's own rules and the bodies in the way: is a heap in the way, and does the pool hold the price. If a block passes it takes the price in the same step that applies the block, so nothing is charged for a refused command and nothing stands in the world unpaid for. The two checks are small pure functions in `src/systems/economy/build-rules.ts` that the host's `Economy` and the player's screen both call, and the screen's ask goes through a new hook on the aim (`AimInput.rules`, asked last, so the reach and the world's rules still come first): a ghost that is not red is a block the host will accept, and a click on a red one sends nothing and says why in the caption for a moment. A guest asks of its mirror of the pool and the heaps, so for the 200 milliseconds a frame takes to arrive the screen can show a ghost green that the host then turns down; the host's word is shown the same way as any refusal's. Where there is no pool to read (a host with no run, or a guest before the host has spoken) nothing is charged, as the host charges nothing there. Nothing new travels: the commands and frames are the ones 5a had, the refusals have words (`not enough Scrap`, `debris is in the way`), and the protocol stays at version 10 ([multiplayer](multiplayer.md#what-building-costs-phase-5b)).
- **A heap keeps a round column of ground clear.** While a heap lies there with strips left, no block can be placed in a column round its middle: `DEBRIS.keepClear.radius` 1.7 blocks across from the axis and `height` 2.4 tall for a heap of size 1, and `below` 0.5 under the ground its middle is on, all multiplied by the heap's size, so a big pile keeps more clear than a small one (the column is about as wide and tall as the pile is drawn; the thin mast goes higher). A cell is in the column when any part of it is, so the rule does not depend on which way a block's corner points. A heap with no strips left is gone and keeps nothing; one not yet uncovered keeps nothing either, so the crew can build on ground a heap will come up through, which is the next point's reason. Blocks around the column are allowed, so a crew can wall a heap in; the heap is then as good as lost, and that is the crew's own choice.
- **A heap that is due waits for the ground to be free.** The 48 heaps uncovered at dawns come up out of the ground at their dawn. If the crew has built in a due heap's column by then, the heap does not come up through the blocks, and the blocks are not taken down for it: it waits and the host looks again at each later dawn, so a heap comes up the dawn after the ground is clear. The heaps of the first batch lie in the open from the start, so nothing can already be built on them.
- **What the player sees.** Nothing new is drawn: the hotbar's counts drop as the crew builds and rise as it breaks, the ghost goes red, the crosshair's ring goes red and the caption says "Not enough Scrap" or "Debris is in the way" in the red the other refusals use ([art direction](art-direction.md#phase-5b-costs)). The caption names the material that is short because the four slots can be short in different ways.
- **`?stock=<n>` opens the pool with n of every material.** A run that opens with a little Scrap makes it slow to test building a wall, or placing Alloy, so the page has a switch for it, like `?night=` and `?waves=`: `?stock=200` opens the pool with 200 of each (never more than the pool holds, 400, and `?stock=0` opens it empty). It is the host's page that decides, a guest's has no say, and Retry and New Run open the next run with the same stock. Tests that build a lot use it.
- **Left for later on purpose.** The price of each material and shape, which is for play and for 5j; the refund of an upgraded block (5e); the dearer materials (done in 5c); a refund for a block the machines chew (a loss is the point of a wall); a heap's collision and sound (a player still walks through one); a cost on the blaster's shots (the blaster uses Energy, not materials); and saving the pool with the run (Phase 6).
- **For trying it.** With `?debug`: `mp.pool()` reads the pool, `mp.setPool(material, amount)` sets it, `build.aim()` carries `placeRefusal`, `mp.rejections()` the host's words. The browser specs are `tests/e2e/costs.spec.ts` (one player: the pool dropping and rising with every shape, the red ghost for a short pool and for each material, a heap keeping its ground, and a due heap waiting at the dawn) and a room of a host and a guest in `tests/e2e/multiplayer.spec.ts` (one pool for both, the host's words reaching the guest).

## Phase 5c: research (2026-10-24)

The third part of the economy: the crew earns research points by surviving nights, the points open the better materials one row at a time, and the better materials come only from a few heaps. This is SPEC section 9 ("Research Points are earned primarily by surviving nights (small bonus possible for low base damage or efficiency)", "unlocks the next material tier and new modules at clear thresholds") and `notes/economy_and_waves.md` (8 to 12 points a night survived, Tier 2 at about 20 to 30 points, Tier 3 at about 50 to 70, higher tiers "less frequently or require research unlocks"). Until now the three dearer slots of the hotbar refused every block because nothing could fill them (5b); from here they have a way to open and a way to fill. The numbers are in `RESEARCH` and `DEBRIS.grades` (`src/systems/economy/balance.ts`) and are first guesses for playtesting and, from 5j, for the balance simulator.

- **The part is split in two: this is 5c, and the Research Terminal is 5d.** The plan had one part for "the Research Terminal, research points and the thresholds that gate the tiers and modules". The points, the ladder, the locks and the better heaps are one reviewable change that needs no new model, command or shape of cell, and the Terminal is the first module: a thing the crew builds and stands next to, which needs the cell shapes that have no mesher geometry, a model drawn in code, a hotbar slot and a `use` command. So 5c gives the crew research and a lock, with the unlock automatic at the dawn the points are reached, and 5d makes an unlock need a standing Terminal. Every later letter moves by one: 5e upgrade in place, 5f the basic turret, 5g power by radius, 5h storage, bed and light, 5i the armored, flying and breaching machines, 5j the balance simulator. The split is the same kind of call as 4g, 4h and 4i; the roadmap and the earlier sections of this file use the new letters.
- **Points are a running total, never spent.** The spec says the Terminal "unlocks ... at clear thresholds", which is a ladder to climb; `notes/economy_and_waves.md` says the points are "spent at Research Terminal", which would make the crew choose between two unlocks. Until 5e and 5g there is only one thing to unlock at a time, so a spend would be a choice with one option, and a total is what a results card ("research earned", SPEC section 8's list) wants to show. If play wants a price, `Research` is a small class and a spend is a method away. This is the one place the notes and the spec read differently, and it is flagged in the pull request.
- **A night survived earns 10 points, and 2 more for a night the Core was not touched.** The number is the middle of the notes' 8 to 12 and the bonus is the "small bonus for low base damage". They are credited once, at the dawn that ends the night (the run that ends in the night earns nothing for it), and before that dawn's heaps are chosen, so a tier the points have just opened can have its heap come up at once. "Not touched" means the Outpost Core took no damage between nightfall and dawn; a player going down, or a block being chewed, does not count. A crew that holds every night cleanly has 12, 24, 36, 48, 60 ... after each dawn, and one that is hurt every night 10, 20, 30 ...
- **The ladder has three rows today: 20, 55 and 100.** 20 points opens Tier 2 (Reinforced Composite), 55 opens Tier 3 (Advanced Alloy) and 100 opens Tier 4 (Energy-Infused). The first two sit inside the notes' ranges (20 to 30 and 50 to 70); the third is a guess in the same stride. A clean crew opens Composite at the dawn of its second night, Alloy at its fifth and Energy-Infused at its ninth, a hurt crew at the second, sixth and tenth, which is a tier every three or four nights; the rows the modules add (10 points for the Power group in 5g, 40 for the module upgrades in 5e) fill in the 2 to 3 nights the spec asks for between unlocks. The table lists what each row opens, so a new row is a line of data.
- **A material that is not researched cannot be placed.** The host asks after the heap's ground and before the pool: a locked piece is refused with `<Material> needs research` (`Reinforced Composite needs research`), so a crew that has neither the points nor the material is told about the lock first, which is the thing it can do something about. The screen asks the same function ([multiplayer](multiplayer.md#research-phase-5c)), so a ghost that is not red is a block the host will accept, and where there is no pool to read (before the host has spoken, or a host with no run) nothing is locked, as nothing is charged there. Tier 4 stays shut until something can fill it: Energy-Infused has no source, because the special machines that drop it come in 5i.
- **Better heaps are few, and they give less.** Every dawn's batch of four has one fixed place for a better grade (`DEBRIS.grades`): from the second dawn the fourth heap of a batch is Reinforced Composite (2 or 3 strips of 5 to 7), from the fifth dawn the third is Advanced Alloy (2 strips of 4 to 6). The grade only changes how many strips a heap has and what a strip gives, drawn from the same numbers as before, so where every heap lies, how big it is and which way it faces are what 5a made them. That is 11 Composite heaps and 8 Alloy ones among the 48 that dawns uncover, about 165 Composite and 80 Alloy if every one were stripped, against 660 Scrap in the first 22 heaps: a block costs one of its material, and the upgrades that spend them (5e on) are few. A heap of a grade the crew has not researched is not uncovered at its dawn; it waits, like one built over, and comes up at a later dawn once the points are there. A crew that is hurt every night has 50 points at its fifth dawn, so the Alloy heap of that dawn waits one more night.
- **What the player sees.** A locked slot shows its block dim behind a padlock with no count. A research pill joins the column under the clock (the Core's gauge at night, the respawn charges when they change): the points, a lavender bar to the next row and `12/20`, which becomes a plain `100` and a full bar at the top; it shows in Build Mode and for ten seconds after the points change. The line over the hotbar for a locked piece says `Needs research · 12 of 20`. At a dawn that pays, two toasts, one after the other: `Research +12` and `Reinforced Composite unlocked`; the toast queue is new for this (a dawn has several things to say, and the second no longer overwrites the first). The results card gets a sixth tile, "Research earned". A heap is dressed in its grade: Composite is layered grey-green panels with a cyan strip down each and a cyan lamp on the mast, Alloy is brushed dark steel inlaid with amber and a white lamp, so a better heap can be told from across the valley ([art direction](art-direction.md#phase-5c-research)). Everything on the screen is worked out from the pool's frame, so a guest sees what the host sees.
- **`?research=<n>` opens the run with n points** (0 to 9999), as `?stock=` opens it with a pool: `?research=55` has Composite and Alloy researched, `?research=100` everything. It is the host's page that decides, a guest's has no say, and Retry and New Run keep it. A run that starts at a later night with `?night=N` has had the dawns before it: 10 points for each (no bonus, nobody was there to be hurt), and the heaps they uncovered, the better ones included once the points allow; `?research=` replaces those points.
- **Nothing is saved yet and nothing is spent.** The points go with the run (Phase 6 saves it). There is no command for research, so there is nothing for a guest to forge; the only new thing on the wire is one number in the pool's frame, and the protocol moves to version 11.
- **Left for later on purpose.** The Terminal, and an unlock that needs one (5d: the Terminal stands in 5d1, [Phase 5d1](#phase-5d1-modules-and-the-research-terminal-2026-10-26), and the unlock that needs it follows in 5d2); the 10-point Power row and the 40-point module rows (5g, 5e); Energy-Infused heaps (5i); what each tier costs and gives, which is for play and for 5j; a sound for an unlock and a longer fanfare than a toast (Phase 6); and a research screen with the whole ladder in it, which belongs with the Terminal's own panel.
- **For trying it.** With `?debug`: `mp.pool()` reads `research` and `tier` as well as the stock, `mp.setResearch(n)` sets the points (host only), `mp.heaps()` and `mp.heapsShown()` carry each heap's `grade`, and `?research=55&night=6` is a run with every better heap lying out and nothing else to do. The browser specs are `tests/e2e/research.spec.ts` (padlocks, caption, pill and toasts as the points rise; the points a night earns and the card; a later start and a heap waiting for its tier; `?research=`) and a room of a host and a guest in `tests/e2e/multiplayer.spec.ts` (the host's points on the guest's screen, and the host's words for a locked material).

## Phase 5d1: modules and the Research Terminal (2026-10-26)

The first half of the Research Terminal, and the first thing the crew builds that is not a block: a functional module. SPEC section 4 says functional modules "use models that also snap" to the grid and section 9 lists the MVP set (the turret, the generator and node, storage, the research terminal, the bed and the light). This part builds what they all stand on and the first of them, so a player can choose the Terminal from the hotbar, turn it, place it, break it for a refund and see it, and so can everyone else in the room. It does nothing yet: an unlock that needs a standing Terminal is 5d2. The numbers are in `MODULES`, `MODULE_FOOT` and `MODULE_SINK` (`src/shared/blocks.ts`) and are first guesses for playtesting and, from 5j, for the balance simulator.

- **The part is split in two: this is 5d1, and the unlock that needs a Terminal is 5d2.** 5d as planned was "the Research Terminal, which an unlock then needs". The framework (a new kind of cell, its model, its place on the ground, its slot on the hotbar and its price) and the first model are one reviewable change with no rule change and no protocol change. The rule that an unlock needs a standing Terminal, the panel that shows the ladder, the new words and the frame that carries them are another, and are easier to judge with a Terminal to look at. So 5d1 leaves research as 5c made it (an unlock is still automatic at the dawn its points are reached) and 5d2 changes it. The letters after 5d do not move: 5e is still the upgrade in place. It is the same kind of call as 4g, 4h, 4i and 5c.
- **A module is a cell.** A cell's state already packs a shape in the low byte and a material in the high byte, so a module is a new group of shape ids, `SHAPE.module` (32) plus `(module - 1) * 4 + facing`, where the facing is the way the front looks (toward +x, +z, -x or -z), and its material byte is its level (1 to the number of levels the table gives it; the Terminal has one, and 5e's upgrades will be the level going up). That is all the world needs: a module is placed, broken, sent in a late-join snapshot and saved by the commands and the edit list that carry a block, so nothing new travels and the protocol stays at version 11 ([multiplayer](multiplayer.md#modules-phase-5d1)). The table says what a module is called, how many levels it has, what it costs and the box of its cell it fills (`body`, its front toward +x). Movement stands against that box and the cursor aims at it, and the mesher draws no faces for the cell: the model is drawn from code (below). A cell of a state the table does not know (a level the module lacks) is refused as any unknown piece is.
- **The Terminal costs 40 Scrap, and is not locked.** The defaults priced it at about 40 Scrap because the notes' Composite is what it unlocks. It comes out of the shared pool as a block does, and breaking it gives the 40 back. A run opens with 30 Scrap (5a), so the first Terminal needs a strip or two of a heap first, or fewer blocks built before it: a crew that has spent its Scrap on a wall learns it from the caption ("Not enough Scrap") rather than from a lock. A module's material byte is its level, not a material, so the tier gate does not apply to one; the research that opens the other modules comes with them (the 10 points of the Power group in 5g, the 40 of the module upgrades in 5e), and the Terminal is the one thing the crew can always build, since it is what research will go through.
- **Where a module may stand.** The terrain is smooth and cuts a cell at any height, and a module is a model, not a block that fills a cell, so a rule was needed for the ground. The first picture of a Terminal in the cell the ground cut at 82 percent showed why: it was a thin rod over the grass. The rule has three parts and the host and the cursor share the numbers. The model carries a foundation that reaches `MODULE_SKIRT` (0.98) below the floor of its cell and never goes through a block; the host accepts a module whose cell floats up to `MODULE_FOOT` (0.9) over the ground at its middle, or that stands on a block or the upper half of a slab (a lower slab does not carry one); and where the ground reaches higher than `MODULE_SINK` (0.25) of the cell the cursor puts the module in the cell above, so a Terminal is not mostly buried, as a slab picks the half the ground leaves open. A cell the ground already cuts is accepted, as it always was, unless it lies wholly under the ground (`buried`). The refusal for anything else is the world's usual `unsupported`, and the caption says "Needs ground or a block to rest on". The next modules inherit all of it: a model has to fit inside its cell above the floor, reach its foundation below, and keep its body box in the table.
- **The Terminal is a field console on a steel pad.** A cabinet stands at the back with a lit screen on its front, a sloped desk falls away from it toward the player with a second screen and a row of amber keys, and two masts carry a blinking amber beacon; the pad has a cyan light strip and the cabinet four cyan light bars, so the Core's palette says "on". It is 1,544 triangles in six meshes (steel, lighter trim, amber, cyan lights, screen glass, beacon), merged for each look and shared by every Terminal, drawn in code like the Core and the machines, so `CREDITS.md` does not change ([art direction](art-direction.md#phase-5d1-the-research-terminal)). The screens shimmer, the lights breathe and the beacon blinks from the stage's animation clock. There is no power until 5g, so a module is always on until then; the cyan of "on" and the amber of "working" are the look the later modules take.
- **The ghost is the model.** The translucent piece a player aims with is the Terminal itself, cyan when the host would accept it and the warm red of any refusal when not, and the outline of a Terminal under the cursor is its body box. The module slot is slot 6 of the hotbar (key 6, where a made-up pick stood; the torch and the charge after it are still made up), and with it in hand Q chooses the next module (there is one), R turns it through its four facings (it starts facing the player, so its screens are what they see) and the caption says "Research Terminal" and "Cost 40 Scrap". The slot's icon is a drawing of the console in the HUD's own style: a steel cabinet, a lit cyan screen, a bronze strip and a mast with a beacon.
- **The machines chew a module as they chew Scrap.** A crawler's chewing time keys on the cell's material byte, which for a module is its level, so a Terminal is eaten in the 2 seconds a Scrap block takes, and nobody is refunded for it, as for any block the machines break. That is a placeholder, flagged below, and a table entry (`gnawSeconds`) away from changing.
- **Everything goes through the host, as a command.** Placing and breaking a module are the `place` and `break` commands, checked in the host's order (down, rate, ready, reach, the world's rules, a player in the way, a heap's ground, the pool), so a Terminal a guest asks for in the air, short of Scrap, or at a level the table does not have is refused with the words the screen would have shown, and costs nothing. The screen asks the same functions before it shows the ghost green, as it does for a block.
- **Left for later on purpose.** The unlock that needs a Terminal, its panel and its words (5d2); a Terminal that is off, and any power (5g); module levels and what an upgraded module refunds (5e); instancing the models (a Terminal is six draw calls and the turret of 5f will be built many times: that is when to instance them); a sound for placing it and for the screens (Phase 6); collision that follows the model (movement stands against the body box, so the desk's slope is only drawn and the masts and the beacon rise above it); and a tier lock of the Terminal's own if play wants one.
- **For trying it.** With `?debug`: `mp.place(x, y, z, state)` and `mp.placeNear(dx, dz, state, up)` send the host a command as a player at that spot would (`up` is how many cells above the cell the ground is in: pass 1 where the ground reaches higher than `MODULE_SINK`, as the cursor would), `game.world.view.modules.count` and `.at(x, y, z)` read what is drawn, `mp.block(x, y, z)` the state in the world, `mp.rejections()` the host's words, and `?stock=200` opens the pool with enough to build a few. The browser specs are `tests/e2e/modules.spec.ts` (one player: the slot, the caption and a ghost red for a pool one short, the Terminal facing the player, a quarter turn, a click that takes the price and stands it on the ground, the cursor on its body and a break that gives the price back; and the host's own rules for a pool one short, a cell in the air and a level the table does not have) and a room of a host, a guest and a latecomer in `tests/e2e/multiplayer.spec.ts` (the guest's Terminal and the host's break on every screen, the one pool, and the late join shown it).

## Phase 5d2: an unlock needs a Terminal (2026-10-26)

The second half of the Research Terminal. 5c made the points open the better materials by themselves at the dawn they were reached, 5d1 let the crew build a Terminal that did nothing, and this part joins the two: what the crew has earned opens nothing until a standing Terminal has read it. SPEC section 9 lists the Research Terminal as the thing that "unlocks the next material tier and new modules at clear thresholds", and until now nothing the crew built mattered to research. The numbers are the ones 5c set (`RESEARCH`, `src/systems/economy/balance.ts`); this part adds none.

- **Earned and read are two numbers.** `Research` keeps the points the crew has earned (as before: 10 a night, 2 more for an untouched Core, never spent) and the points a Terminal has read (`claimed`). The tier the crew can build in, and the heaps that rise, follow the read points. A crew that never builds a Terminal holds 12, 24, 36 ... points and stays at Scrap.
- **A Terminal reads when it is placed and at every dawn it stands through.** Placing one reads every point earned so far, so building it is what opens Composite for a crew with 24 points waiting. At each dawn the host credits the night and, if a Terminal stands in its world, reads the night's points in the same step, before the dawn picks which heaps come up, so a tier opened at a dawn can have its heap rise at once, as in 5c. "Stands" is a scan of the world's edit list for a cell holding a Terminal (`moduleStands`): there is no list of Terminals to keep in step, so one a guest built, one in a late-join snapshot and one in a run started over all count the same way, and a scan a dawn and a scan a placement is cheap.
- **What was read stays open.** Breaking the Terminal refunds its price (5d1) and takes no tier away. An unlock is knowledge, and a rule that shut it again would punish a crew for the machines chewing a Terminal (they do, 5d1) or for moving one. What losing the Terminal costs is that the points earned after it wait for the next.
- **It is the standing of a Terminal that counts; nobody uses it.** The notes say points are "spent at Research Terminal". 5c made them a running total that is never spent, so there is nothing to choose at a Terminal, and a button for the only result it could have would be a chore. This is flagged in the pull request as the one place to say otherwise: a "use" command (E on the Terminal) could ask for a reading, and would cost the protocol a command.
- **Where the rule is applied.** The host. `Economy.placed` (called by `HostSession.runCommand` after the pool has paid) for a placement, and `SurvivalSim.step` for a dawn, whose only question of the world is `SurvivalOptions.standing`. A run that opens at a later night with `?night=N` has read the nights behind it, and `?research=` and `mp.setResearch` read what they set. The host's words are unchanged: a piece of a material that is not open is refused with `<Material> needs research`.
- **The words a player sees.** When the points earned reach a row of the ladder that the points read do not, the pill turns amber and says `Needs Terminal` in place of `Research`, the line over the hotbar for a piece of that material says `Needs a Research Terminal` (for a piece the points do not reach it still says `Needs research · 12 of 20`), and a toast says `Needs a Research Terminal` after `Research +12`. A crew with 12 points and no Terminal has nothing to be told, so the pill stays lavender. When a Terminal reads them, the toast is `Reinforced Composite unlocked`, as in 5c. The results card still counts every point earned.
- **The ladder.** E with the cursor on a Terminal in reach, and no heap of debris in front of it, opens a read-only panel beside the crosshair: the points earned, a bar to the next row (`20 to Alloy`), a row for each material with its block icon, what opens it (`From the start`, `20 points`) and where the crew stands (`Open`, `Not read yet` in amber, `35 to go`), and a line on how a night earns points. It is not a window: the mouse stays where it is and the run goes on, because in a room nothing pauses. It shuts with E, with Esc, when the player goes down, when they are a block past the reach that opened it and when the Terminal goes. Its rows come from the table the lock does (`RESEARCH.steps` and the materials), so a new row in the table is a new row on the panel. While a Terminal is aimed, the card under the crosshair says `E  Research ladder` and the points.
- **On the wire** one number: `ec` is `{ pl, cap, rp, cl }`, the tier worked out from `cl` on every screen, protocol version 12 ([multiplayer](multiplayer.md#research-read-by-a-terminal-phase-5d2)). No new command.
- **Left for later on purpose.** The 10-point Power row and the 40-point upgrade rows (5g, 5e: the panel lists whatever the table lists); research that costs something, or a choice between two things (see above); a Terminal per player, or a range a Terminal reads over; and a sound for a reading and for the panel (Phase 6).
- **For trying it.** With `?debug`: `mp.setResearch(n, false)` sets the points earned and leaves them unread (the default reads them), `mp.pool()` carries `claimed` as well as `research` and `tier`, and `?stock=200` pays for a Terminal. The browser specs are `tests/e2e/terminal.spec.ts` (one player: unread points turn the pill amber and leave the padlock, a built Terminal reads them, E opens and shuts the ladder, more points move it, breaking the Terminal keeps what it read), `tests/e2e/research.spec.ts` (a night's points wait until a Terminal is built, and a later start with a Terminal standing) and a room of a host and a guest in `tests/e2e/multiplayer.spec.ts` (the guest's pill, padlock and toasts follow the host's reading).

## Phase 5e: upgrade in place (2026-10-26)

The third piece of the economy's second half, and the first use of the better materials the research opens. SPEC section 5 gives the interact key (E) "while aiming at a structure: upgrade or use it if materials/requirements are met", section 9 says "most structures can be upgraded in place by aiming and pressing the interact key while holding the required materials" with a changed look, and section 8 puts "upgrades completed" on the results screen. Until now a crew that had earned Composite could only place new blocks of it; from now on it can raise the wall it has. The prices are the ones 5b set (`MATERIALS`, `src/shared/blocks.ts`) and this part adds no number of its own.

- **One press, one material up.** E on a player-made block of Scrap, Composite or Alloy swaps it for the same shape in the next material: the same facing, the same half of the cell, a slab stays a slab and a wedge keeps rising the way it did. Nothing is better than Energy-Infused, so a block of it has nothing to offer. A press does not climb as far as the crew can afford: it is a decision per block per tier, and the host's answer to the next press is the next tier's.
- **Not a module.** A module's material byte is its level, and the only module so far has one (the Terminal), so E on a Terminal still opens its ladder. Levels come with the first module that has more than one, the turret (5f), through the same command.
- **The price is the better piece's, from the one pool.** An upgrade costs what a new block of the better material costs (`pieceCost`), whatever the shape, and the block it replaces is used up: nothing goes back for it. A later break gives back the price of the material the block is then made of, as every break does. The table has every material at 1, so today an upgrade costs one of the next material and the SPEC's "upgrade costs rise with tier" is only as true as the table; the rule asks the table, so 5j can make it so by changing numbers, not code. A refund of the old piece is the other fair rule and would be one `refund` call in `HostSession.runUpgrade`; it is flagged in the pull request as a call to make.
- **The better material has to be open.** The host asks the same lock a placement does: a material the points a Terminal has read do not reach is refused with `<Material> needs research`. The screen says more, as it does for a block (`Needs research · 5 of 20`, or `Needs a Research Terminal` when the points are earned and unread). There is no research row of an upgrade's own: 5c and 5d2 pencilled in "upgrade rows" for this part, and none was needed, because an upgrade is opened by the material it makes and the three material rows already gate those. The ladder is unchanged; rows for modules (the Power row, the turret's levels) come with their modules.
- **The command carries the piece the player was shown.** `{ type: 'upgrade', x, y, z, state }`, where `state` is the better piece the screen offered. The host works out the better piece itself and refuses the command if it is not the one named (`that block has changed`): a block that another player upgraded a moment ago, broke, or broke and rebuilt as something else is not upgraded again on a stale press, and nothing is paid twice. A cell with nothing in it is `nothing to upgrade there`, and a block with no better self `already fully upgraded`. The rest is the order every command has: not down, the edit rate, reach, then the economy's (the lock, then the pool).
- **Energy, and the machines.** An upgrade costs the Energy an edit does (`VITALS.energy.perEdit`) and counts toward the run's upgrades. A crawler chewing the block loses its progress when the block changes (the chew is reset by any change to the cell), and the better block holds longer (`ENEMIES.crawler.gnawSeconds`: Scrap 2 s, Composite 3.5, Alloy 6, Energy-Infused 9), so an upgrade is also a repair under fire.
- **On the wire** a command and a number: `upgrade` is a new `BlockCommand`, the event is the `set` every change uses, and the results card's counts, `st` in `sv`, gain a fifth number, `upgraded` ([multiplayer](multiplayer.md#upgrading-a-block-phase-5e)). Protocol version 13.
- **What the screen does.** E strips a heap of debris first, opens a Terminal's ladder next and otherwise upgrades the block under the cursor, so none of the three can be reached by mistake for another. It works in Build Mode or out of it, as a click does (SPEC section 5: outside Build Mode a right click still places). A card under the crosshair says `E  Upgrade to Reinforced Composite` and `Cost 1 Reinforced Composite`. In Build Mode the card also shows an upgrade that would be refused, in red, with the reason in the words above (`Not enough Reinforced Composite`); outside it the card is only there while the upgrade would go, so a crew with nothing to spend is not nagged by every wall it looks at, and a press still says why it did nothing. The status list, the Build Mode key list and the pause card say that E upgrades. The Outpost Overrun card has a seventh tile, `Upgrades completed`, after `Blocks placed`; seven tiles lie as four above and three below.
- **The picture is the material.** An upgrade is a new tile on the same shape, drawn by the meshing every block already goes through, so there is no new model and no new draw call. A block of a better material reads at a glance because the four materials were drawn to (art direction, Phase 2c).
- **Left for later on purpose.** A flash or a sound on the upgraded block, which every screen would play from the event (Phase 6, polish); module levels (5f); the "electrified" wall, which is a wall that hurts what chews it and needs power (5g); a cost that rises with tier and a refund for the old block (5j and the call above); upgrading a whole wall with one press; and a tool for what the machines have chewed, since a broken block is placed anew.
- **For trying it.** With `?debug&stock=200`: `mp.setResearch(n)` opens the tiers (20, 55 and 100 points), `mp.upgrade(x, y, z)` asks the host to upgrade a cell as E does (a fourth argument names the piece this screen believed it was offering), `build.hands().upgrade` is the piece the cursor's block would become and `upgradeRefusal` why not, and `mp.clock().upgraded` is the count the results card shows. The browser specs are `tests/e2e/upgrade.spec.ts` (one player: a closed material turned away on the screen, the card and its price, three presses up to Energy-Infused, nothing better, a short pool, E outside Build Mode, a stale press, the results tile) and a room of a host and a guest in `tests/e2e/multiplayer.spec.ts` (a guest's upgrade swaps the block in every world, the one pool pays, a latecomer is shown it, and the host's three kinds of refusal reach only the guest).

## Phase 5f1: the Basic Turret (2026-10-26)

The first module that acts. SPEC section 9 lists the basic turret among the functional modules of the MVP and gives its upgrades as "basic turret → auto-turret → heavy turret"; until now the only thing that hurt a machine was a player holding the blaster, with Energy to spend. A turret is the crew's own gun: it stands in a cell, and while it stands it shoots the nearest machine it can see, again each time its gun is ready, with nobody at the trigger. This part builds the turret at its first level: the module in the table, the rule by which it shoots, the shot on every screen and the model whose head turns to it. The numbers are in `TURRET` (`src/systems/survival/balance.ts`) and `MODULES` (`src/shared/blocks.ts`) and are first guesses for playtesting and, from 5j, for the balance simulator.

- **The part is split in two: this is 5f1, and the levels are 5f2.** 5f as planned was "the basic turret". The turret itself (placed, drawn, shooting, seen by everyone) is one reviewable change. The levels (E on a turret raises it to the Auto-turret at Tier 2 and the Heavy turret at Tier 3, each with a look and numbers of its own, and the research that opens them) are another, and are easier to judge with a turret standing and shooting in front of you. It is the same kind of call as 5d1 and 5d2, and the letters after 5f do not move. Until 5f2 a turret has one level: its material byte is 1, a state naming a second level is refused as an unknown piece, and E on one says `already fully upgraded`.
- **A turret is a module (5d1), and costs 25 Scrap.** Shape ids 36 to 39 (module 2, four facings), a body box of 0.72 by 0.78 by 0.72 of a cell that movement stands against and the cursor aims at, placed, turned, broken for a refund, sent in a late-join snapshot and saved by the commands and the edit list a block uses: nothing about placing it is new, and the rules of 5d1 for where a module may stand apply to it unchanged. The price is the one the defaults gave (about 25 Scrap, the notes price it in Composite, which a Terminal unlocks), out of the shared pool, and it is not locked: a module's material byte is a level, not a material, so the tier gate does not apply, and the turrets of the better levels are what research opens (5f2). The hotbar's module slot (key 6) now has two modules and Q chooses between them. A Terminal is placed facing the player, so that its screens are what they see, but a turret is placed facing the way the player looks, so that its barrel points outward where they were looking (`ModuleDef.facesPlayer`, in the table with the rest of what a module is); R turns it a quarter turn at a time, and its barrel points where its front looks until it has something to shoot at.
- **What it does.** Every host step (20 a second) a turret whose gun is ready looks for the machines within its range, 18 blocks from the pivot of its head to the middle of a machine's hitbox, and takes the nearest. It traces a bolt from its muzzle to that point, the way a player's shot is traced (`traceShot`): the bolt stops at the first block the crew built, the ground or the Core in the way, and if it reaches the machine the machine takes 5 points and the gun waits 0.5 seconds. That is 10 points a second against the blaster's 28.6 (10 points every 0.35 seconds), so a crawler of 30 health takes a lone turret six shots, a little under three seconds, and the machines of a later night (tougher by 10 percent from the third and by 8 percent more for every night from the fifth) take longer. The table has one row for the turret's level, so 5f2 adds rows (the Auto-turret and the Heavy turret) and not code.
- **A turret needs to see.** This is the one call in the part to look at. A turret shoots only along a clear line, the line a blaster's bolt would take from the barrel: a machine on the far side of a wall of the crew's blocks is not shot, and a turret whose cell is walled in at its own level cannot shoot in that direction at all. A turret on top of a block, a wall or a roof shoots over what is below it, with one blind spot: the foot of its own pillar. The bolt starts inside the turret's own cell and the first block under the gun is the one it stands on, so on a pillar three blocks high a machine within about two and a half blocks of the foot is out of sight, and the lower the pillar the smaller that ground is. The alternative, a shot that goes through blocks, would make walls pointless against what the turret shoots and would make the turret's reach independent of where it stands. When the nearest machine is behind a block the turret tries the next one, up to three a step (`TURRET.tries`) and then gives that step up, so a crawler chewing at a wall does not hide the one in the open behind it, and a step costs three traces at most.
- **No Energy, no ammunition, no friendly fire.** A turret costs the crew nothing to run: SPEC section 9's power (5g) is about a node boosting a turret nearby ("do not require a full simulation"), so a turret that needs power to work would make 5g's generator a requirement and not a reward. Players' bodies are never in the way of a bolt (the blaster's rule too) and a bolt never hurts one. The machines do not hunt turrets: they go for the Core and the players as before and chew through a turret's cell if it is in their way, as they chew a Scrap block (a crawler's gnaw time reads the cell's material byte, which for a module is its level, so a level 1 turret goes in 2 seconds and nobody is refunded). Both are placeholders flagged below.
- **The crew's kills.** What a turret fells counts with the blaster's in the results card's `Machines felled` (`felledCount`), and a night's waves, the research a night earns and the run's end are untouched by who fired.
- **It fires only while the run is going.** The host plays the turrets inside the survival step, after the machines have moved (`SurvivalSim.stepTurrets`), and only when the host steps the run: alone in the world with the mouse free the run is held and so are the turrets, with a guest in the room it always runs, and once the Core has fallen nothing fires. A turret shoots whenever a machine is in its reach, which by day is rare, because the machines still standing at dawn fall back.
- **A shot reaches every screen as the blaster's does.** The host announces it with the same `sh` frame, which gains an optional cell: the turret that fired, with `p` zero (nobody). The codec accepts a zero player only together with a cell and a cell only together with a zero player, so neither can pass for the other ([multiplayer](multiplayer.md#the-basic-turret-phase-5f1)). Protocol version 14. Every screen, the host's included, draws the bolt from the report and none draws it ahead of time: nobody clicked. A turret's bolt is amber (`FX_COLOR.turret`) where a player's is the player's colour, so the crew's guns read apart from its hands, and a kill bursts as a player's does. The hit marker stays the shooter's own, and a turret has no shooter, so there is none.
- **The bolt leaves the barrel, and the head turns to it.** The muzzle is a point on the line from the pivot of the head to the target at a set distance (`TURRET_GUN`, `src/shared/turret.ts`), a pure function of the cell and the end of the bolt: the host traces from it, every screen draws the bolt from it and the model's head is turned along the same line. So the bolt starts where the barrel ends, whichever way the turret faces, and nothing about the head's turn has to travel. The head is turned for the first time when the first shot is drawn and then holds its aim until the next one (or until the cell is drawn again, as when a turret is broken and built anew): a turret does not sweep, it snaps to each shot's end, and between shots it points where it last shot. A guest who joins later sees every turret at rest until it fires.
- **The model.** A steel pad, a column and a housing with a sleeve, a barrel with an amber ring at its tip and a cyan eye over it, with a strip of cyan down the front of the column so that the way the turret faces reads from every side: it is drawn in code like the other modules and `CREDITS.md` does not change ([art direction](art-direction.md#phase-5f1-the-basic-turret)). The pad and the column stand still. The head, a group of its own, turns about the pivot at 0.6 of the cell's height: heading about the vertical and then a lift about its own side, so that a machine below the turret is shot from above and one on a wall from below. It costs 14 draws (the Terminal's 12 and two more, each mesh drawn twice, in the view and in the shadow pass), so a base of turrets will want the cheaper kind that [5d1](#phase-5d1-modules-and-the-research-terminal-2026-10-26) predicted.
- **Left for later on purpose.** The levels (5f2); power by radius, which will boost a turret (5g); machines that hunt turrets, or aim at the nearest thing that hurts them, which is for the special machines of 5i; a turret that sweeps between shots or follows a target; a muzzle flash and a sound for a shot (Phase 6); instancing the models, which a base of a dozen turrets will want (each is 14 draws, 168 for the dozen), and skipping the thin lit parts in the shadow pass; the head clipping into the column by up to 6 cm at the steepest dips (a 70 degree dip, measured in a unit test); and a refund for a turret a machine chews.
- **For trying it.** With `?debug&stock=200`: `mp.place(x, y, z, 292 + facing)` (a turret facing `facing` is `256 + 36 + facing`: the module's shape in the low byte, its level in the high one) or `mp.placeNear` stands one as the host's command path does, `mp.spawnCrawlerAt(x, z)` and `mp.clearEnemies()` set the machines, `mp.shots()` reads the bolts as the screen drew them (a turret's carries its cell as `turret`), `game.world.view.modules.at(x, y, z)` is the group that draws a module and its child named `head` is what turns, and `build.ghost()` is the translucent piece. The browser specs are `tests/e2e/turret.spec.ts` (one player: the module slot's second module, its caption and price and a click that stands it and a break that gives the price back; a crawler sent at a turret is shot with an amber bolt from its barrel, the head turned to it, until it falls, with the shots named for the turret's cell and no player, and the turret falls quiet, its head where it last pointed; a broken turret that stops, and the host's refusal of a level the table does not have) and a room of a host and a guest in `tests/e2e/multiplayer.spec.ts` (a guest's turret fells the host's crawler, every screen draws the bolt from its barrel and turns its head, the kill is the crew's and a break stops the fire).

## Phase 5f2: turret levels (2026-10-26)

The second half of the turret, and the first module with more than one level. SPEC section 9 gives the ladder ("basic turret → auto-turret → heavy turret") and says an upgrade "improves health, damage, range, efficiency, or other stats and changes the visual appearance"; 5e built the key and the command for blocks and said that module levels would come "with the first module that has more than one". From this part a crew that has earned the better materials raises the turret it has, in place, and what stands there afterwards shoots faster or harder and from farther away, and looks it. The numbers are in `TURRET` (`src/systems/survival/balance.ts`) and `MODULES` (`src/shared/blocks.ts`) and are first guesses for playtesting and for the balance simulator of 5j.

- **A level is a row in two tables and a byte of the cell.** `ModuleDef.raises` lists the steps above a module's first level, each a name and a price in one of the pool's materials; the Terminal lists none. A cell's material byte is its module's level (1 as it is built, as 5d1 said), so a turret at any level is the same shape and facing with another byte, and `moduleLevels`, `moduleLevelName` and `moduleLevelCost` answer everything that asks: the name on the card, the price, the lock and the refund of a break. What a level does is the row of `TURRET.levels` with the same number: range, damage, seconds between shots and the weight its bolt is drawn with.
- **E raises it, through 5e's command.** E strips the heap in front of the cursor first, opens a Terminal's ladder next, and otherwise upgrades the piece under the cursor, which for a turret is the same piece one level up. There is no new key and no new command. The host's order and its words are 5e's (not down, the edit rate, reach, then `that block has changed` if the cell is not the piece the player saw, then the lock, then the pool), and so are the card under the crosshair (`E  Upgrade to Auto Turret` over `Cost 10 Reinforced Composite`), Build Mode showing a refusal in red, and the card outside Build Mode only for a raise that would go. A turret at the top of its ladder says `Already fully upgraded`, as Energy-Infused does, and a Terminal, which has no ladder, still opens its research ladder.
- **The steps and the numbers.** The Basic Turret is as 5f1 built it: 25 Scrap, 5 damage every 0.5 s (10 points a second), 18 blocks. The **Auto Turret** is raised with 10 Reinforced Composite, Tier 2: 5 damage every 0.25 s (20 points a second, so a lone one fells a crawler of 30 health in six shots, 1.25 s), 20 blocks. The **Heavy Turret** is raised with 10 Advanced Alloy, Tier 3: 24 damage every 0.6 s (40 points a second, a crawler in two shots, 0.6 s), 26 blocks, against the blaster's 28.6. The Auto Turret is a quicker Basic Turret and the Heavy Turret a different gun: it spends its damage in few shots, so later machines, which are tougher, do not take it many more, and it reaches farther than anything else the crew has. Ten of the better material for each step is a placeholder that makes the price rise with the tier, as SPEC section 9 asks ("upgrade costs rise with tier") as far as Alloy is the dearer of the two.
- **Research is the material's, with no row of its own.** What opens a step is the material that pays for it: the Auto Turret once the points a Terminal has read open Reinforced Composite (20), the Heavy Turret once they open Advanced Alloy (55). That is 5e's call for blocks carried over: `lockedRefusal` asks the material that pays for a module above its first level, the words are the same (`Advanced Alloy needs research`), and the card says `Needs research · 20 of 55`, or `Needs a Research Terminal` when the points are earned and unread. The comment on `RESEARCH.steps` had pencilled in a row for "module upgrades (40)"; none was needed, so the ladder is unchanged.
- **The old level is used up.** A raise costs the new level's price and nothing goes back for the one before, as for a block. A break gives back the price of the level the turret stands at: 25 Scrap for a Basic Turret, 10 Reinforced Composite for an Auto Turret, 10 Advanced Alloy for a Heavy Turret. A crew that raises a turret and then breaks it gets back less than it put in, and the Scrap and the Composite are not refunded. Refunding the whole ladder is the other fair rule; it is flagged below.
- **A turret is built at its first level.** The hotbar offers the Basic Turret only, and a `place` that names a turret at level 2 or 3 is `unknown-block` from the world itself (`VoxelWorld.checkPlace`), or a guest could buy a Heavy Turret at the price of a Basic one by naming it. A raise does not pass through that check: the host applies it as an edit of a cell that holds the turret already.
- **Sturdier as it climbs.** A machine gnaws a cell by its material byte (`ENEMIES.crawler.gnawSeconds`), so an Auto Turret takes a crawler 3.5 s to chew through and a Heavy Turret 6 s, where a Basic Turret takes 2. That is a side effect and not a rule of this part, and a raise starts the chewing over, so it is a repair under fire as a block's is.
- **On the wire** nothing new in shape: the `upgrade` command, the `set` event and the edit list a late joiner is sent. The version moves to 15 because a turret at level 2 or 3 is a state a 14 build does not know ([multiplayer](multiplayer.md#raising-a-turret-phase-5f2)).
- **What the screen draws.** Three models of one pattern, each in the paint of the grade that pays for it ([art direction](art-direction.md#phase-5f2-turret-levels)): the Basic Turret as it was, the Auto Turret in the grey-green and cyan of Reinforced Composite with a twin barrel, and the Heavy Turret in the brushed steel and amber of Advanced Alloy with a big sleeve and side panels. The level is read from the cell's state in each screen's own world and nothing about it travels with a shot, so a screen draws a bolt at the weight of the level it sees. The Heavy Turret's bolt is twice as wide as a player's and brighter, with a larger flash at the muzzle, so a heavy shot reads across the clearing; the Auto Turret's bolt is the Basic Turret's, twice as often. The weight is a number per level (`TURRET.levels[].heft`, never drawn past 2.5), and the debug shot log (`mp.shots()`) records the one a screen drew, so a browser test can tell a screen that shows the raise from one that does not.
- **Left for later on purpose.** A flash or a sound at the raised turret, which every screen would play from the event (Phase 6, with 5e's); power, which will boost a turret (5g); a turret that sweeps or follows a target; instancing the models, which three kinds make more worth doing (the Heavy Turret is 16 draws in view where the other two are 14); refunding the whole ladder; a research row for the steps; the hotbar offering the better levels; and raising a whole row of turrets with one press.
- **Calls to look at.** Ten of the better material a step; the Heavy Turret's 24 damage every 0.6 s against the Auto Turret's 5 every 0.25 s; the old level's price being used up, and so a break giving back only the level it stands at; the gnaw times that make a raised turret sturdier; and the research being the material's, which makes the Auto Turret a use for the first Composite a crew finds and the Heavy Turret one for the first Alloy.
- **For trying it.** With `?debug&stock=200`: `mp.setResearch(n)` opens the tiers (20, 55 and 100 points), a turret stands with `mp.placeNear` as in 5f1 (its state is `256 + 36 + facing`, the level in the high byte) and `mp.upgrade(x, y, z)` raises it, asking the host as E does (a fourth argument names the piece the screen believed it was offering); `build.hands().upgrade` is the piece the cursor's turret would become and `upgradeRefusal` why not; `game.world.view.modules.at(x, y, z).userData.level` is the level a screen draws, and `mp.shots().shots[].heft` the weight it drew a turret's bolt with. The browser specs are the two new tests of `tests/e2e/turret.spec.ts` (E raises a turret in Build Mode, a level at a time: the card shut and open, a short pool, the pool charged for the better level only, the top of the ladder offering nothing; a raised turret fires the Heavy Turret's bolt and fells a crawler in two shots, and breaking it gives back only the Alloy), the old turret test that now also turns away a turret placed at level 2, 3 or 4, and a room of a host and a guest in `tests/e2e/multiplayer.spec.ts` (a guest's raise is the host's to allow, in words, to the guest alone; the one pool pays; every screen draws the raised turret and its heavier bolt; a latecomer is shown it).

## Phase 5g1: the Generator (2026-10-27)

The first module that gives, and the first power. SPEC section 9 lists a "power generator / power node" among the functional modules and calls the synergies optional ("do not require a full simulation"); the approved defaults say that a 10-point research row opens the Generator and the Power Node, that power works by radius with no wiring, and that Energy "regenerates slowly near a powered generator". Until now Energy came back in one place, inside the Core's field eight blocks from the reactor, so a crew working at a wall on the far side of the clearing walked home to recover. This part builds the Generator: the module in the table, the research row that opens it, the field it gives and the ring on the ground that shows where the field ends. The Power Node, the boost to a turret and the lights are 5g2. The numbers are in `POWER` (`src/systems/survival/balance.ts`), `MODULES` (`src/shared/blocks.ts`) and `RESEARCH` (`src/systems/economy/balance.ts`) and are first guesses for playtesting and for the balance simulator of 5j.

- **The part is split in two: this is 5g1, and the Node is 5g2.** 5g as planned was "power by radius". A Generator that stands, is opened by research, charges the players near it and shows how far is one reviewable change, and it makes Energy matter away from the Core by itself. The Power Node that carries a field on, the turret that fires faster inside one and the lights that dim without power all need a power that already exists to read, and are easier to judge with a Generator standing in front of you. It is the same kind of call as 5d, 5e and 5f, and the letters after 5g do not move.
- **A Generator is a module that stands, and standing is powered.** `MODULE.generator` is id 3, four shapes (40 to 43, one for each facing, after the Terminal's 32 to 35 and the turret's 36 to 39), 40 Scrap, one level, and like the Terminal it is placed facing the player and turned with R. It burns nothing, takes no fuel and no wiring: the notes' "powered" is "standing". The host looks again at which Generators stand at the start of every step (`Generators.refresh`, from the same list of modules the turrets are found by), so one placed, broken or chewed through is known the moment the world knows it, and nothing has to be told. A level above the first is `unknown-block`, as for the Terminal.
- **Ten points open it, as a row of its own.** `RESEARCH.steps` held the three rows that open a material (20, 55 and 100). `RESEARCH.groups` is the new table for a row that opens modules: `{ id: 'power', name: 'Power', points: 10, modules: [Generator] }`, with 5g2 adding the Node to its list. `RESEARCH_ROWS` merges the two in order of points (Power sits first), and `nextRow`, `rowsReached`, `moduleLock` and `groupRefusal` read it (`nextStep` is gone: a ladder with two kinds of row has no "next material"). What opens the group is what a Terminal has read, as for a material: the host answers a placement the points do not reach with `Generator needs research`, the ghost is red and says `Needs research · 0 of 10` (or `Needs a Research Terminal` once the points are earned and unread), and the module slot is drawn dim behind a padlock while the group is shut. The lock sits after the material's in the host's order (a heap in the way, the material, the group, then the pool). The first night survived is worth 10 points, and 12 with a clean Core, so a crew has the Power row after night one and builds a Terminal first to read it: the Terminal's ladder now has a row for it, second from the top because the rows go in the order the points reach them (`Power  10 points · Generator`, drawn with the Generator's icon), and the toast at the dawn it is read says `Power unlocked`.
- **The field: 6 blocks, 1 Energy a second, the best field wins.** `POWER.generator = { radius: 6, perSecond: 1 }` is a smaller and slower copy of the Core's field (8 blocks, 2.5 a second). One a second more than pays for a run (the idle drain is 0.08 a second, sprinting adds 0.5) and for a slow build (0.25 an edit), but not for a held click at full pace (six edits a second cost 1.5): a Generator lets a crew work beside a wall, not forever. The distance is measured from the middle of the cell to the middle of the player's body, as the Core's is, so a player on a rise above it is farther than one on the level (`reachDistance`, `inReach` in `src/shared/power.ts`). Fields do not add up: a player in the Core's field and a Generator's, or in two Generators', gets the best one. It reuses the `charging` flag the vitals frame already carries, so the HUD's `Charging` word and the green of the Energy bar say what they said at the Core, with no new frame field.
- **The ring on the ground is where the charge stops, by the same rule.** In Build Mode, with the Generator in hand, a ring of light is drawn round the cell the ghost stands in, and with anything else in hand it is drawn round a Generator the cursor is on (`build.reach()`). It is not a circle drawn at the radius: the reach is a ball and not a column, so ground that climbs or drops away from the cell pulls the edge in, and `reachEdge` finds that edge by halving the span for each of 96 bearings, with the very `inReach` the host charges by. A browser test stands 0.4 block inside and outside the ribbon at four bearings on a slope and sees `charging` flip there. The ribbon lies on the ground, and a curtain of light rises from it 1.25 blocks and fades out, because a ribbon alone is lost in the grass (the first picture of it was); dashes of light march slowly round it from the stage's animation clock. The ring is drawn in Build Mode only, with the pointer taken, as the ghost is.
- **The player is charged only where the host sees them.** The host reads where each player stands while the run goes (as the Core's field does), so alone with the pointer free the run is held and nothing charges, and a guest is charged by where the host last saw them: the host moves a guest it sees move faster than they could (a bank of about 14 blocks) back, so there is no standing at a Generator by teleport. A guest's screen shows the charge from the vitals frame the host sends, like the Core's. A Generator a guest builds charges at once, and one anybody breaks stops charging at the next step.
- **What it looks like.** A field reactor on a steel pad: an upright drum with a band of cyan light round it stands toward the back; a control box on its front carries a gauge and three amber lamps (the side a player sees, as the Terminal's screens are); radiator panels hug its sides; a mast on top carries a blinking amber beacon, the Terminal's blink; and a cyan strip runs round the pad. The cyan band says it is running, which it always is. It is drawn in code in the same six looks as the Terminal and the turret, so `CREDITS.md` does not change ([art direction](art-direction.md#phase-5g1-the-generator)). It faces the player, so its front is the first thing seen. The hotbar's icon is a drawing of it in the HUD's own style, on the cyan grid: a steel drum with a lit band that has a bolt across it, a control box beside it and a bronze beacon on its mast.
- **Where it may stand** is the module ground rule of 5d1, with the same numbers, and its foundation reaches below the floor of its cell so that it stands on a slope as the Terminal does.
- **On the wire** nothing new in shape: a Generator is a state in the `place` command, the `set` event and the edit list a latecomer is sent, and Energy and `charging` come in the vitals frame that has carried them since 4c. The version moves to 16 because a Generator is a state a 15 build does not know ([multiplayer](multiplayer.md#a-generator-and-the-power-row-phase-5g1)).
- **Left for later on purpose.** The Power Node that carries a field on, the turret that fires faster inside one and the lights that dim without power (5g2); a Generator that runs out of anything, or that the crew has to feed (SPEC section 14 bans automation chains); a Generator that charges at a Core's pace on a higher level (it has one level); a ring for the Core's own field; the machines going for a Generator; a sound for it humming and for the ring coming on (Phase 6); and instancing the models, which three kinds of module make more worth doing.
- **Calls to look at.** The numbers: 40 Scrap, 6 blocks, 1 Energy a second (a Generator is cheap enough that a crew builds one or two before a wall and not a field of them); the lock being the Terminal's reading and not the points earned, so a first night with no Terminal shows `Needs a Research Terminal` against the Generator; the ring being a ball's edge on the ground and not a circle; and whether a crew will want Energy away from the Core at all while its field is so near, which play will say.
- **For trying it.** With `?debug&stock=200`: `mp.setResearch(10)` opens the Power row (it sets the points and has the Terminal read them; a second argument of `false` leaves them unread), `mp.placeNear(dx, dz, state, up)` stands a Generator (its state is `256 + 40 + facing`, the level in the high byte) at least eight blocks from the Core, `mp.vitals()` reads Energy and `charging`, `build.reach()` the cell and radius the ring is drawn for, and `game.world.view.reach.geometry.getAttribute('position').array` the ribbon's points (97 pieces, an inner and an outer vertex each). The browser specs are `tests/e2e/generator.spec.ts` (the slot behind a padlock and its words, the ghost and the ring in Build Mode and off it, a click that builds and a break that refunds; the host's rules in words before the points and after; Energy rising beside a Generator and the ring's edge on a slope, with the charge flipping at it, and breaking it ending the charge) and a room of a host and a guest in `tests/e2e/multiplayer.spec.ts` (the crew's points open it for a guest, every screen draws it, the guest beside it is charged on the guest's own screen and the host out of reach is not, a hop out of reach and back flips her flag, and breaking it ends it), with `tests/unit/power.test.ts` and `tests/unit/session-power.test.ts` for the rule itself and the host.

## Phase 5g2: the Power Node and power for turrets (2026-10-27)

The second half of power by radius. 5g1 gave the crew a Generator that charges the players near it; this part makes power something a base has and its turrets feel: a Power Node that carries a Generator's reach on, a turret that fires one and a half times as fast while it has power, lights that burn low on a turret or a Node without any, and a tag under the crosshair that says which it is. SPEC section 9 lists the Node beside the Generator and calls the synergies optional ("power node boosts nearby turrets. Do not require a full simulation"), SPEC section 14 bans automation chains, and the approved defaults say power works by radius with no wiring. So the whole of it is one pure function of the modules that stand, with nothing to lay out, no load and nothing to run out. The numbers are in `POWER` and `TURRET` (`src/systems/survival/balance.ts`) and `MODULES` (`src/shared/blocks.ts`), first guesses for playtesting and for the balance simulator of 5j.

- **A Power Node is a relay, the fourth module.** `MODULE.node` is id 4, four shapes (44 to 47, one for each facing), 12 Scrap, one level. It joins the Power row, so the same 10 points open it (`RESEARCH.groups[0].modules` is now the Generator and the Node, and the Terminal's ladder says so: "10 points · Generator, Power Node", with a no-break space in the Node's name so that a narrow row wraps after the comma), and like the Generator it is placed facing the player and turned with R. A Node that has power carries it `POWER.node.radius` = 8 blocks on, to the turrets and Nodes within that reach of the middle of its cell. It gives no Energy to a player: only a Generator and the Core do, so a Node is not a place to work out at. Eight is a first guess: a Node between a Generator and a wall covers the turrets along the wall, and a crew can walk the whole chain in a few seconds.
- **Power is a function of what stands.** `PowerGrid` (`src/systems/survival/power.ts`, which replaces 5g1's `Generators`) looks at the world's list of modules and works out who has power. A Generator always has it. From every module that has it, power goes out to each Node and turret within that module's reach (a Generator's is the 6 it charges by, a Node's is 8), measured from the middle of a cell to the middle of a cell, in three dimensions, inclusive. Only a Node passes it on: a turret uses what it gets and relays none. A Node with no chain of Nodes back to a Generator has none, however many Nodes stand near each other. The look is repeated only when a module was placed, broken or moved since the last one (`refresh` compares the list), and a base has a handful of modules, so the walk costs nothing a step would notice.
- **A turret with power fires 1.5 times as often, and one without fires as it always did.** `TURRET.poweredRate = 1.5`: the wait between shots is the level's `interval` divided by it, so the three levels shoot every 0.33, 0.17 and 0.40 seconds instead of 0.5, 0.25 and 0.6, and the damage of a shot is unchanged (10, 20 and 40 points a second become 15, 30 and 60). The wait is counted in fractions of a step, carried from when a shot was due, so the 1.5 holds exactly whatever the interval and however the 20 steps a second fall (a turret at 1.5 times shoots three times in two intervals). Power is a boost and never a requirement: SPEC section 9 calls it optional, and a crew before the Power row, or one whose Generator was chewed through at night, still has turrets that work. The boost does not cross levels either (a powered Basic Turret does 15 a second, an unpowered Auto Turret 20), so the ladder of 5f is still what a crew spends on.
- **Lights dim without power.** SPEC section 4 asks for "emissive accents on powered or active tech", so a turret or a Node with no power keeps its shape and its plates but burns its lights low: the glow of the cyan strips and crystal, the amber lamps, the screens and the beacon drops to `DIM_GLOW` = 0.08 of what it is and holds still (no beat on the strips, no blink on the beacon, no breathing on the screens). The renderer makes a dim copy of each paint's glowing looks the first time a module needs one and shares it among the modules of that paint. A Generator never dims (it is its own power) and neither does the Terminal (it takes no part in power). The level was chosen by eye, a powered module and an unpowered one side by side by day and by night ([art direction](art-direction.md#phase-5g2-the-power-node-and-power-for-turrets)): at 0.16 the strips and lamps of a standing module were still bright enough from a few blocks off to read as lit; at 0.08 they are a dull teal and a brown, clearly the same machine at rest; at 0.04 and below the strips are lost. A consequence to look at: until a crew has the Power row and a Generator, every turret it builds burns low, although it works as well as ever. The tag below says why when the cursor is on it.
- **The screens work the lights out themselves.** `world/render` imports nothing from `systems`, so the app does it: a `PowerWatcher` (`src/app/power-watcher.ts`, one for each `ValleyWorld`) runs the same `PowerGrid` over the screen's own copy of the modules each time one is placed, broken or raised, and tells the renderer which have power. Nothing about it travels, and a screen never has to be told that a Generator went: a guest's turret goes dark as the host breaks the Generator it stood by, in step with the host's own turret slowing down. A module drawn again (a turret raised a level) starts lit, so the watcher dresses every module on every change, not only the ones whose power changed.
- **A tag under the crosshair says which.** In Build Mode, with the cursor on a turret or a Node, a small tag under the crosshair (below the interact card, where a turret's `E` card is up) says `Powered` with `Fires 1.5× faster` for a turret and `Passes power on` for a Node, or `No power` with `A Generator or Node in reach boosts it` and `Needs a Generator or Node in reach`: a cyan dot and the words, a grey dot and fainter ring when there is none. The cursor reaches 8 blocks (a placing reach of 6 and two blocks more to look with), so a base can be read from a little way off, as the ring is. A Generator gets no tag (it always has power) and neither does the Terminal. The words and the rate in them come from `powerTag()` and `TURRET.poweredRate`, so they cannot drift from the rule.
- **The ring is the same ring.** A Node in hand draws a ring of 8 blocks round the cell the ghost stands in, and with a block in hand one is drawn round a Node the cursor is on, by the `reachEdge` rule of 5g1 and `reachOfModule` (which is now the Generator's 6 or the Node's 8). The ring is laid for a player's body, a module's reach is measured cell middle to cell middle, so a turret on the level is in reach where the ring ends and on a slope within about half a block of it. The aim's tag is what settles a close call.
- **What it looks like.** A relay pylon on a small steel pad: a slender column rises from a collar and carries three disks that shrink toward the top, like the coils of a relay, with bands of amber insulator between them, a band of cyan above the top one, a bright cyan crystal on the top and a small amber needle above it that blinks. A little console on the front, a screen with its lines of light and three amber lamps, is the side a player sees, and a cyan strip runs round the pad. It uses the Terminal's six looks, so `CREDITS.md` does not change ([art direction](art-direction.md#phase-5g2-the-power-node-and-power-for-turrets)). The hotbar's icon is a drawing of it in the HUD's own style on the cyan grid: a mast of disks with a crystal on top.
- **On the wire** nothing new in shape: a Node is a state in the `place` command, the `set` event and the edit list a latecomer is sent, and power is worked out on every screen from them. The version moves to 17 because a Node is a state a 16 build does not know ([multiplayer](multiplayer.md#a-power-node-and-power-for-turrets-phase-5g2)).
- **Left for later on purpose.** A power that runs out or has to be fed, a load that a Generator can carry, wires or lines of light between modules (SPEC section 14 bans automation chains, and the ring and the tag are the display), turrets that need power to fire at all, a Node that charges players, the other modules that will take power (the Light of 5h will; storage and the bed will not), a hum for a Generator and a click for a Node taking power (Phase 6), the machines going for a Node, and instancing the models.
- **Calls to look at.** The numbers: 12 Scrap, 8 blocks, 1.5 times; the dim look being the default for a turret until the crew has a Generator (an alternative is to dim only a module that had power and lost it, which would make the loss the thing that reads, at the cost of a plain turret never looking powered); a Node costing 12 when a Generator costs 40 (a relay is cheap so that power is a layout choice and not a purchase); the boost not crossing levels; and a Node that carries power from a Node to a Node without limit, so a long enough line of them crosses the valley (it costs 12 Scrap a hop and each can be shot, so the host's machines have something to chew).
- **For trying it.** With `?debug&stock=200` as in 5g1: `mp.setResearch(10)` opens the Power row, `mp.placeNear(dx, dz, state, up)` stands a Node (its state is `256 + 44 + facing`, the level in the high byte; a turret's is `256 + 36 + facing`), `game.world.power.stateAt(x, y, z)` says `on`, `off` or `none` for a cell as this screen works it out, `game.world.view.modules.isPowered(x, y, z)` whether the lights of the module drawn there are up (or null), `build.hands().power` and `build.hands().powerModule` what the cursor is on, and `.ph-power-tag` (with `data-state`) is the tag. The browser specs are `tests/e2e/power-node.spec.ts` (the Node in the module slot behind its padlock and its words, the price, the ghost and the ring of 8, placing and breaking, and the tag; a turret by itself, a Generator beyond its reach, a Node between them, the Generator broken, and a Generator beside the turret with no Node) and a room of a host and a guest in `tests/e2e/multiplayer.spec.ts` (a guest builds the chain, the host breaks the Generator, and both screens put the same lights out); the rule is in `tests/unit/power.test.ts`, the turret's pace in `tests/unit/turrets.test.ts` and, through the host's whole session, `tests/unit/session-shots.test.ts`, the lights in `tests/unit/module-render.test.ts` and `tests/unit/power-watcher.test.ts`, and the tag in `tests/unit/build-controller.test.ts` and `tests/unit/build-hud.test.ts`.

## Phase 5h1: Storage and the Camp row (2026-10-27)

The first of the base's comforts. SPEC section 9 lists the Storage, the bed (a respawn point) and the Light among the modules of the MVP, and the approved defaults say "Storage raises the shared pool cap". The 40-point row of the ladder, which the turret's levels left free (they ride on the material tiers instead), is where they go: one row that opens all three, each as its own part is built (5h1 the Storage, 5h2 the Bed, 5h3 the Light). This part is the Storage and the row that opens it. The numbers are in `POOL` and `RESEARCH` (`src/systems/economy/balance.ts`) and `MODULES` (`src/shared/blocks.ts`), and they are first guesses for the balance simulator (5j) to judge.

- **The Storage is the fifth module.** `MODULE.storage` is id 5, four shapes (48 to 51, one for each facing), 30 Scrap, one level. It is placed facing the player and turned with R, on the Terminal's ground rule, and it takes no part in power, so its lights never dim. 30 is about what the other base modules cost (a Terminal 40, a Generator 40, a turret 25): it raises the ceiling for good, so it is a purchase and not a relay like the Node.
- **Each Storage that stands lets the pool hold 100 more of every material.** The pool's ceiling was one number, `POOL.cap` = 400, for all four materials. It is now `POOL.cap + n * POOL.perStorage`, with `POOL.perStorage` = 100 and n the Storages standing. A `Storehouse` (`src/systems/survival/storehouse.ts`) counts them once a simulation step from the world's own list of modules, as the turrets and the power grid do, and gives the pool its ceiling; a Storage placed, broken or chewed changes it within a step. There is no limit on how many a crew builds, because each one is a cell the machines can chew, so the ceiling follows what the base holds. The base's 400 stays: 5j will say whether it should move.
- **A ceiling that falls leaves the stock where it is.** A crew does not lose its stock because a machine ate a Storage. What the pool holds above a lowered ceiling stays, and nothing more goes in until the crew has spent it down under the ceiling: a heap is refused as full, and the price of the broken Storage is not given back into a pool that is over its ceiling. `Pool.setCap` has always worked this way, and the amber count (below) is what shows it.
- **The Camp row.** `RESEARCH.groups` has a second row, `camp`, at 40 points, that holds the Storage. The ladder now reads Scrap at the start, Power at 10 points, Composite at 20, Camp at 40, Alloy at 55 and Energy at 100. A night is worth 10 points and 12 with a clean one, so the fourth dawn reaches it. The Bed and the Light join the row in the next two parts, so it is named for the three and not for the first. "Camp" is my word for it and it can be renamed in one place (`name` in the table; nothing else reads it).
- **What the crew sees.** The Terminal's ladder gets a row, "Camp · 40 points · Storage", with the Storage's icon, and the toast "Camp unlocked" when the points are read, as for any row. The module slot (key 6, then Q four times from the Terminal) shows the Storage's icon, a steel container with a door, behind a padlock until the row is read, and the line over the hotbar says "Storage" over "Cost 30 Scrap". There is no ring: it reaches nowhere. When the ceiling changes a toast says so, "Storage built · the pool holds 500 of each" or "A Storage is gone · the pool holds 400 of each", and the count on a material's hotbar slot turns amber while the pool holds all of it that it can (the stock is at the ceiling or over it). The first look at a run, a guest that has not heard the host yet and a run that starts over (the ceiling goes back to 400 with the research points) are not changes, so they say nothing.
- **What it looks like.** A steel container on a small pad, built in the Terminal's six looks (steel, trim, amber, cyan lights, a screen and a beacon) so `CREDITS.md` does not change: ribbed side panels, a roof with a vent hood, a double door with a status strip over it and two small cyan lamps by the handles, amber hazard dashes on the pad's front, and a beacon at the back of the roof that blinks ([art direction](art-direction.md#phase-5h1-the-storage)). The hotbar's icon is a drawing of it in the HUD's own style on the cyan grid.
- **On the wire** nothing new in shape. ## Phase 5h2: the Bed (2026-10-27)

## Phase 5h3b: the light a Light throws (2026-10-27)

The second half of the Light. A Light with power now lights what it faces: by night a warm cone falls from its lens on the ground, the blocks, the modules, the grass and trees, the machines and the fragments of a broken block in front of it, in the lamp's own colour, so what burns on the pad is what lands on the ground. By day it is a tenth of itself, because a floodlight does nothing to the sun. It is a look and not a rule: nothing in the game reads it (no sight, no fear of the dark, no machine drawn to it or shy of it), which is a conversation of its own and not this part's. SPEC section 4 asks for emissive accents on powered tech and section 9 lists a light; this is what makes the Light worth its 10 Scrap.

- **A lamp is a few numbers in the atmosphere, not a light in the scene.** The renderer has no three.js lights: every lit surface is a shader of its own that shares the atmosphere's uniforms, the sun and the sky among them. A lamp joins them: where the light leaves the lens and which way it is aimed, as two 4-vectors in an array of eight, with the colour, the shape and how many are in use beside them (`src/engine/lamps.ts`). One GLSL function, `lampLight`, adds up what the lamps give a point and its normal (`src/engine/glsl/atmosphere.glsl.ts`), and each lit shader adds one line to its own light before the atmosphere is applied: terrain, blocks, modules, trees, grass, machines and debris. Nothing is drawn for a lamp, so no draw call, mesh or pass is added.
- **The eight nearest, and the preset says how many of them.** The stage keeps the list of Lights that burn and, each frame, writes the nearest to the camera into the eight slots; Low uses 2, Balanced 6 and Ultra 8 (`lamps` in `src/shared/quality.ts`). A base of forty Lights costs the shaders what eight do. The price is that a lamp past the nearest few goes out until the camera comes nearer, and a lamp changing places in the ranking can make a pool of light jump: a known gap, and not one a base of a few Lights meets.
- **Which Lights burn is worked out where their lights already are.** The `PowerWatcher` that dims the turrets and Nodes of a screen hands the stage the Lights that have power, each with the place its lens is drawn (`src/world/render/lamp-source.ts` reads the model's own numbers: the lean of the head, its height, the lens in front of the pivot) and the way the face of the lens looks. Nothing is sent: a latecomer's screen works the same answer out from the edit list it is given, as it does for the lights themselves, and the version stays 20.
- **What the light is like.** A cone with a soft edge (full strength within 43 degrees of the lens's axis, gone to a tenth of that at 62), a falloff that is half at 2.6 blocks and nothing past 15, a tenth of the strength spilled outside the cone so the ground beside and behind a Light is not black, and a half-wrap on the surface's angle so a pool on flat ground reads as one. The numbers are in `LAMP_LIGHT` in `lamps.ts`; they were chosen by eye in the night pictures (the first guess, a brightness of 9, burned the crates out, so it is 7), and the shader reads them through the shared uniforms, so retuning is a change to that one table.
- **A tenth by day, all of it by night.** The lamp's colour is its brightness times `0.1 + 0.9 x night`, where `night` is the sky's own darkness (0 until the sun is within 2 degrees of the horizon, 1 once it is 14 degrees below it, and smooth between). By day a lamp adds a faint warmth to what is close to it and nothing more.
- **No shadows.** The sun has a shadow map and a lamp does not, so the light goes through what is in the way: the ground, and whatever stands behind a wall or a tree, takes the light as though the wall or the tree were not there, if it is in the cone and in range. A shadow per lamp would be a shadow map or a distance field each, the cost the Light is meant not to have. Said in the pictures, not hidden.
- **Left out on purpose.** Shadows; the lake, the backdrop and the intro film, which have shaders of their own and are not lit by lamps; a lamp coming on over a moment, flickering or being switched; colours and bigger or smaller Lights; the player's own light; anything in the game that reads the light; instancing the models.
- **Calls to look at.** The look of it: the warm colour, the reach (15 blocks, half at 2.6) and the brightness, all set by eye in software-rendered pictures and to be judged on a real display. The light going through walls. The eight-lamp limit. The Light not yet being an advantage in play, which is a decision for when something reads it.
- **For trying it.** Open the game by night as in 5h3a (`?debug&stock=200&time=night`), `mp.setResearch(40)`, press B, 6 and Q six times, right-click to stand a Light and `mp.placeNear(-3, 1, 296)` to stand a Generator beside the player: the cone comes on with the Generator and goes out when it is broken. `stage.atmosphere.lamps.burning` is how many Lights burn and `.active` how many the last frame was lit by; `stage.atmosphere.uniforms.uLampShape.value.set(half, range, spill, wrap)` retunes the shape live. The unit tests are `tests/unit/lamps.test.ts` (the list, the choice of the nearest, the quality limit, the colour by day and by night, the shaders carrying the function, and a scan that every lit shader asks for it), `lamp-source.test.ts` (the source is where the lens is drawn, in front of it and square to it, at all four facings) and `power-watcher.test.ts` (which Lights burn); the browser spec `tests/e2e/light.spec.ts` follows the count with the Generator and the strength with the sky.

## Phase 5i1: the armored crawler and the cells it leaves (2026-10-27)

The first of the special machines, and the first source of Tier 4. SPEC section 7 asks for tougher variants and specials (armored, flying, base-targeting) every few nights, and the approved defaults say "Tier 4 cells drop only from special enemies and rare anomaly nodes": until now the top row of the research ladder opened a material nothing in the game gave. The wave plan has named an armored machine from night 4 (one, and one more every third night after) since 4f, but it fought as a crawler with two and a half times the Health. This part gives it a body of its own, a plating that turns part of every hit, and a drop, and gives every kind of machine a table of its own to be read from, so the flying skimmer (5i2) and the breacher (5i3) are rows and not rewrites. The numbers are in `ENEMIES` and `WAVES.kinds` (`src/systems/survival/balance.ts`) and are first guesses for the balance simulator (5j) to judge.

- **A kind is a body.** `ENEMIES` had one machine, and the simulation read it everywhere. Each kind now has an `EnemyBody` (Health, speed, turning, size, the box a bolt is judged against, the step it climbs, its bite and reach, how far a player pulls it off its way, how long it takes to chew each material, how much of a hit it takes and what it leaves), `bodyOf(kind)` finds one, and a machine carries its body from the moment it is made. The walking, chewing, biting and crowding read the machine's own body; a kind the table does not know is made as a crawler. `hitboxOf` (the blaster, the turrets and the bolts every screen draws) reads the same table, so there is one list of what the machines are.
- **The armored crawler is kind 1.** 75 Health against the crawler's 30, a walk of 2 blocks a second against 2.6 (a player's is 4.4, so it can be outrun), a bigger body (a radius of 0.5 and 1.1 high, a box 1.5 across and 1.25 high for a bolt), a bite that takes 9 off a player and 8 off the Core every 1.2 seconds after a half-second wind-up (6 and 5, every second, for a crawler), a pull of 6 blocks on a player against 7, and it climbs one block and chews through a wall in the same time a crawler does. It comes to the Core the way a crawler comes, by the same map.
- **Plating is a share of every hit.** A hit takes `takes` of its points off, 0.6 for the armored crawler and 1 for a plain one. A multiplier and not a flat reduction, so no weapon is ever useless against it: a flat 5 off every hit would turn a basic turret's shot of 10 into 5 and the blaster's into 5, and a better weapon is always better. It applies at the one place a machine is hurt, so the blaster, every level of turret and anything that comes after it are plated alike. On night 1 a spawned armored crawler takes thirteen shots of the blaster to a crawler's three; on night 4, when it first comes in a wave, with the nights' extra 10 percent, fourteen to four. A basic turret on its own takes about fourteen seconds, an Auto Turret seven and a Heavy Turret three and a half.
- **A felled one leaves Tier 4 cells in the pool.** Three Energy-Infused cells, a cell being what one block of it costs (decisions, Phase 5b), so one armored crawler pays for three blocks. They go straight into the shared pool, as much as it has room for, and not onto the ground to be picked up: there is nobody to be the one to pick them up in a crew, nothing to fetch in the dark at the height of a night and no way for a heap to be lost to a chewing machine. A crawler leaves nothing. Machines that fall back at dawn, or are cleared, leave nothing, and neither do those felled after the Core has fallen. The cells count with what the crew has gathered, for the results card. Whether the Tier 4 row has been researched does not matter to the pool: it holds the cells, and the padlock is on building with them.
- **The crew is told.** Every screen gets the word `drop` (the cells and the material) and the HUD says "A machine left 3 Energy-Infused in the pool". It is a toast, as the others are, one for each machine felled, and nothing is said when the pool had no room for any of it. The count itself reaches every guest in the pool's own frame, so a guest that misses the word still has the right count.
- **The night 4 wave is the first with one.** The wave director now makes each machine with the body its kind names (`WAVES.kinds`), so the armored special of night 4 and of every third night after it comes as the new machine, with the night's extra Health on top of its own. The flying and breaching specials still come as crawlers, until 5i2 and 5i3 give them theirs; their slots in the plan, and so the counts of every night, do not change.
- **It is drawn as itself.** A model of its own, drawn 1.2 times the size of a crawler: the crawler's build bulked out, with a deeper hull under two decks of plate and amber ribs across the top, plate along the flanks and over the brow, a ram under the head, heavier jaws and thicker legs, in a darker steel with a warmer, rustier plate and a brighter amber so that it reads at dusk, and a slower, heavier stride for the ground it covers. Each kind is one instanced mesh, so a night with both is two draw calls (and two in the sun's shadow pass) and a night with only crawlers is the one it was: the mesh of a kind with none is put away. The look is in `docs/art-direction.md`.
- **Protocol 21.** The `drop` event is new, and kind 1 is a machine a version 20 build would refuse the packet for. The number of kinds the wire accepts is now 2.
- **Left out on purpose.** A flash, spark or sound for a hit the plating turns (the hurt flash is the same as a crawler's); any weapon that pierces plating; the flying skimmer and the breacher (5i2 and 5i3); the rare anomaly nodes that are the other source of Tier 4 the defaults name; cells lying on the ground; a different drop for a machine felled by a turret and one felled by a player; a drop that grows with the night; the numbers beyond a first guess.
- **Calls to look at.** All the numbers: 75 Health and 0.6 are about right for a machine that should be a threat to a base of a few turrets at night 4 and a nuisance by night 10, and 3 cells a kill is a handful (one armored crawler on night 4, two on night 7, three on night 10: eighteen blocks by then if every one is felled) that the simulator should say is too few or too many. One toast for each kill may be loud on a night with several. The model's look, judged on a real display. The pool filling with Tier 4 cells the crew cannot yet build with, which is a reason to hurry the 100-point row or a pile that does nothing, as the play shows.
- **For trying it.** Open the game by night (`?debug&night=1&waves=0`); `mp.spawnCrawlerAt(x, z, 1)` puts an armored crawler on open ground at (x, z) (the third number is the kind, and it is a plain crawler without it), `mp.fireAtMachine()` shoots the one nearest the player, and `mp.pool().stock[3]` is the Tier 4 count. The unit tests are `tests/unit/enemy-bodies.test.ts` (the table: every kind the wire admits has a body, the armored crawler is heavier and slower and plated, the drop), `enemies.test.ts` (an armored machine walks, bites, chews and takes a share of a hit), `survival-drops.test.ts` (the cells arrive once, from the blaster and from a turret, as much as there is room for, never for a crawler, after dawn or once the run is over), `waves.test.ts` (night 4 sends one), `session-enemies.test.ts` (the kind and the word reach a guest), `wire.test.ts` and `run-hud.test.ts` (the event and its message) and `enemy-render.test.ts` (a mesh and model for each kind); the browser specs are `tests/e2e/armored.spec.ts` (alone) and one test in `multiplayer.spec.ts` (a guest fells the host's armored crawler).

## Phase 5i2: the flying skimmer (2026-10-27)

The second of the special machines, and the first that does not walk. SPEC section 7 names a flying special among the tougher variants, and the wave plan has sent one from night 5 (and one more every fourth night after it) since 4f, but it came as a crawler. This part gives it a body: a skimmer that crosses the lake, the cliffs and the crew's walls in a straight line, hangs 2.6 blocks over whatever is under it and zaps from above. It is the first machine that a wall cannot answer, so the answer is the blaster and the turrets (which aim up at it), and a roof. The numbers are in `ENEMIES.flyer` and `WAVES.kinds` (`src/systems/survival/balance.ts`) and are first guesses for the balance simulator (5j) to judge.

- **It is kind 2, and a body that flies.** `EnemyBody` gains an optional `flies` (how high it hangs, how fast it can climb or sink, how far ahead it looks and how high above the ground it looks for blocks), and a machine with one skips everything that was about the ground: the route, the straight-line check, the step, the chewing. Everything else about it is the same table as the others. The wire accepts kinds 0 to 2 now, and a kind it does not know is still made and drawn as a crawler.
- **It goes for a player, else for the Core, in a straight line.** The nearest player within 10 blocks (13 once it has chosen one, as a crawler does), over whatever is between; with nobody near, the middle of the Core. It does not walk round the lake or look for a gap in a wall. It flies at 3.6 blocks a second against a player's 4.4, so a player running can keep ahead of one but not get away from it, and a crowd of them does not bunch up on one another (machines that fly keep apart from each other, and pass over those that walk).
- **It hangs a hover over the highest cover.** Its height is 2.6 blocks over the highest of the ground (or the water, over a lake) and whatever the crew has built in the column under it, and in the columns along its heading for 3.5 blocks, a block apart, so it starts to rise before a wall and a wall one block thick is seen. It eases there no faster than 8 blocks a second, which is quick enough to clear a wall of about ten blocks from the 3.5 blocks it looks ahead; a taller wall, or a sheer cliff, is more than it can rise for in the time, and it brushes the top of it (nothing collides with a skimmer, so nothing is hurt, but the hull is seen passing through an edge). The Core is left out of what it looks ahead at, so it stops beside the Core's wall to zap it, at the height it was flying at, and not over the Core.
- **It zaps from above.** 5 off a player and 4 off the Core, after a 0.4 second wind-up, every 1.3 seconds: a little weaker than a crawler's bite (6 and 5 every second), because it does it from where nothing answers it. A player is in reach when it is within 1.8 blocks across and no more than 4.2 blocks above or below the underside of its hull, the side of the Core within 2.4. It keeps settling to the cover under it while it zaps, so a tower raised under it lifts it, and it hangs still while it does.
- **A roof keeps a player out of its reach.** A player under any roof is more than 4.2 blocks below its hull, which hangs over the roof, so it hovers there and cannot touch them, and it cannot be hit from under the roof either (the blaster and the turrets see only along a line the roof blocks). That is the answer the building gives, as a wall is to a crawler. The Core has no such cover: its reach has no height, so a skimmer zaps it from beside its wall whatever is built over it.
- **It comes in anywhere.** A wave puts it on the ring round the Core like the rest, but where a walker needs open ground with a way in, it needs only the spot to be on the map, so it comes in over the lake and the cliffs too. It arrives already at its height, growing in over the same 0.6 seconds that the others rise out of the ground in.
- **Fragile, and nothing to plate it.** 20 Health against a crawler's 30, and it takes all of every hit: two blaster shots on the first night (three for a crawler), more with the night's extra steel. A turret aims up at it, at the middle of its hull. A box 1.3 across and 0.55 high is what a bolt is judged against, so a miss by a little over the top of it is a miss.
- **A felled one leaves 2 Energy-Infused in the pool.** The drop of 5i1, as a row of the table: two cells against the armored crawler's three, because it is the easier kill. They go straight into the shared pool, count with what the crew has gathered, and the crew is told in the same message.
- **The night 5 wave is the first with one.** The wave director makes it with the body its kind names (`WAVES.kinds`), one on night 5, two on night 9, three on night 13, with the night's extra Health on top of its own. The breacher still comes as a crawler until 5i3.
- **It is drawn as itself.** A model of its own, drawn 1.25 times its model: a flat hull of blue-grey steel with a deck of plate, an amber spine and amber strips down both flanks, a red eye across its nose, a fin behind, four rotor pods on arms with crossed blades that turn all the time, and an emitter under its belly, a lens that smoulders and burns as a zap charges, with a thin beam that grows out of it. It leans into a flight as far as its speed takes it, bobs a little and dips its nose to zap. It is one more instanced mesh (a kind with none on the field is put away) and the look is in `docs/art-direction.md`.
- **Protocol 22.** Kind 2 is a machine a version 21 build would refuse the packet for. Nothing else is on the wire: its height is the `y` of the pose the packet already carries, and the zap is the bite every machine has.
- **Left out on purpose.** The breacher (5i3); any attack by the skimmer on blocks or modules (it harms players and the Core only); a line-of-sight check for its zap (it can zap through a low wall); a sound, a hit effect or a health bar; a deflect from anything; a drop that grows with the night; being shot out of the air as a different kind of hit (a bolt lands, or it does not); flying kinds for other waves; the numbers beyond a first guess.
- **Calls to look at.** All the numbers: 20 Health and two shots, a zap a little weaker than a crawler's bite, and a hover of 2.6 are guesses that the simulator should say are too soft or too hard. That a roof of any kind puts a player out of reach: it makes the first skimmer a thing to build against and it may be too easy a cure, which is for play to say (the other way to read the rule is that nothing on the ground is safe, and a skimmer should zap through a roof). The Core's having no cover. The skimmer hanging over the top of a tall wall and not being hit by anything on the ground behind it. The look, judged on a real display.
- **For trying it.** Open the game by night (`?debug&night=1&waves=0`); `mp.spawnCrawlerAt(x, z, 2)` puts a skimmer at (x, z) (anywhere on the map; the third number is the kind), `mp.warp(seconds)` plays the host's clock while the pointer is free, `mp.fireAtMachine()` shoots the one nearest the player, and `mp.enemies()` lists them with their heights. The unit tests are `tests/unit/flyers.test.ts` (it is anywhere on the ring and at its height over land and water, straight to the Core over a lake, a ridge and a wall and chewing nothing, up and over a wall and back down, climbing no faster than it can, zapping the Core from beside it and not from over it, following the land while it zaps, the nearest player in its pull and not one beyond a wall, the zap's reach down, a roof that keeps it off, passing over walkers and spreading from flyers, and falling to a blow once), `enemy-bodies.test.ts` (the table: the skimmer is small, quick, fragile and the only one that flies), `waves.test.ts` (night 5 sends one with the night's steel on it), `wire.test.ts` (kind 2 is accepted and kind 3 refused), `session-enemies.test.ts` (every screen draws it hanging over the ground, it crosses a wall that holds the walkers and zaps the Core from beside it, and its cells reach the pool and every screen), `survival-drops.test.ts` (two cells, once, from the blaster aimed up at it and from a turret) and `enemy-render.test.ts` (a mesh and model of its own, its rotors turning however fast it flies, the lean, the budget of a hundred); the browser specs are `tests/e2e/skimmer.spec.ts` (alone: it hangs in the air, goes over a wall, zaps from above and is felled) and one test in `multiplayer.spec.ts` (a guest fells the host's skimmer).

## Phase 5i3: the breacher (2026-10-27)

The last of the special machines, and the first whose job is the base and not the crew. SPEC section 7 names a base-targeting special among the tougher variants, and the wave plan has sent one from night 9 (and one more every fourth night after it) since 4f, but it came as a crawler. This part gives it a body: a ram that takes no notice of the crew, walks the crawlers' map to the first wall of the crew's on its way (or to the Core), stands facing it, charges for 1.2 seconds and goes off, once: a hole in the wall, a wound for whoever is standing near, and a bite out of the Core. A wall that holds a crawler for half a minute holds a breacher for as long as it takes it to walk up to the wall, so the answer is the blaster and the turrets before it arrives (nine shots), walls a long way out from what matters, and not standing next to them. The numbers are in `ENEMIES.breacher` and `WAVES.kinds` (`src/systems/survival/balance.ts`) and are first guesses for the balance simulator (5j) to judge.

- **It is kind 3, and a body that goes off.** `EnemyBody` gains an optional `blast` (the wind-up, how far ahead of it the blast is centred, its radius, how near a player has to be to be wounded and by how much, what it takes off the Core and how near the Core's walls the blast has to be). A machine with one does nothing else: it has no bite, no notice of players and no chewing. The wire accepts kinds 0 to 3 now, and a kind it does not know is still made and drawn as a crawler.
- **It walks the crawlers' map, and takes no notice of the crew.** One route field serves the crawler, the armored crawler and the breacher (the body that is solved for is the crawler's), so it goes where a crawler would go, and a player standing in its way does not pull it off it. It blows a wall where the route goes through one, which is where chewing it is cheaper than walking round, so a wall with a way round it is walked round and a ring with none is not.
- **It stops at a wall and charges.** When the next column of its route is walled and within 0.75 blocks of it, or when the Core is within 1.4 blocks of it, it stands still, turns to face it (within 0.2 radians) and charges for 1.2 seconds. The charge is the flag every machine's bite has, so the picture builds it up and a guest sees it with nothing new on the wire. Once begun it is committed: it goes off even if the wall has been taken away, and a breacher felled in the middle of the charge does not go off at all.
- **What the blast does.** It is centred 0.9 blocks ahead of the machine's middle, at half its height. Every block of the crew's whose middle is within 1.8 blocks of that point is broken, which against a wall one block thick is a hole of about three wide and two high: the cells in front of it and one beside it on each side, and a row above or below only when the machine stands low or high of the row. A module in the way goes with its block. The Core's own cells stay, and the ground is not the crew's to lose, so terrain is untouched. A player whose chest is within 3 blocks of that point is wounded by 35, flat, through anything that stands between. The Core takes 150 of its 1000 when the point is within 2 blocks of its walls, so a wall closer than three blocks to the Core is no cover for it. Then the machine is gone: no drop, nothing felled, no message.
- **The host breaks the blocks, as it breaks a chewed one.** The blast is worked out in the host's simulation and the cells it clears are queued for the host, which breaks them as the world's own edit (`set`), so every screen sees them go, and a guest who joins later is shown the hole. They are broken only while the run is going, as chewed blocks are, so a run that is held (a player alone in a menu) keeps its walls until the player is back.
- **Felled before it goes off, it leaves 4 Energy-Infused.** The most of any kind (3 for the armored crawler, 2 for the skimmer), because it is the one a good crew should kill: 90 Health, taking every point of a hit, so nine blaster shots on the first night (against a crawler's three), and the slowest of the machines, at 1.8 blocks a second against a crawler's 2.6: it takes about 25 seconds to cross the 46 blocks from where the machines come in to the Core, and a turret reaches 18 to 26 blocks, so one at a wall has it in its sights for ten seconds or more. A felled breacher does none of what it came to do.
- **The night 9 wave is the first with one.** The wave director makes it with the body its kind names (`WAVES.kinds`), one on night 9, two on night 13, three on night 17, with the night's extra Health on top of its own. Every kind of the plan has a body of its own now: nothing in the waves stands in as a crawler.
- **It is drawn as itself.** A model of its own, drawn 1.3 times its model: a broad low hull of dark iron with plate of a demolition yellow gone dull down its flanks and over its deck, a blunt head with a ram plate across its face (wings of plate back along its sides, a grille of amber bars under a red eye strip) and teeth at the foot of it, an engine with two stacks behind, and on its back an orb in a cage of four posts, a dull ember at rest. It walks slowly on four stout legs. As it charges, over the 1.2 seconds, the whole of it shudders and sinks on its legs, its amber lights burn up, and the orb swells until it strains the cage and burns from an ember to white. It is one more instanced mesh (a kind with none on the field is put away), and the look is in `docs/art-direction.md`.
- **A blast is an event, with a flash and no words.** The host announces `blast` with the point it went off at (to a tenth of a block), so that every screen draws a flash and a spray of sparks there (`ShotFx.blast`). It has no message: the hole in the wall and the wound say it. The event is for the flash alone and costs a few bytes once per breacher.
- **Protocol 23.** Kind 3 is a machine a version 22 build would refuse the packet for, and `blast` is an event it does not know. A host and a guest on different versions refuse each other and say why.
- **Left out on purpose.** A sound, a hit effect on the player beyond the wound flash every hit has, a shake of the screen, a health bar; a blast on terrain; damage that falls off with distance, or a check for the line from the blast to a player (it wounds through a wall); a breacher that goes for modules or turrets in particular (it goes off at whatever wall is on its way, and takes with it what stands in the cells); being deflected or blocked by anything; a drop that grows with the night; the numbers beyond a first guess.
- **Calls to look at.** All the numbers: 90 Health, 1.8 blocks a second, a 1.2 second charge, a radius of 1.8, 35 off a player within 3 blocks and 150 off a Core of 1000 are guesses that the simulator should say are too soft or too hard (150 is a seventh of the Core, and the Core mends a quarter of that every ten seconds of daylight, so one is a nuisance and four in a night a danger). That a blast wounds through walls and breaks modules as freely as blocks. That it uses the crawlers' map, so a wall with a gap in it is walked round and a breacher is only dangerous to a wall that closes the way. That a breacher at a ring goes off at the first wall it meets, whichever that is, and not at the weakest. The look and the cost of a fourth mesh, judged on a real display.
- **For trying it.** Open the game by night (`?debug&night=1&waves=0`); `mp.spawnCrawlerAt(x, z, 3)` puts a breacher on open ground at (x, z) (the third number is the kind), `mp.warp(seconds)` plays the host's clock while the pointer is free (the host breaks blocks only while the pointer is taken, so take it for a moment after a blast), `mp.fireAtMachine()` shoots the one nearest the player, `mp.blocks().count` and `mp.block(x, y, z)` read the crew's blocks, and `mp.clock().coreHealth` the Core. The unit tests are `tests/unit/breachers.test.ts` (it takes the crawlers' route to the Core at its own pace and takes no notice of the crew; it goes off at the Core's wall and nowhere else, taking the Core's health and leaving nothing behind; it walks round a short wall rather than blast it when the map says that is cheaper; it stops at the wall its route runs into for the whole of its wind-up with the attack flag up; its blast breaks the blocks within its radius, all at once, and no others, and spares the Core's own cells; it wounds the players whose chests are within reach, whoever they are, and no others; two that are ready in a step both go off and both are spent; felled during its charge it does not go off, no block breaks and nobody is wounded; it takes its ninety points of blows to fell on the first night; a charge that has begun goes off over a wall that is gone; in a run the screens are told where it went off and the host is given the blocks to break), `enemy-bodies.test.ts` (the table), `waves.test.ts` (night 9 sends one), `wire.test.ts` (kind 3 is accepted, kind 4 refused, and the blast event round-trips), `session-enemies.test.ts` (every screen draws it, its charge flag reaches a guest, the hole in the wall is seen by the host and the guest and the Core is wounded), `survival-drops.test.ts` (four cells, once, from the blaster and from a turret), `run-hud.test.ts` (a blast has no words), `enemy-render.test.ts` (a mesh and a model of its own, its charge built up over the time the table says) and `shot-fx.test.ts` (the blast's flash and sparks); the browser specs are `tests/e2e/breacher.spec.ts` (alone: at the Core, at a ring of walls, felled on its way) and one test in `multiplayer.spec.ts` (a host's breacher blows a ring open and every screen sees the hole).

## Phase 5j: the balance simulator and what a block costs (2026-10-27)

The last part of the economy. SPEC section 9 asks for enough materials in a day for "1-2 meaningful upgrades" if the group is reasonably efficient, for "meaningful progress every 2-3 nights" for a coordinated group, and for upgrade costs that rise with tier; `docs/architecture.md` asks for a headless balance simulator that prints income against cost per night, "so tuning is checked rather than guessed"; and the roadmap's test for this part is that the report shows one to two upgrades a day and an unlock every two to three nights. Until now every number of the economy was a first guess. This part builds the simulator, reads the tables with it, and changes the two sets of numbers it found out of line.

- **A model that plays the real economy.** `src/systems/economy/simulator.ts` is pure and has no clock. It drives the game's own `Economy` (`harvest`, `dawn`, `pay`, `upgradeRefusal` and the research's `credit` and `claim`) over `debrisField(seed, …)`, the field of heaps a real run of that seed has, a day at a time, with a crew that is only a few numbers. There is no second copy of a rule to keep in step: a change to a price, a threshold, the yield of a heap or a gate changes the report. The crew buys and raises pieces through `pieceCost`, `pay` and `upgradeRefusal`, so the research gate is the game's, not the model's own.
- **The crew is two profiles.** `CREWS` in `balance.ts`: `reasonable` strips 60 percent of the strips left in the heaps each day, does not keep the Core untouched (10 research points a night, not 12) and fells half of the special machines before they do their work, and `coordinated` strips 90 percent, keeps the Core clean and fells 90 percent. They are the spec's "reasonably efficient" group and "coordinated group". Both strip the better materials first and the nearest heaps first, put up the Research Terminal on day 0 as soon as the pool can pay for it (nothing opens without one), build a turret and a section of wall of 12 blocks in Scrap each day (three and four on the first day), and spend what the pool holds on the next step of what they have built: a turret up its levels, a section of wall up the materials. A step is one upgrade, whatever it costs.
- **The report.** `npm run balance` prints each crew's days on one seed (the points and tier, what was gathered of each material, the Scrap spent building, the turrets and walls on each rung, the pool, the upgrades bought and the rows the dawn opened) and then the pacing across eight seeds: upgrades a day (mean, fewest, most) and the nights between unlocks (mean, longest). It is a Vitest file of its own (`tests/report/balance.report.ts`, run by `vitest.balance.config.ts`), so `npm test` does not print it.
- **What it is held to.** `TARGETS` in `balance.ts` carries the spec's numbers, and `tests/unit/balance-sim.test.ts` holds both crews to them on ten seeds, five that the prices were tuned on and five that they were not: a mean of one to two upgrades a day over the days from the first on which a better material comes in, and an unlock of the research ladder every two to three nights on average, never more than four nights apart (the nights of the unlocks do not depend on the seed). Beside them are the gates: no heap of a better material is taken before the research that opens it, no step is bought before its tier even from a pool that is full of everything, nothing opens when no Terminal ever stands, the pool stays between nothing and its ceiling, the research opens each row on the night the points reach it, a run plays out the same every time, and the ladders are read from the tables (every step is priced as the game prices the piece it makes, and costs more than the one below it).
- **What it said about the tables as they were.** Every block cost 1 whatever it was made of, so "costs rise with tier" held for the turret's two steps and for nothing else, and an upgrade in place cost as little in Energy-Infused as in Reinforced Composite. The crews made 2.06 and 2.08 upgrades a day on average, with 4 on the busiest day: just over the top of the spec's range, and the cheapest upgrades first. The research ladder was already in range: unlocks on nights 1, 2, 4, 6 and 10 for the reasonable crew (1, 2, 4, 5 and 9 for the coordinated one), a mean of 2.25 and 2.0 nights apart.
- **The prices.** A block costs 1, 2, 3 and 5 of its own material, Scrap to Energy-Infused (`MATERIALS` in `src/shared/blocks.ts`), and the Heavy Turret's step costs 20 Advanced Alloy, twice the Auto Turret's 10 Reinforced Composite (`MODULES`). An upgrade in place takes the price of the better piece, so these are also what an upgrade costs. The crews now make 1.64 and 1.67 upgrades a day on average, every seed within one to two (1.55 to 1.73 and 1.64 to 1.73), with the first, middle and last thirds of a run within a few tenths of each other, and the unlock gaps are as they were. The modules' own prices (in Scrap), the research thresholds (20, 55 and 100 points, and the Power and Camp rows), the points a night earns, the pool and the heaps' yields were left alone.
- **Which numbers do the work.** The simulator says that the pacing is carried by the price of Reinforced Composite and the Heavy Turret's step. Moving only the turret leaves the crews at 2.10 and 2.15 upgrades a day, moving only the blocks at 2.00 and 2.03, and both gets 1.64 and 1.67. The price of Advanced Alloy (2 to 4 tried) and of Energy-Infused (3 to 8 tried) does not move the count at all: the turrets take the Alloy before a wall can, and the Energy has one use. They are set to rise with tier because the spec asks for it, and can be moved freely.
- **The tests that assumed one a block follow the tables.** The unit and browser tests that placed or raised a better block and counted the cost now read it from `MATERIALS` and `pieceCost` (`tests/unit/helpers/prices.ts` for the unit tests), so the next change to a price does not break them; a test that means to pin a price says so in its own words.
- **No protocol bump.** Nothing on the wire changed. The ghost and the card read the tables on each screen, so a tab left open on an older build shows the old price until it is reloaded, and the host charges the new one.
- **Left out on purpose.** A refund for the block an upgrade replaces (there is still none, and Scrap is so plentiful in the model that the pool ends every day nearly full of it); a far Bed against the seat (5h2's rule stands); the real valley as the world of the model (it is a flat test world, since the heaps' places only decide which the crew reaches first); more than two crews, and any crew behaviour beyond the few numbers; the machines' and the waves' numbers (each was judged in its own part); a sweep that picks prices for itself.
- **Calls to look at.** The price ladder is the first change to what a thing costs, so it is a call: a block of Composite at 2, Alloy at 3 and Energy at 5, and the Heavy Turret at 20. The Tier 4 row of the ladder arrives four nights after Tier 3's (night 9 or 10 against night 5 or 6), the one gap over three; the thresholds were approved on 2026-10-20 and are left, but it is the long wait in the research. Tier 4 cells pile up in the model (50 and 85 in the pool by day 12) because the only thing they build is a section of wall; more uses for them, or fewer from the machines, are for the play to say. An upgrade pays for the better piece and gives nothing back for the old one. And the model is a model: it says what the tables allow a crew that spends everything it has, and a real crew will spend on repairs, on the wrong thing and on nothing; its numbers are for comparing one table with another and with the spec, not a forecast of an evening's play.
- **For trying it.** `npm run balance` prints the report; change a `cost` in `MATERIALS` or a step in `MODULES` and run it again to see what the change does before anyone plays it. The simulator's tests are `tests/unit/balance-sim.test.ts`.
