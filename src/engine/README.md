# engine

The renderer, camera, input and game loop. This layer may use three.js and the DOM, and it must not import the game layers (`world`, `systems`, `net`, `ui`). See [architecture rules](../../docs/architecture.md). The layers above it hand in what it draws and collides with: `src/app` builds a `GameWorld` and passes it to the game.

- `stage.ts`: the renderer, scene, camera and render loop, with the atmosphere, sun, materials, quality presets and the adaptive resolution governor. A stage knows nothing about gameplay; `setMoment` holds a time of day, `setSkyDriver` lets the game's own clock say where the sky is whenever none is held, and `onBeforeRender` lets other layers update before each frame; `precompile` compiles an object's shader programs ahead of its first draw.
- `game.ts`: the fixed-step loop on top of a stage: input, the local player, the camera rig, the avatar, timing, and the benchmark sweep. It reads the world through the `GameWorld` interface (collision, spawn, benchmark route, build timings), so the valley, a test deck or an empty plane all fit. `teleport`, `loadWorld`, `onTick` and `onBeforeRender` are the hooks the net layer and the builders use.
- `input.ts`: keyboard and pointer lock, by physical key, with the F5 rule. While the mouse is taken it also reports the two mouse buttons, the number keys and the wheel (`onPointer`, `onHotbar`) B (`toggleBuild`), Q (`cycleShape`) and R (`turnPiece`) to whoever builds.
- `camera-rig.ts`: first and third person, boom clearance against the terrain and the cells, sprint field-of-view kick, landing dip.
- `bench.ts`, `bench-sweep.ts`: the `?bench=1` state machine (warm-up, a route counted in simulation ticks, a report) and the sweep that runs it once for each quality preset, with the governor off.
- `atmosphere.ts`, `lighting.ts`, `sky.ts`, `materials.ts`, `lit-material.ts`, `texture-bake.ts`, `pipeline.ts`, `perf.ts`, `glsl/`, `post/`: sky, haze, sun and shadows, the shared lit shaders, the procedural textures (noise and ground at start-up, the tiles block faces show the first time a block is drawn), the post-processing chain, and the frame governor and GPU timer.
- `lamps.ts` (5h3b): the crew's Lights as the shaders see them. A lamp is where the light leaves a lens and which way it is aimed; the game layers hand the stage the lamps that burn (`atmosphere.lamps.set`), and each frame the nearest few to the camera (as many as the quality preset's `lamps` allows, at most `LAMP_SLOTS`, 8) are written into the atmosphere's shared uniforms with the colour (a tenth of its strength by day, all of it by night), so every lit material has them and a base full of Lights costs no more than the nearest eight. `lampLight` in `glsl/atmosphere.glsl.ts` adds up what they give a surface, and each lit shader adds it to its own light before `applyAtmosphere`; the lake, the backdrop and the intro film are not lit by lamps. `LAMP_LIGHT` is the table of numbers (colour, brightness, reach, cone, spill, wrap).
- `avatar.ts`: the third-person body; it takes an accent colour, so the other players in a room wear their seat's.
- `remote-players.ts`: the other players in a room: a suit per seat in the seat's colour, walking and turning as its owner does, with a name tag. Poses come already interpolated from the network layer.
- `enemy-render.ts`: the machines that come for the Core, one instanced mesh for each kind, built from boxes in code: a six-legged crawler of dark steel with amber joints and a red eye, its armored cousin (5i1), the same build bulked out and drawn larger in a rustier plate, and the flying skimmer (5i2), a saucer of blue-grey steel on four rotor pods with a lens under its belly that smoulders and burns as a zap charges, and the breacher (5i3), a low broad ram of dark iron under dull yellow plate with an orb in a cage on its back that swells from an ember to white as it charges to go off, in the world's own sun, shadows and haze. The legs, the rotors (which turn all the time), the jaws, the lunge of a bite, a skimmer's lean into its flight and the beam that grows out of its lens, a breacher's shudder, its sinking on its legs and the swelling of its cell as it charges, a hit's flash and the rise out of the ground are done in the vertex shader from a few numbers per machine, so a hundred of a kind cost one draw call and one more into the shadow map, and the mesh of a kind with none on the field is put away. A kind the picture does not know is drawn as a crawler. Poses come already interpolated from the network layer.
- `shot-fx.ts`: the light of the pulse blaster: the flash at the muzzle, the bolt streaking to its mark, the sparks where it lands and the burst of a machine felled, as two instanced meshes (bolts and sparks) drawn additively into the HDR target, so a firefight is two draw calls. What an effect does while it plays is worked out in the vertex shader from the moment it was born, so the CPU writes a few numbers when something happens and nothing while it plays out; strikes and bursts take a delay so they wait for the bolt to land. A strip of a heap of debris (`strip`) is a flash and a spray of amber scrap in the same two meshes, and a breacher's blast (`blast`) a big flash, a white core and a fan of sparks. A bolt and its muzzle flash take a `heft` (1 for a player's, more for a heavy gun's, drawn no heavier than 2.5): the bolt is that many times as wide and its colour is turned up by the square root of it, which the additive light shows as brighter.
- `debris-render.ts`: the heaps of wreckage the crew strips, as one instanced mesh built from boxes, cylinders and bent slabs in code (a buckled hull plate, a rib of the station's frame, crates, barrels or a broken console on top, scorched rubble and a mast with an amber lamp), in the world's own sun, shadows and haze. A heap is dressed by its grade (5c): Scrap is the rust-and-paint patchwork, Reinforced Composite grey-green layered panels with a cyan strip and a cyan lamp, Advanced Alloy brushed dark steel with an amber inlay and a white lamp, all from one model and one draw. The model is made in layers and the vertex shader takes them away as strips are taken, the top of the pile first; it also does the rise out of the ground at a dawn and the sink when a heap is stripped bare. The renderer keeps only what the picture needs and the host does not say (the eased share left, the flash of a strip, the glow of the cursor) and draws what the network layer's `DebrisView` holds.
- `cinematic.ts`: the one hook by which a film takes the camera: `Game.setCinematic` gives a `Cinematic` (`update(dt, view, out)` writes the camera's pose, `end()` is called when it is taken away) the camera for as long as it plays, with the avatar hidden and the mouse's look ignored, and hands it back by blending the camera into the player's own view over the film's last moments. `CameraPose` gained an optional `roll`, which only a film uses.
- `title-drift.ts`: the title screen's camera, a `Cinematic` that drifts to and fro along a route over the live valley (`shared/title.ts` has the pace, `world/terrain/viewpoints.ts` the two views) until Play, then either hands the camera to the film or glides it into the player's own view with no cut.
- `audio/`: the game's sound, every bit of it made in code (see [decisions](../../docs/decisions.md#the-films-sound-2026-10-27)). `hub.ts` is the one way out to the speakers: the audio context is made inside a click (`unlock`, which the Play buttons call), everything the game sounds goes through a limiter and the master volume, and the context sleeps with a hidden tab. It is the only file here that loads with the page; the rest loads with the film's chunk. `noise.ts` is the seeded white, pink and brown noise the synth shapes, `reverb.ts` a room made as an impulse response, `voices.ts` the one-shot sounds a cue of the score names (a boom, a clang, a groan, a bell, a pad note, a timpani hit, a riser and the rest, `VOICES`) and `beds.ts` the long sounds that are steered along curves while they run (`BEDS`: the roar of the fall, the grinding of the skid, the wind, the drone and the Core's hum).
- `intro/`: the intro film (see [decisions](../../docs/decisions.md#the-intro-film-2026-10-27)). `director.ts` is the film: its clock, its camera (`tracks.ts` are the camera's keys for each scene, in the crash site's own terms: forward along the wreck's last run, to its right, above the ground), its sky (`Stage.setSkyOverride`), its overlay frame for the HUD layer and its hand-over to the player. The picture is four layers built from the film's time alone (`layers.ts`): `fireball-layer.ts` (the wreck's hull, `hull.ts`, its glow and its wake as it falls and skids), `impact-layer.ts` (the dust, the fires of the furrow, the sparks and the flashes where it touches down and stops), `aftermath-layer.ts` (embers, the smoke column, the small fires, the Core's glow and its rings of light) and `crawlers-layer.ts` (the machines that rise at the clearing's edge, drawn by the game's own `enemy-render.ts`). `particles.ts` is the particle fields: instanced quads of four kinds whose whole life is a closed form of their age, written once by the CPU and played by the GPU; `glow.ts` is the camera-facing burst of light. `sound.ts` is the film's sound: told the film's clock every frame by the director, it hands the audio thread whatever starts within the next fifth of a second at exactly the matching moment (so a stalled picture holds the sound back with it), fades out for a skip or a cut and lets the last of the music ring out into play when the film ends; `sound-render.ts` draws the whole soundtrack to samples with no one listening, in step with a clock the way a film plays, which is how it is measured. Nothing in it touches the world: it adds one group to the stage and takes it away.
